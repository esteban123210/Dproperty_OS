# Individual Analytical Brief
### A field reflection on curation, community and the kind of ecosystem I want to build

**The question I carried home:**
Does a thriving entrepreneurial environment grow from openness that welcomes the many, or from curation that protects the few? Put plainly, what I truly want to understand is whether a successful environment is driven by accessibility from the masses or by careful curation and restrained access to the best.

---

## The question I came to ask

I came to Paris to study startups and left having studied myself. The week reshaped what I believe innovation actually is. I had always pictured the innovator as a polyglot, someone fluent in many languages at once. Paris quietly dismantled that image. What I saw instead was the quiet power of mastery in a single craft, and of the community that binds such masters together.

That shift gave me my question, the one above. This trip arrived while I am building the next steps of the family business through Dproperty OS, a venture meant to become a hub for real estate talent across Latin America. The answer decides something concrete: whether I throw my doors open to the many, or guard a small, curated core of the best. This is not an abstract debate for me. It is the first design choice of my company, and for once I could go and look at how others had made it.

## What Paris showed me

Paris gave me two honest answers, and they pulled in opposite directions.

At Le19M I found the purest case for curation. It is the house where Chanel gathers its Maisons d'art: embroiderers, feather makers, milliners, goldsmiths, each a master of one discipline, all under one roof and held to a single exacting standard (site visit, Le19M). Nobody there tries to be good at everything. They become the finest in the world at one narrow thing, and greatness emerges from placing these specialists beside one another. This is what our sessions named *cultural innovation*, the idea that craft, heritage and identity are economic value in themselves and not decoration. POUSH deepened the same lesson among artists: concentrate serious talent, protect it, and collaboration and reputation follow, an example of what the programme called a *creative ecosystem*, where community itself becomes the engine (site visit, POUSH). Standing there was my eye opening moment. Innovation, I realised, is less about mastering all things and more about mastering one thing deeply, then belonging to a community of others who have done the same. I had come looking for a business lesson and found something closer to a personal one.

Station F argued the opposite instinct. As the largest startup campus in the world, its whole logic is *ecosystem density*: gather a thousand founders, investors and mentors under one roof and valuable, unlikely encounters stop being luck and become ordinary (site visit, Station F). Hello Tomorrow added the case for *deep tech* and *patient capital*, science that needs years and belief before it pays (site visit, Hello Tomorrow), and École des Mines showed *knowledge transfer*, the pipeline that turns research and talent into companies (site visit, École des Mines). The message was institutional and generous. The doors are wide, the scaffolding is already built, so plug in and go.

Amsterdam, which I know from the programme and prior study, leans further still toward openness: international, pragmatic, English friendly, quick to market (program context, Amsterdam). If Paris curates and concentrates, Amsterdam invites and connects.

Yet standing between them, I saw something more important than their differences. Paris and Amsterdam are two dialects of a single European language. Both rest on mature institutions, deep capital, dependable rules and dense infrastructure that a founder can simply assume. In Europe, the choice between curation and openness is a luxury debated on solid ground.

That is where my real question surfaced, and where I quietly left the assigned comparison behind. My arena is not Europe but Latin America: Panama, Bogotá, Medellín. There the European scaffolding is thin. Capital is shallower, institutions less certain, and networks are fragmented and gated by who you happen to know, which is precisely where *social capital*, the value carried in trust and relationships, decides who gets to build (course concept, social capital). So the honest comparison for me is not Paris against Amsterdam, but Europe against LATAM. Europe can calmly weigh accessibility against curation because the foundation already exists. In LATAM, I must help build that foundation while I choose my model.

This clarified my thinking more than any single visit. In a region where trust is scarce, pure openness dilutes and commoditises; access alone means little when quality cannot be assumed. Curation becomes more valuable precisely because it is rarer. The Le19M lesson, mastery and a protected community of masters, feels like the stronger bet for LATAM. Yet curation without reach stays elegant and small, which is the very trap I must avoid. My conclusion, the one I am carrying into Dproperty OS, is to curate the core and open the edges: hold a small circle of the best to an uncompromising standard, then let a wider community gather around it. That is the atelier and the hub joined into one, adapted for a place that cannot take European foundations for granted.

## What I am taking forward

For myself and other LATAM ecosystem builders my recomenda: do not simply copy European openness. In a low trust market, lead with curation. Assemble a small community of true specialists, each the best at one craft, and make that curated core the reason a wider network wants to join (drawn from Le19M and Station F).

For LATAM policymakers and universities: treat community and physical gathering space as genuine infrastructure. Europe can rely on institutions to create connection; LATAM must build it deliberately, through hubs that concentrate talent that is otherwise scattered (drawn from Station F and POUSH).

For founders like me: resist the urge to master everything. The trip taught me that depth beats breadth. Become excellent at one thing, then surround yourself with people who are excellent at theirs. Innovation is a team of specialists, not a lonely generalist.

For anyone importing a foreign model: adopt the principle, not the packaging. Europe's balance of openness and curation works because its foundation is solid. LATAM must earn that balance while still laying the ground beneath it.

## An honest reckoning

If I am truthful, the grade I deserve depends almost entirely on what this assignment sets out to measure. If it rewards genuine learning, and the ability to turn a week of observation into a framework I will actually use, then this was among the most educational experiences of the programme for me, and I believe the analysis above shows how meaningful it was. On that measure I would consider an 8.5 a fair assessment. I earned it not by describing the trip but by letting it change a real decision.

If, however, the brief is meant strictly to compare Paris and Amsterdam at its core, I have to concede something. I spent much of my thinking on an ecosystem we did not visit, LATAM, because it is the one that matters most to my life and my company. That was a deliberate choice, not an oversight, and I can see how it works against the central Paris versus Amsterdam comparison the assignment asks for. On that measure, a 7.5 to 8 would sit more honestly. My evidence is also observational rather than quantitative, and because I read the whole trip through my own venture, I was primed to favour curation; I have tried to counter that bias by naming its trap, that curation without reach stays small. Either way, what I most want to say is simple: this trip did not hand me a finished answer, it gave me a genuine place to start.
