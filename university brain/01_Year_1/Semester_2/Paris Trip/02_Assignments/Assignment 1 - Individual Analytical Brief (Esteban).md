# Individual Analytical Brief

**Guiding question:**
Does a thriving entrepreneurial environment grow from openness that welcomes the many, or from curation that protects the few? Put plainly, what I truly want to understand is whether a successful environment is driven by accessibility from the masses or by careful curation and restrained access to the best.

---

## 1. Question and Relevance

I came to Paris to study startups and left having studied myself. The week reshaped what I believe innovation actually is. I had always pictured the innovator as a polyglot, someone fluent in many languages at once. Paris quietly dismantled that image. What I saw instead was the quiet power of mastery in a single craft, and of the community that binds such masters together.

That shift gave me my question, the one above. This trip comes at a time where i am building the next steps of the family business through Dproperty OS, a venture meant to become a hub for real estate talent across Latin America. The answer decides something concrete: whether I throw my doors open to the many, or guard a small, curated core of the best. This is not an abstract debate for me. It is the first design choice of my company.

## 2. Analysis and Evidence

Paris gave me two vivid answers, and they pulled in opposite directions.

At Le19M I found the purest case for curation. It is the house where Chanel gathers its Maisons d'art: embroiderers, feather makers, milliners, goldsmiths, each a master of one discipline, all under one roof and held to a single exacting standard. Nobody there tries to be good at everything. They become the finest in the world at one narrow thing, and greatness emerges from placing these specialists beside one another. POUSH showed the same truth among artists: concentrate serious talent, protect it, and collaboration and reputation follow. This was my eye opening moment. Innovation, I realised, is less about mastering all things and more about mastering one thing deeply, then belonging to a community of others who have done the same.

Station F argued the opposite instinct. Its logic is density and access: gather a thousand founders, investors and mentors under one roof and valuable, unlikely encounters become ordinary. Hello Tomorrow and École des Mines added patient capital and scientific credibility; WILCO added structured acceleration. The message was institutional and generous. The doors are wide, the scaffolding is already built, so plug in and go.

Amsterdam, which I know from the programme, leans further still toward openness: international, pragmatic, English friendly, quick to market. If Paris curates and concentrates, Amsterdam invites and connects.

Yet standing between them, I saw something more important than their differences. Paris and Amsterdam are two dialects of a single European language. Both rest on mature institutions, deep capital, dependable rules and dense infrastructure that a founder can simply assume. In Europe, the choice between curation and openness is made on solid ground.

That is where my real question surfaced. My arena is not Europe but Latin America: Panama, Bogotá, Medellín. There the European scaffolding is thin. Capital is shallower, institutions less certain, networks more fragmented and gated by who you happen to know. So the honest comparison for me is not Paris against Amsterdam, but Europe against LATAM. Europe can calmly debate accessibility versus curation because the foundation already exists. In LATAM, I must help build that foundation while I choose my model.

This clarified my thinking. In a region where trust is scarce, pure openness dilutes and commoditises; access alone means little when quality cannot be assumed. Curation becomes more valuable precisely because it is rarer. The Le19M lesson, mastery and a protected community of masters, is the stronger bet for LATAM. Yet curation without reach stays elegant and small, which is the very trap I must avoid. My conclusion is to curate the core and open the edges: hold a small circle of the best to an uncompromising standard, then let a wider community gather around it. That is the atelier and the hub joined into one, adapted for a place that cannot take European foundations for granted.

## 3. Recommendations

For myself and other LATAM ecosystem builders: do not simply copy European openness. In a low trust market, lead with curation. Assemble a small community of true specialists, each the best at one craft, and make that curated core the reason a wider network wants to join.

For LATAM policymakers and universities: treat community and physical gathering space as genuine infrastructure. Europe can rely on institutions to create connection; LATAM must create it deliberately, through hubs that concentrate talent that is otherwise scattered.

For founders like me: resist the urge to master everything. The trip taught me that depth beats breadth. Become excellent at one thing, then surround yourself with people who are excellent at theirs. Innovation is a team of specialists, not a lonely generalist.

For anyone importing a foreign model: adopt the principle, not the packaging. Europe's balance of openness and curation works because its foundation is solid. LATAM must earn that balance while still laying the ground beneath it.
