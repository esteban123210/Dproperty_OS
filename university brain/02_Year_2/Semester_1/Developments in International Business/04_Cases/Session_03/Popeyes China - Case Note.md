## Decision and study interpretation

After earlier failures, Popeyes must assess whether the Tims China arrangement can produce a viable and scalable Chinese operation.

Separate market attractiveness from entry-mode and execution fit. Assess partner resources, incentives, menu and pricing adaptation, leases, supply chain and brand differentiation. Compare contracts, equity collaboration and direct ownership; test unit economics before interpreting outlet targets as success.

Yongchen Lu, the chief executive officer (CEO) of Tim Hortons International Limited China (Tims China), 
was once again tasked with developing another foreign fast-service brand in China. As the founding CEO, Lu 
played a pivotal role in establishing Tims China in 2 018, the national franchisee for Tim Hortons, the 
renowned Canadian coffee brand. In less than four years, he successfully expanded the coffee franchise, 
locally branded as Tims Coffee ( 天好咖啡 ), into a nationwide chain boasting over 700 locations. 
Additionally, he led the company to go public on Nasdaq, a stock exchange based in New York City. In March 
2023, Tims China acquired PLKC International Limited (Popeyes China) and gained the franchise rights to 
develop Popeyes Louisiana Kitchen, Inc. (Popeyes) fried chicken restaurants throughout the country. 
The Popeyes brand, originating in the US, had been among the world’s largest fried chicken franchises and, 
like Tim Hortons, was also under the ownership of Restaurant Brands International (RBI). The chicken 
brand experienced its second unsuccessful attempt to enter the Chinese market, just prior to being acquired 
by Tims China. The question lingered: could Lu make it count this time? Furthermore, with Tims China 
managing two fast-service brands under the same corporate umbrella, the crucial question arose —would 
there be enough synergy between the two to ensure successful co-development? 
CHINESE FAST-FOOD MARKET 
Western fast food was introduced to China by KFC Corporation (KFC) with its first restaurant in 
Tiananmen Square, Beijing, in 1987.2 After KFC, many other Western food brands endeavored to penetrate 
the Chinese market (refer to Exhibit 1), bringing choices like pizza, burgers, fried chicken, and tacos to a 
population that had been accustomed to rice, buns, noodles, and dumplings for ce nturies. These foreign 
offerings brought exotic novelty and, after over three decades of development, were embraced by local 
consumers. In 2021, burgers ranked third in the top ten most popular online orders for food and beverages 
in China, trailing only milk tea and rice meals. A recent survey in 2022 indicated that over half of Chinese 
consumers frequently dined in Western fast-food restaurants.3

Some unique consumer preferences had remained, however. Western fast foods were typically prepared 
through frying, roasting, boiling, and baking, with a limited array of sauces and spices such as tomato 
sauces, barbecue sauces, pepper, cheese, butter, mayonnaise, cream, mustard, and milk. In contrast, Chinese 
cooking methods were much more diverse, encompassing stewing, steaming, sautéing, braising, and stir -
frying. Even more diverse were the spices, including ginger, garlic, cumin, green onion, coriander, vinegar, 
soy sauce, cooking wine, and a wide variety of peppers —not to mention numerous regional ingredients. 
The greater diversity in cooking methods and spices resulted in a broader range of tastes in the market.4 
These persistent, distinctive preferences in the market necessitated extensive localization. Chinese 
consumers long considered grain items like rice, noodles, or buns as the necessary main components of a 
meal—something that could easily fill the stomach and provide lasting strength. Consequently, combos that 
comprised a grain item (rice or noodles) and a Western side (e.g., fried chicken nuggets and French fries) 
were typically top sellers in Western fast-food restaurants in China.5 Western salads had been a tough sell, 
as Chinese individuals usually cooked vegetables prior to consumption, reflecting the nation ’s deep 
preference for hot or warm foods and beverages. Cookies and chips, common in the West, also encountered 
failure in Chinese fast-food restaurants as people did not perceive them as a fast-food companion.6 
To better cater to the demands of local consumers, some Western brands went so far as to adopt more 
authentic Chinese foods, including stuffed buns, preserved eggs, porridges, fried dough sticks (油条), and 
boiled skewers (关东煮 ).7 Consequently, fast-food menus typically featured significantly more choices in 
China than in their home countries. KFC restaurants, for example, offered around 50 different items in 
China compared to only about 30 in the US.8 This extensive food variety required much larger kitchens and 
a more complex logistical network. Fusion between Western and traditional Chinese food was considered 
an inexorable trend and a necessary recipe for Western fast-food brands to compete in the country. 
Contrast With Chinese-Style Fast Foods 
In the early 2000s, local brands offering Chinese-style fast food emerged and rapidly captured a significant 
portion of the fast -food segment. In 2021, the market scale of Chinese fast food reached ¥774.4 billion,9 
increasing by ¥115.4 billion from the previous year, and was projected to approach ¥1,268.5 billion in 2025. 
Western fast-food market recorded a scale of ¥283.4 billion, reflecting an¥45.3 billion increment from the 
previous year, and projected to reach ¥476.2 billion in 2025. Thus, Chinese fast food commanded a 70.4 
per cent share of the total fast-food market in 2021, compared with Western fast food’s 25.8 per cent.10 
Despite occupying a relatively larger market segment, Chinese fast food exhibited a significantly lower 
market concentration compared with its Western counterparts. The top five Western brands held a 53 per 
cent share in their market segment, while the equivalent figure for Chinese brands was 2.9 per cent. 11

Furthermore, Chinese fast -food providers across different regions varied extensively in items offered, 
flavors, and methods of cooking and consumption. In comparison, Western fast-food providers were more 
standardized, utilized more pre-made components, and fulfilled a higher number of takeout orders. Western 
brands also led with a high level of chain operation, whereas most Chinese fast-food restaurants were stand-
alone businesses. 
Overall Market Conditions 
The fast -food industry boomed at the turn of the twenty -first century, coinciding with China’s rapid 
urbanization, increasing household income, accelerated pace of life, and heightened cultural exchanges. In 
terms of store count, the percentage of fast-service restaurants in the entire food industry surged from 33.6 
per cent in 2015 to 49.2 per cent in 2019. 12 The scale of the fast -food market reached ¥1,099.4 billion in 
2021—an increase of 17.78 per cent from the previous year —and was projected to reach ¥1,809.2 billion 
in 2025. 13 The comparatively lower dine -out rate in China —35 per cent versus 45 per cent in other 
developed countries like the US and Japan—also promised substantial growth.14 
Compared with dining in a formal restaurant, which typically involved several courses, fast food had 
become an economical option for most Chinese consumers. Around 92 per cent of Western fast-food brands 
clustered below ¥40 per capita consumption, with 63 per cent even below ¥20 in 2022. Less than 4 per cent 
of brands exceeded ¥60 (see Exhibits 2 and 3).15 Besides sensitivity to price, Chinese consumers were much 
more inclusive and welcoming towards novel flavors and innovations than people in other countries, 
showing exceptional enthusiasm for embracing new brands and offerings. KFC China reportedly introduced 
approximately 50 new items a year in China, compared to one or two in the US.16 
Lu observed that a significant number of fast-food service outlets in Western countries were located on the 
outskirts of cities or even rural areas and located in a standalone property with a drive-thru, prioritizing take-out 
services. A long-term contract often bound the property owner and the restaurant operator. He compared this 
with China, where virtually all fast-food restaurants were concentrated in malls, business complexes, and the 
food courts of office buildings in city centers, and where the rental contract tended to be short. Subsequently, 
rents could be a much more substantial cost component in China than in other parts of the world. 
First-tier cities17 like Shanghai, Beijing, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen had the most brands and the highest 
density of fast-food outlets. These cities were characterized by huge and dense populations (roughly 20 million 
or more), a fast pace of life and work, higher income, and an inclusive dietary culture. While most Western 
brands were still expanding in first-tier cities, the growing market congestion resulted in intermittent declines 
in foot traffic and the closure of certain locations. In 2021, KFC experienced a decrease in operating profits 
from $827 million to $787 million in 2022, and Pizza Hut saw a decline from $111 million to $70 million. 
This occurred despite both brands consistently adding new stores during the same timeframe.18 To explore 
additional avenues for growth, prominent brands were compelled to initiate expansion into lower-tier cities.

After consuming fast food for nearly four decades, Chinese consumers became increasingly discerning 
about what they ate. In addition to considering the taste and appearance of items, the restaurant 
environment, and service quality, consumers began to pay m ore attention to the health and safety aspects 
of ingredients, their origins, receipts, and cooking processes.19 Numerous reports linked the consumption 
of fast foods to the exacerbation of health problems such as obesity, further reinforcing the long -standing 
impression of fast food as junk food.20 In comparison to other cooking methods (e.g., boiling and stewing), 
frying had traditionally been viewed as less healthy due to its extreme heat and greasiness. Chicken, often 
associated with hormone feed, was perceived as lower quality meat than beef. 21 COVID-19 raised these 
health concerns to a new high. Pressure on fast food companies to source healthier ingredients and replace 
existing choices with more health-conscious alternatives rapidly mounted.22 
Guochao (which translated to “nationalistic fashion”) consumption, surfaced with a new generation of 
Chinese youth, notably among Generation Z, and significantly influenced the fast -food industry. This 
generation demonstrated a stronger preference for Western food infused with clear Chinese elements. 
Riding this momentum, Tastien, a Chinese burger brand had expanded at an unprecedented speed, opening 
more than 3,500 stores between 2020 and early 2023 .23 Tastien’s menu comprised burgers made from 
traditional Chinese dishes such as tofu, Beijing roast duck, and stir-fried spicy chicken. Nothing showcased 
the brand’s identity and commitment to Chinese customers better than its slogan: “Chinese stomachs love 
Chinese burgers.”24 Guochao consumption added another thrust to the localization endeavors that many 
Western fast-food brands embarked on at the turn of the twenty-first century. 
Chicken Segment 
China’s fast-food industry took root with chicken items (e.g., wings, nuggets, tenders, and sandwiches ), 
which consistently ranked among the top choices.25 A Western-style eatery without a chicken item was hard 
to find, and fried chicken was offered from stalls in countryside farmer ’s markets to upscale city center 
restaurants. According to annual registration statistics, the proliferation of new fried chicken brands had 
been exponential since 2015. Both 2021 and 2022 witnessed the emergence of approximately 20,000 new 
fried chicken brands.26 
The dominant players in the chicken market were a select few nationwide chains offering Western -style 
fried chicken. These included foreign giants like KFC, McDonald’s Corporation (McDonald’s), and Burger 
King Corporation (Burger King), as well as local brands such as Wallace and Dicos . As of 2022, these 
leading brands had each established over 1,000 restaurants nationwide (see Exhibit 4). With over 9,000 
locations, KFC stood out as the only nationally recognized specialist in friend chicken, while Wallace led 
the pack with over 20,000 restaurants overall—albeit with significantly lower system-wide sales due to its 
strategy of setting chicken at half the price of KFC.27

A noteworthy trend in the market involved the emergence of a number of regional chicken brands. These 
brands typically entered the market with Chinese-style fried chicken featuring innovative flavours, achieved 
either through traditional cooking methods or unique ingredients, such as garlic. Often, these newer brands 
focused on establishing a strong presence in a specific region, strategically leveraging regional identity, 
internet celebrity marketing, and the Guochao trend. For instance, Pala Catering Management Co . Ltd.

to over 3,000 locations in Wuhan and adjacent cities.28 
In addition to traditional dine-in restaurant formats, there was a surge in chicken chains exclusively catering to take-
out orders in many cities.29 These outlets targeted the growing segment of customers preferring online orders with 
home delivery. In 2022, Zhengxin Chicken Steak (正新鸡排 ) operated over 25,000 outlets, while newer brands 
like Chirpyhut Fried Chicken (叫了个炸鸡), Kuafood (夸父), Three Mr (三个先森 ), and Xijie (喜姐炸串 )—

offered were more affordable and could be served more quickly compared to restaurant brands.30 
POPEYES: ONE OF THE WORLD’S LARGEST FRIED CHICKEN CHAINS 
The inaugural Popeyes restaurant opened in a New Orleans suburb, Louisiana, on June 12, 1972, 
specializing in New Orleans-style fried chicken. Founded by Al Copeland, the venture—known originally 
as Chicken on the Run and later renamed as Popeyes Mighty Good Chicken—aimed to rival KFC. With its 
flavorful blend of Cajun and Creole influences, Popeyes ’ spicy chicken quickly gained popularity among 
the local clientele. The budding brand ventured into franchising in 1976.31 
Copeland adopted the name “Popeyes” from a then -popular character, Popeye Doyle , from the film The 
French Connection. Nonetheless, many associated the brand with the beloved cartoon character Popeye, 
which originally appeared in the comic strip Thimble Theatre and went on to become both a film and 
television series. This was largely due to the chain’s 35-years of campaigns which featured characters from 
the show for advertising, packaging, and decoration. In 1980, Popeyes debuted its trademark tagline, “Love 
That Chicken,” and began promoting it in TV and radio commercials set to the backdrop of New Orleans 
funk, and rhythm and blues music. In 2008, the formal name “Popeyes Louisiana Kitchen, Inc. ” was 
adopted to symbolize the chain’s distinctive heritage from the Louisiana style of cooking (see Exhibit 5).32 
Over the years, Popeyes had consistently updated its menu by experimenting with a variety of items like fried 
shrimp, crawfish, chicken tender, and wings, among others. Buttermilk biscuits were introduced in 1983 and 
became a permanent menu item for their interesting appeal. By 2021, biscuits accounted for about 25 per cent 
of the overall sales of the company, making the item one of the most popular in fast-food history.33 
Popeyes’ most famous chicken sandwich was launched in August 2019, and contained a buttermilk-battered 
breast fillet, pickle slices, and Cajun spreads stacked in a brioche bun. The sandwich went viral instantly,

POPEYES’ EARLY ENTRIES INTO CHINA 
First Attempt 
Popeyes entered China for the first time in 1999 with a company-owned outlet in Beijing. The plan was to 
build 390 locations in the following three years. At the time, Chinese consumers were far from accepting 
any foreign food as an option for regular consumption , and Western food was only for a select few. In 
addition, the embryonic market was already dominated by KFC and McDonald’s, two giants which had 
been operating in China for about a decade. Popeyes withdrew from the country in 2003.39 
Second Attempt 
In 2019, RBI unveiled a plan to reintroduce Popeyes to China through a partnership with TAB Food 
Investments (TFI), a Turkish operator of fast -service brands. TFI had become the franchisee of RBI’s 
Burger King brand in China in 2012 and significantly accel erated the brand’s expansion. While Burger 
King had established 52 company-owned locations in seven years after its entry in 2005, with TFI on board, 
it expanded to 900 locations in just six years. By 2022, TFI successfully operated over 1,000 Burger King 
restaurants in 150 cities across China.40

Under this new partnership, the first Popeyes flagship restaurant opened its doors in Shanghai in May 2020. 
The restaurant featured a spacious dining hall of 470 square metres, accommodating up to 200 customers 
simultaneously.41 It was strategically located on Huaihai Central Road, a prominent high street at the heart 
of the city, surrounded by upscale malls, hotels, and office buildings. The opening was nothing short of a 
sensation, with over 50,000 customers snapping up coupons for the day from online platforms, and 
thousands patiently waiting, some for as long as 8 hours, to enter.42 
This inaugural restaurant marked the beginning of an ambitious growth plan: to make China the second-largest 
market for the chicken chain within the next two to three years and to establish over 1,500 restaurants in Shanghai 
and adjacent cities over the next ten years. The then-CEO that operated Popeyes brand, a Brazilian national, 
emphasized, “A foreign fast-service brand in China must rapidly expand its locations from the first to at least 
400; otherwise, survival would be difficult, let alone to become profitable.”43 
However, the plan did not unfold successfully. Only nine locations were opened two years later. In August 
2022, seven Popeyes restaurants were closed, except for the two in Shanghai. Amid the pandemic, timing 
was not favorable for Popeyes, and there were additional challenges. Early patrons considere d the fried 
chicken small in size, high in price, and too greasy. One overseas returnee commented, “The price here was 
almost several times that abroad. It felt like an entirely different brand. ”44 Furthermore, the two partners 
fell out . In early 2022, RBI disclosed that, due to certain disagreements with TFI, it had decided to 
prematurely dissolve its collaboration on Popeyes, with a settlement of $100 million to its partner.45 
Despite the restaurant closures, Popeyes assured its followers via social media, “You may have heard about 
the closing of our restaurants, but we have never left. We will return soon.”46 
ACQUISITION: THE THIRD STRIKE 
On August 10, 2022, RBI announced a non -binding term sheet co -signed with Cartesian Capital Group 
(Cartesian), permitting Cartesian as the new operator of the chicken brand in China. Cartesian subsequently 
created a new company, Popeyes China, with an investment amounting to $30 million. As a private equity 
company, Cartesian had managed investments of over $3 billion worldwide;47 its track record of nurturing 
businesses in emerging markets was particularly impressive. “RBI and Cartesian had collaborated quite 
fruitfully for more than a decade,” said Peter Yu, who was one of the executives of Cartesian and also the 
chairman of Tims China.

The Acquirer: Tims China 
In February 2023, Tims China announced the acquisition of Popeyes China. Tims China had been a joint 
venture formed between RBI and Cartesian in 2018. RBI served as a brand franchisor, and Cartesian as the 
capital investor and majority owner. The top management team consisted of Lu, the founding CEO, and 
several others who were native Chinese with postgraduate degrees from top foreign universities. Lu had 
been an experienced manager in the fast-service industry. Before his work with Tims China, he had been 
the chief financial officer of Burger King China for several years, during which, as a main force, he charted 
the burger chain’s swift expansion. 
Led by Lu, Tims China had followed a strategy of coffee paired with warm foods (e.g., bagels, wraps, 
sandwiches, etc.) since the opening of the first Tims Coffee shop in 2019. This strategy had set Tims China apart 
from other coffee chains, which often only offered refrigerated foods alongside coffees. The menu featured some 
essential coffee items from the Canadian franchisor, constituting about 10 per cent of all offerings. However, a 
substantial portion of the beverage and food options stemmed from vibrant local innovations. 
In September 2022, Tims China went public on Nasdaq. Despite the interruption of COVID-19, the coffee 
chain opened more than 700 locations by April 2023, marching in full force toward the goal of 1,000 
locations by the end of the year (Exhibit 6). At this speed, Tims Coffee would hit the ten-year objective of 
1,500 locations within just five years. The expansion target was accordingly adjusted to 3,000 locations by 
2026. Lu attributed the fast progress of Tims China to the reputable brand in the coffee category, continuous 
capital investments, and the competent local management team. 
“After we set Tims Coffee on a solid trajectory, my team started to explore the idea of adding another brand 
as the growth thrust,” explained by Lu. When its parent company, Cartesian, reached out with the proposal 
of buying Popeyes, Lu felt ready to take it. 
The Deal 
The acquisition of Popeyes was an all-stock transaction. In this deal, Tims China gained the exclusive rights 
to develop and sub-franchise Popeyes restaurants in mainland China and Macau, as well as a cash injection 
of $30 million from Popeyes China. Tims China would earmark another $60 million for growing the 
Popeyes franchise in the years to come. This topped up the starting capital for Popeyes brand to $90 million, 
almost ten times the amount the chicken brand received under TFI. RBI, therefore, franchis ed two of its 
fast-service brands to Tims China. 
Both parties in the transaction were quite positive about the merger of the two fast -food service brands. 
“We are very excited about growing the Popeyes brand in China as part of our broader global growth 
strategy. We have strong confidence in our partner who has a deep understanding of the Chinese market, 
and we believe it will position us well for success in the country,” said David Shear, RBI’s president. “With 
this transaction, Tims China gains a tremendous additional pathway for growth in the world’s m ost 
compelling consumer market,” expressed Yu.

Synergy 
Lu saw considerable synergy from the merger regarding aspects of location selection, logistics, shared 
innovation, and negotiating with property owners and suppliers. He noted, “ We are thrilled to have the 
opportunity to welcome Popeyes, a beloved global brand and proven winner, to the Tims China family. The 
two brands are a natural fit, with complementary product offerings and exceptional growth potential. Both 
brands will benefit from greater scale, a stronger financial model, and synergies. We cannot wai t to serve 
all our guests, including our over eleven million loyalty members, a spicy chicken sandwich.” 
The future of Popeyes under the new operator was not without doubts. As the two highly reputable brands in 
the fast-service industry, Tims Coffee and Popeyes offered essentially distinctive products. Relatedly, the 
ingredients, operation process, store management, and consumer groups all meaningfully differed between 
the two. Unlike soft drinks, coffee had not traditionally been closely associated with the consumption of fried 
chicken, and this perception was unlikely to change in the near future. How to define and tap into the synergy 
promised by the acquisition would be tricky. “It is important to respect the differences between the two 
brands,” said Lu. “Certain functions must be separated completely in order for them to co-exist.” 
THE WAY FORWARD 
Tims China’s acquisition of Popeyes was successfully closed one month after announcement. The 
subsequent challenge facing Lu was to cultivate the chicken brand in the market. Having experienced two 
fizzled attempts beforehand, Popeyes had lagged its major rival KFC for almost four decades in bringing 
its fried chicken to the nation. In addition, the acquisition transformed Tims China into a company with two 
foreign, fast-service brands. Calibrating the relationship between these brands presented another com plex 
but pivotal imperative. Could Lu make the fried chicken brand fly in the Chinese market?
