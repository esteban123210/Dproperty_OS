# Bean of Africa - Case Note

**Case:** Puhr, H. (2026). *The Bean of Africa: Cross-Border Trade, Intermediaries, and the True Cost of a Morning Cup*  
**Session:** 1 - What is International Business? Why do MNEs exist?  
**Purpose:** Class discussion case

## Situation
Felix Wagner tries to purchase specialty coffee from Uganda and have it delivered to Austria. What initially appears to be a simple cross-border purchase becomes a chain of failures, workarounds, intermediaries, and hidden costs.

Uganda's coffee is commercially attractive and, as an LDC, can benefit from preferential EU tariff treatment. Yet the practical transaction proves difficult because formal market infrastructure does not make the cross-border exchange seamless.

## Frictions revealed by the case

### Sales channel
- Mt. Elgon Wild Coffee's website fails when Austria is selected.
- WhatsApp communication does not produce a functioning sales process.
- The producer appears unable to serve the European customer directly.

### Intermediation
- Clare, Felix's safari guide, becomes an informal intermediary.
- She purchases the coffee locally and organizes shipment.
- Her local knowledge solves problems that neither the formal website nor standard sales channel can solve.

### Logistics
For the initial 2.5 kg order:
- DHL quotation: about USD 200.
- FedEx quotation: about USD 150.
- Posta Uganda: about USD 100, with a much longer delivery estimate.

The shipping cost changes the economics of the order and encourages Felix to increase quantity simply to spread the fixed logistics cost.

### Payment
- A previous USD 250 international bank transfer had generated EUR 35 in fees.
- The alternative WorldRemit / mobile-money route repeatedly fails.
- Felix ultimately splits payments into multiple transactions until enough transfers succeed.

### Documentation and customs
- Address formatting creates uncertainty.
- Small exporters have limited documentation capability.
- Even with favorable tariff treatment, the transaction faces customs and administrative uncertainty.

## Why the case matters for International Business
The case demonstrates that **price is not the full cost of a market transaction**.

The coffee itself may be attractive and inexpensive, but the transaction also requires solving:
- search and communication;
- trust;
- payment;
- currency conversion;
- logistics;
- documentation;
- customs;
- coordination;
- information gaps;
- enforcement / accountability.

These are transaction costs.

## Intermediary map
| Party | Role / problem solved | New dependency / cost created |
|---|---|---|
| Mt. Elgon Wild Coffee | Produces and sells coffee | Weak international sales capability |
| Felix | Buyer / potential importer | Lacks local knowledge and established process |
| Clare | Local intermediary | Informal, non-scalable dependency |
| WorldRemit / payment providers | Move money across borders | Failures, fees, compliance friction |
| MTN Mobile Money | Local receipt of funds | Account / identity / transfer constraints |
| Posta Uganda / courier | International logistics | High cost, long transit, uncertainty |
| Austrian customs | Border compliance | Documentation and classification uncertainty |
| EU trade regime / TARIC | Tariff framework | Requires correct origin and documentation |

## The strategic question
At the end of the case Felix sees a possible business opportunity: Ugandan specialty coffee appears underrepresented in Europe, input prices are attractive, and he has gained experience with the process.

But the case raises a more important IB question:

> If Felix turned this into a real import business, which activities should remain market transactions, which should be handled by professional intermediaries, and which might eventually need to be internalized?

## Market versus hierarchy interpretation
The case is a practical introduction to Coase/Williamson transaction-cost logic.

### Stay with the market when
- reliable specialist suppliers exist;
- contracts and service quality are easy to verify;
- transaction costs are low;
- scale does not justify internal capability.

### Consider deeper control / internalization when
- repeated transactions create high coordination costs;
- counterparties are unreliable;
- information asymmetry is persistent;
- specialized knowledge becomes strategically valuable;
- failures threaten customer experience or margins;
- scale makes fixed internal capability economical.

## Connection to Liability of Foreignness
If Felix creates an Austrian importer operating with Ugandan suppliers, he will face disadvantages arising from unfamiliarity, distance, institutional differences, logistics, payment systems, and local relationships.

He therefore needs advantages that compensate for those costs, such as:
- superior European market access;
- brand / customer relationships;
- logistics expertise;
- import-compliance knowledge;
- reliable supplier relationships;
- aggregation of volume;
- standardized payment and documentation processes.

## Generalizable lesson
A cross-border opportunity can look highly attractive when evaluated only on **product price and tariff rates**, yet become unattractive once the full cost of transacting across borders is included.

The managerial task is therefore to identify **where the friction sits in the value chain** and choose the governance mechanism—market, intermediary, partnership, or internal organization—that solves it most efficiently.

## Post-class extraction
**Most useful framework:** transaction-cost economics / markets vs hierarchy.  
**Supporting concept:** liability of foreignness.  
**Transferable principle:** do not evaluate international opportunity economics using product margin alone; include the cost of coordinating across borders.
