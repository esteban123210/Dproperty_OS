# 1. Situation

Felix Wagner, based in Vienna, attempts to buy specialty coffee from Uganda after tasting Mt. Elgon Wild Coffee during a safari.

What initially seems like a straightforward small international purchase becomes a chain of:
- digital sales-channel failure;
- informal intermediation;
- expensive logistics;
- payment-system problems;
- documentation uncertainty;
- customs uncertainty;
- address-formatting concerns;
- monitoring problems.

The case is deliberately useful for International Business because the nominal product is simple, but crossing borders makes the transaction complex.

# 2. Uganda and coffee context

Uganda is described as:
- a landlocked East African country;
- heavily dependent on agriculture;
- a major coffee producer/exporter;
- a producer of both Robusta and Arabica coffee.

The case includes exhibits on:
- Uganda's geography;
- food exports as a share of total exports;
- Ugandan coffee exports;
- EU TARIC treatment;
- the final Austrian customs notification.

Uganda's status as a Least Developed Country is commercially relevant because qualifying products can benefit from the EU **Everything But Arms (EBA)** trade preference.

The case indicates that the applicable customs tariff on the coffee can therefore be **0%**, making the opportunity appear attractive on paper.

However, the case demonstrates that **tariff cost is only one small part of the total cost of cross-border exchange**.

# 3. Product and relationship origin

Felix and his wife first encounter the coffee through their Ugandan safari operator.

The safari guides include **Laban and Clare**.

At the end of the trip, the couple receive a bag of Mt. Elgon Wild Coffee's **Sunset Gold Roast** as a gift.

Back in Vienna, Felix likes the coffee enough to try to order more.

This origin matters because the later import transaction depends heavily on the **personal relationship** formed during the safari.

# 4. First problem: the formal online sales channel fails

Felix first tries to buy directly through the producer's website.

Problem:
- the website repeatedly fails when Austria is selected as the destination country.

He then contacts the business using the WhatsApp number shown on the site.

Problem:
- an automated response works;
- a human technical-support contact replies;
- the inquiry is supposedly forwarded to sales;
- no effective sales follow-up occurs.

### IB interpretation

A company can have:
- a website;
- a brand;
- products;
- stated international-shipping capability;

without possessing a robust **international sales process**.

Digital presence does not equal international operational capability.

# 5. Clare becomes the intermediary

Felix asks Clare, the safari guide, to act as a go-between.

She agrees to:
- buy the coffee locally;
- arrange shipment to Austria.

Clare is therefore solving failures in:
- sales access;
- local purchasing;
- supplier communication;
- logistics coordination;
- local trust.

But she is helping as a favor rather than as a professional exporter.

### Strategic implication

The transaction works because of an **informal personal intermediary**, but that arrangement is not inherently scalable or professionally governed.

# 6. Coffee price and logistics economics

Coffee price in the case:
- approximately **USD 14 per 500 g**.

Initial intended quantity:
- **2.5 kg**, approximately USD 70 of coffee.

Shipping quotations discussed:
- DHL: about **USD 200**;
- FedEx: about **USD 150**;
- Posta Uganda: about **USD 100**, with a longer estimated transit time.

The shipping cost is so high relative to the product that Felix increases the order quantity to spread the fixed logistics cost.

Revised order:
- roughly **5 kg** for Felix;
- an additional 500 g for Clare;
- coffee plus shipping and Clare's bag brings the total to about **USD 254**.

### Transaction-cost insight

The economics of an international transaction cannot be assessed by comparing supplier price with consumer price alone.

Fixed cross-border logistics costs can dramatically alter:
- minimum efficient order size;
- margin;
- inventory requirement;
- working capital;
- channel viability.

# 7. Payment problem

A conventional international bank transfer is unattractive because Felix had previously paid around **EUR 35** in bank fees on a roughly USD 250 transfer.

Clare suggests a digital remittance route using **WorldRemit** to an **MTN Mobile Money** account.

Problems:
- initial transfer attempts fail;
- there is no clear diagnosis;
- possible issues include amount, recipient naming, account characteristics, or transaction purpose;
- Felix eventually succeeds by making multiple smaller attempts until the necessary amount is transferred.

### IB interpretation

Cross-border payment infrastructure can create:
- fees;
- failed transactions;
- compliance friction;
- identity/name mismatch;
- currency-conversion issues;
- uncertainty about transaction classification.

A transaction can therefore be commercially attractive yet operationally fragile because money cannot move reliably between buyer and seller.

# 8. Dispatch and address-format problem

Clare takes the coffee to Posta Uganda for export.

Felix coaches her on the Austrian address format because mistakes could make delivery fail.

The package is accepted into the EMS international tracking system.

The case also describes a check of one of the tins at the post office before dispatch.

### Generalizable insight

Even mundane standards—address formats, labeling, forms—can become cross-border coordination problems because parties operate with different assumptions about what is "normal."

# 9. Customs anxiety and the Jordan bracelet story

While the coffee is in transit, Felix hears about a bracelet sent from Jordan that encountered severe Austrian customs problems because of documentation/valuation issues.

This causes him to question whether his own shipment contains the right:
- customs invoice;
- declared value;
- HS/TARIC code;
- origin information;
- agricultural documentation;
- carrier documentation.

He realizes that although he researched the tariff, he does not fully know what documentation was submitted in Uganda.

### IB interpretation

Regulatory knowledge can be fragmented among:
- exporter;
- intermediary;
- carrier;
- importer;
- customs authority.

This creates information asymmetry and monitoring costs.

# 10. Arrival and customs outcome

The parcel reaches Austria surprisingly quickly and clears customs.

The favorable LDC/EBA tariff treatment is applied: **customs duty is zero**.

However, costs remain:
- import VAT;
- Austrian Post handling fee.

The case reports Felix paying approximately:
- **EUR 9.48** import VAT;
- **EUR 7.20** customs/handling fee;
- total around **EUR 16.68** at collection.

The customs documentation uses a declared value that is lower than the amount Felix remembers paying for the coffee. The case does not establish exactly why that valuation appears.

### Important distinction

**Zero tariff does not mean zero border cost.**

International trade can still involve:
- VAT;
- handling charges;
- compliance effort;
- documentation;
- delays;
- uncertainty;
- logistics costs;
- intermediary costs.

# 11. What comes next?

Once the coffee arrives successfully, Felix reframes the experience as a possible business opportunity.

Attractive elements include:
- product quality;
- Ugandan specialty coffee's potential underrepresentation in Europe;
- favorable raw-material economics;
- preferential tariff treatment;
- experiential knowledge gained through the first transaction.

But the transaction has exposed major structural problems:
- weak international sales capability at the producer;
- informal intermediation;
- fragmented payment infrastructure;
- expensive shipping;
- customs/documentation uncertainty;
- multiple parties between producer and final consumer.

The strategic question is no longer simply **"Can the coffee be imported?"**

It becomes:

> **How should a repeatable commercial transaction be structured, which functions should be outsourced or partnered, and which may need to be internalized?**

# 12. Intermediary map

| Party / institution | Problem solved | New cost / dependency / risk |
|---|---|---|
| Mt. Elgon Wild Coffee | Produces/roasts coffee | Weak international order execution |
| Felix | Buyer / potential importer | Lacks established local process and export infrastructure |
| Clare | Local purchasing + coordination + trust bridge | Informal and non-scalable dependency |
| WorldRemit | Cross-border remittance | Repeated failures / compliance uncertainty |
| MTN Mobile Money | Local receipt of funds | Account/identity/transaction constraints |
| DHL / FedEx | Premium international logistics options | Very high cost relative to product value |
| Posta Uganda / EMS | Lower-cost international shipping | Longer/less predictable service and monitoring |
| Austrian Post | Final-mile delivery / customs handling | Handling charges |
| Austrian customs | Import compliance | Documentation, valuation, classification uncertainty |
| EU EBA/TARIC regime | Preferential tariff framework | Benefit depends on correct classification/origin/compliance |

# 13. Transaction stages and costs

## Search
Costs include:
- finding a functional international sales channel;
- finding a reliable intermediary;
- comparing shipping options;
- understanding customs rules.

## Negotiation
Costs include:
- quantity decisions;
- shipping method;
- price coordination;
- informal responsibilities;
- payment method.

## Contracting
The transaction is largely informal, creating uncertainty over:
- responsibilities;
- documentation;
- remedies;
- valuation;
- delivery obligations.

## Monitoring
Costs include:
- repeated payment attempts;
- shipment tracking;
- uncertainty about export status;
- uncertainty about customs documentation and clearance.

## Enforcement / problem solving
Potential problems include:
- failed payment;
- lost parcel;
- incorrect customs treatment;
- incomplete documentation;
- inability to hold an informal intermediary accountable in a formal commercial sense.

# 14. Institutional-theory lens

## Formal institutions
- customs law;
- tariff classification;
- EBA trade preferences;
- tax/VAT rules;
- postal/logistics systems;
- payment compliance;
- documentation standards.

## Informal institutions
- personal trust;
- favors;
- local relationships;
- informal coordination;
- norms around payment and communication.

The case shows that successful cross-border transactions often depend on **both** formal and informal institutions.

# 15. Liability of Foreignness lens

The situation exhibits several costs that a domestic Austrian purchase would largely avoid:
- geographic distance;
- unfamiliarity with Ugandan systems;
- payment incompatibility;
- logistics uncertainty;
- documentation uncertainty;
- limited local roots;
- reliance on local relationships.

A professional importer would need FSAs that reduce those disadvantages.

Possible FSAs:
- import-compliance capability;
- standardized logistics;
- supplier relationships;
- reliable payment architecture;
- aggregation of volume;
- European customer access;
- brand/marketing;
- inventory management;
- repeatable documentation processes.

# 16. Uppsala / experiential-learning lens

The first transaction produces knowledge about:
- supplier access;
- payment channels;
- carrier options;
- order economics;
- customs;
- documentation;
- local relationships;
- hidden costs.

A second transaction would therefore begin with less uncertainty.

The case provides a clean example of the Uppsala mechanism:

**experience -> knowledge -> lower uncertainty -> potentially greater commitment.**

# 17. Market-entry-mode lens

The transaction makes clear that international exchange can be organized through different combinations of:
- direct sales;
- agents/intermediaries;
- logistics specialists;
- local partners;
- distributors;
- owned activities.

The managerial question is not simply whether to "enter" a market, but how much:
- control;
- ownership;
- resource commitment;
- partner dependence

is appropriate at each stage.

# 18. Transaction-cost lens

The case's strongest immediate framework is transaction-cost economics.

The coffee's nominal price is only part of the cost.

Other costs include:
- search;
- information;
- negotiation;
- payment;
- coordination;
- transport;
- monitoring;
- customs;
- documentation;
- uncertainty;
- potential enforcement.

This can make an apparently attractive arbitrage opportunity unattractive unless the transaction architecture is redesigned.

# 19. Markets versus hierarchy / internalization

For a one-off consumer purchase, an informal network may be acceptable.

For a scalable business, Felix would have to decide whether recurring functions should remain:
- market transactions;
- professional outsourced services;
- partnerships;
- or internal capabilities.

### Stay external when
- reliable specialists exist;
- performance is easy to verify;
- contracts work;
- transaction frequency is low;
- scale does not justify fixed internal capability.

### Consider more control/internalization when
- transactions are repeated;
- failure costs are high;
- counterparties are unreliable;
- information asymmetry is persistent;
- specialized knowledge becomes strategic;
- coordination repeatedly consumes management time;
- scale makes internal capability economical.

# 20. OLI lens

If the opportunity became a larger international business, OLI provides a useful structure.

## Ownership advantage
What FSA would the importer possess that local/other firms lack?

Candidates:
- European market access;
- logistics/compliance know-how;
- supplier network;
- brand;
- customer relationships;
- ability to aggregate demand;
- repeatable operating system.

## Location advantage
Why Uganda / why this location?

Candidates:
- coffee quality;
- coffee-production resources;
- attractive sourcing economics;
- trade preferences;
- product differentiation through origin.

## Internalization advantage
Why own/control part of the activity rather than rely entirely on arm's-length actors?

Candidates:
- unreliable export channels;
- repeated coordination costs;
- documentation quality control;
- need for consistent customer experience;
- specialized sourcing/quality knowledge.

# 21. Questions for class

1. Which transaction costs are **one-off learning costs** and which would persist at scale?
2. Which intermediary creates the most value?
3. Which intermediary creates the largest dependency?
4. What would happen to economics at 50 kg, 500 kg, or several tons?
5. Which functions should be outsourced, partnered, or internalized?
6. What FSA could make a European importer competitively defensible?
7. Does zero tariff materially offset the non-tariff/institutional/logistics frictions?
8. How would professional commercial documentation change the risk profile?
9. What would need to change before the opportunity becomes scalable rather than anecdotal?

# 22. Generalizable lesson

> An international opportunity can look highly attractive when evaluated only through product cost and tariff rates, yet become much less attractive once the full costs of coordinating, paying, transporting, documenting, monitoring, and complying across borders are included.

The managerial task is to identify **where the friction sits in the value chain** and choose the governance mechanism—market, intermediary, partnership, or hierarchy—that addresses it most efficiently.

The Bean of Africa 
Cross -Border Trade, Intermediaries, and the True Cost of a Morning Cup 
 
On a Thursday evening in Vienna, Felix Wagner set down his glass and looked across the dinner 
table at his friends. The conversation had turned to customs, paperwork, and a bracelet from 
Jordan that had cost its recipient nearly 200 euros in import duties before a single bureaucratic 
error was even identified. Felix’s coffee, five kilograms of it, was currently somewhere between 
Kampala and Vienna. The EMS tracking system listed the shipment as “received for export 
shipment.” He had no idea whether it was on a plane, stuck in a sorting facility, or sitting 
unopened on a shelf in Entebbe. More worryingly, he had no idea whether the Austrian customs 
authority would treat the shipment the way he had assumed it would. 
The coffee was Mt. Elgon Wild Coffee’s Sunset Gold Roast – light, fragrant, and, when Felix 
had first tasted it during a safari in Uganda several months earlier, the best breakfast coffee he 
had encountered in years. Getting it back to Austria had seemed straightforward at the time. In 
retrospect, it had been anything but. 
Uganda and the East African Coffee Trade 
Uganda, often called the “Pearl of Africa,” lies at the center of the African Rift Valley. Its 
241,000 km² span a diverse environment ranging from high mountains to savannah and 
rainforests. It is home to Africa ’s Big Five as well as to two ape subspecies: the impressive 
mountain gorillas and the resourceful chimpanzees. Uganda is a landlocked country of roughly 
48 million people located in East Africa, bordered by Kenya to the east, Tanzania and Rwanda 
to the south, the Democratic Republic of Congo to the west, and South Sudan to the north 
(Exhibit 1). Its economy is predominantly agricultural (Exhibit 2); coffee is among its most 
important export commodities (Exhibit 3), accounting for a significant share of export revenue. 
Uganda produces both Robusta coffee, for which it is one of the world’s largest exporters, and 
arabica varieties grown in the highland regions near Mount Elgon and the Rwenzori Mountains. 
Despite its agricultural richness, Uganda faces persistent institutional challenges. Logistics 
infrastructure is underdeveloped relative to regional peers such as Kenya. Payment systems 
remain fragmented, with mobile money platforms such as MTN Mobile Mone y and Airtel 
Money handling a large share of consumer transactions, while international bank transfers incur 
significant fees and frequently experience technical failures. The Ugandan shilling is subject to 
exchange rate volatility, and small , informal exporters have limited experience or resources 
preparing the documentation that international customs authorities require. 
For importers in the European Union, Uganda ’s status as a Least Developed Country (LDC) 
under World Trade Organization classifications is commercially significant. Under the EU ’s 
Everything But Arms (EBA) scheme, goods originating from LDCs may enter the EU market 
at zero tariff. Felix had discovered this when consulting TARIC, the EU ’s online tariff portal, 
before his first order (see Exhibit 4). On paper, importing Ugandan coffee into Austria looked 
attractively straightforward.

illustrate either effective or ineffective handling of a business situation.

Mt. Elgon Wild Coffee and the Safari Connection 
Mt. Elgon Wild Coffee is a small Ugandan specialty coffee producer and roaster, founded to 
bring premium Ugandan arabica and robusta to an international audience. The company 
markets itself on the richness of Uganda ’s volcanic soils and the wild -grown character of its 
beans. Its website , polished by regional standards , lists several roast profiles and ships 
internationally, advertising delivery to Europe as one of its services. 
Felix and his wife had first encountered Mt. Elgon Wild Coffee indirectly. They had traveled to 
Uganda with Otter Safaris , a Ugandan tour operator specializing in East African wildlife 
experiences. Their guides, Laban and Clare, had led them through national parks and into 
Bwindi Impenetrable Forest for a gorilla trek – an experience that had exceeded even their very 
high expectation. When the safari concluded, Otter Safaris had given the couple a going-home 
gift: a 500-gram bag of Mt. Elgon Wild Coffee Sunset Gold Roast. Back in Vienna, the coffee 
tin ran out within a week. 
Felix was not in the business of coffee importing. He taught management classes at the 
university and, over the years, ordered coffee online from various suppliers. Only once did he 
buy coffee from outside the European Union: a consignment of Taiwanese coffee from a small 
roaster in the United States, which involved a modest customs duty. That experience had left 
him with the impression that importing small quantities of specialty goods was manageable, if 
slightly tedious. Uganda, he believed, would be simp ler: no tariff to pay and a roaster with a 
nice, functional website. Still, memories of an armed convoy guarding trucks arriving from the 
DRC and customs inspections at the Uganda -Rwanda border raised questions on the ease of 
doing cross-border business in eastern Africa. 
The Import Attempt 
The Online Channel Fails 
Felix’s first instinct was to order through Mt. Elgon Wild Coffee ’s website. The process 
appeared simple: select the product, proceed to checkout, and enter a shipping address. Each 
time he attempted it, however, the website crashed when he selected Austria as the destination 
country. He tried multiple browsers over several days. The result was the same. 
He then contacted Mt. Elgon Wild Coffee through the WhatsApp number listed on the site. An 
automated message replied instantly. When he followed up, a human responded, but identified 
himself as a member of the technical support team. He would, he said, forward Felix’s inquiry 
to the sales department. Felix heard nothing further.

Clare Steps In 
Felix turned to Clare, the safari guide. Clare , thankfully, agreed to act as a go -between: she 
would purchase the coffee from Mt. Elgon Wild Coffee and arrange its shipment to Vienna. The 
coffee was priced at 14 USD per 500 grams – a price worth paying for an exceptional breakfast 
coffee. He asked Clare to order 2.5 kilograms. 
 
Clare came back with the shipping options. DHL quoted 200 USD for delivery to Austria. Felix 
did the math. The 2.5 kilograms of coffee he wanted would cost roughly 70 USD. Adding 200 
USD in shipping made no commercial sense whatsoever. Helpful as ever, Clare came up with 
alternatives: FedEx quoted 150 US D, and Posta Uganda quoted 100 USD, with an estimated 
transit time of up to four weeks. 100 USD seemed more reasonable. But if he was going to pay 
100 USD to ship coffee from Kampala to Vienna, he needed eno ugh coffee to make the cost 
worthwhile. He revised his order upward and asked Clare to purchase five kilograms, plus an 
extra 500 grams for Clare herself. The total for the coffee came to 140 USD, plus 100 USD for 
Posta Uganda shipping, plus Clare’s own bag. 254 USD in all. 
The Payment Problem 
Now, the 254 USD , or rather 925,000 Uganda n shillings, had to be transferred from a bank 
account in Vienna to Clare’s phone in Uganda. Felix had wired money to Uganda before, during 
the safari booking: a 250-USD transfer had attracted 35 euros in bank fees. That cost, relative 
to a 254-USD order, was disproportionate. 
Clare suggested WorldRemit, a digital money transfer platform that routes payments from 
European bank accounts or credit cards to African mobile money accounts. In Clare’s case, an 
MTN Mobile Money number. Felix set up a WorldRemit account. The first transfer attempt 
failed. So did the second. And the third. There was no clear reason for the failure. Maybe the 
amount was too high, maybe the recipient name was wrong (Clare’s English vs. Luganda name), 
maybe the transaction purpose (“purchase of goods and ser vices”) did not fit Clare’s account 
type. He eventually succeeded by splitting the total into multiple smaller transactions, most of 
which also failed, until enough attempts succeeded to transfer the full amount. The 254 USD 
arrived on Clare’s phone in segments over several days.

Dispatch and the Address Problem 
Clare purchased the coffee and brought it to the Posta Uganda office for export processing. 
Felix coached her on the Austrian address format , a source of anxiety he had not anticipated. 
Austrian addresses require the street name, building number, stairwell identifier, and apartment 
number to be presented in a specific sequence. Errors at any point can make a parcel 
undeliverable. Clare sent a photograph of the completed address label; it was correct. 
 
For good measure, the post office clerks checked one of the coffee tins for drugs. It turned out 
to be just coffee. The parcel was logged into the EMS international tracking system as received 
for export shipment. And there, for a few days, it stayed. 
Dinner, Doubt, and the Jordan Bracelet 
One Thursday evening, two days after the dispatch, Felix and his wife had dinner with another 
couple. The conversation drifted to the Ugandan coffee shipment, and their friends shared an 
experience that Felix found unsettling. A business contact had sent the couple a bracelet from 
Jordan. The package had been stopped by Austrian customs because it lacked a proper customs 
invoice. Unable to assess the declared value, the customs authority had estimated the shipment’s 
value based on its weight . Yet the methodology produced an implausible figure and , 
correspondingly, a high import duty of roughly 200 euros. The couple had paid, received the 
bracelet, challenged the decision through formal channels, and eventually received a partial 
refund, all over several months. 
Felix thought about his coffee shipment. He had checked TARIC. He was reasonably confident 
the EBA zero-tariff rate applied. But he had not spoken to Clare about what documents she had 
filed with Posta Uganda. He did not know whether the customs invoice showed the correct 
value, HS code, or country-of-origin declaration. He did not know who the intercontinental 
carrier would be, or whether they would add their own documentation requirements. He did not 
know whether Austrian customs would ask for a phytosani tary certificate for an agricultural 
product crossing the EU border. 
What he did know was that the parcel had left Uganda two days ago, and there was nothing he 
could do. Still, it was hard not to think about armed convoys and African border crossings.

Arrival 
On Tuesday of the following week, Felix received a push notification through the Austrian Post 
mobile application. A new parcel had entered the Austrian Post system. It had already cleared 
customs. From Kampal to Vienna in about a week. It was hard to believe. 
Immediately, he examined the customs documentation. The applicable tariff rate was zero 
percent. Indeed, the LDC exemption had been correctly applied. There was, however, import 
V AT to pay, assessed on the shipment’s declared export value. The figure on the Posta Uganda 
export documents was 47.39 euros (Exhibit 4). This was considerably less than the 140 -odd 
USD Felix had actually paid for the coffee. Whether the customs invoice deliberately 
understated the value, or whether the Austrian customs authority had applied its own estimate, 
Felix could not tell. In any case, there was no reason to complain. 
He collected the parcel from the post office and paid 16.68 euros at the counter: 9.48 euros in 
import V AT and 7.20 euros in customs handling fees charged by Austrian Post. 
The coffee, when he brewed it that evening, was exactly as good as he remembered.

What Comes Next? 
Felix’s research and teaching focused on international business. As he thought through what the 
import had involved (the failed website, the unresponsive sales channel, the WhatsApp 
intermediary, the expensive and unreliable payment infrastructure, the underdeveloped logistics 
options, the customs uncertainty, the informality of every step ), he found himself asking a 
different kind of question. 
There was, he suspected, a genuine commercial opportunity. Ugandan specialty coffee was 
well-regarded yet underrepresented in the European premium market. The raw material was 
cheap. The LDC tariff exemption eliminated a significant cost factor. And he had just 
accumulated valuable experience about coffee imports from Uganda. 
But the transaction had also revealed how many things could go wrong, and how many parties
