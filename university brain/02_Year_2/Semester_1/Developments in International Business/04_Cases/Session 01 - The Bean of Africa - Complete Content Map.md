## Case setting

The case follows an Austrian consumer/academic, Felix, trying to import specialty coffee from Uganda after discovering it during a safari. What initially appears to be a simple small cross-border purchase becomes a chain of practical frictions involving digital sales channels, payments, intermediaries, logistics, documentation, customs, and uncertainty.

## Country / trade context

Uganda is:
- a landlocked East African country;
- heavily agricultural;
- a major coffee exporter, producing both Robusta and Arabica;
- classified as a Least Developed Country for the trade arrangement discussed in the case.

The case notes that EU trade preferences under the Everything But Arms framework can make qualifying Ugandan goods eligible for a zero customs tariff. That makes the opportunity look attractive on paper, but the transaction still produces substantial non-tariff and coordination costs.

## Transaction actors

Key actors include:
- Felix, the European buyer/importer;
- Mt. Elgon Wild Coffee, the Ugandan producer/roaster;
- Clare, a safari guide who becomes an informal intermediary;
- digital payment / remittance infrastructure;
- MTN Mobile Money;
- Posta Uganda / EMS;
- international logistics alternatives such as DHL/FedEx;
- Austrian Post;
- Austrian customs authorities;
- the EU tariff/customs framework.

The case is useful because each intermediary solves one problem while potentially adding cost, dependency, information asymmetry, or coordination requirements.

## Main friction points

### Sales-channel failure
The producer's web channel does not successfully complete an Austrian order, and direct digital communication does not produce an effective sales response.

### Informal intermediation
A personal contact in Uganda becomes essential to bridge the gap between buyer and seller. The transaction therefore depends on trust and a personal network rather than a robust commercial export process.

### Logistics economics
Courier options are expensive relative to the value of the coffee, making order size and carrier choice economically important.

### Payment-system mismatch
Conventional international bank transfer is costly relative to the order size. A remittance/mobile-money route is used instead, but multiple transfer attempts fail before the payment is successfully completed.

### Address / documentation issues
The exporter and intermediary need to satisfy Austrian addressing and customs-document expectations. The buyer has limited visibility into whether the correct invoice, valuation, classification, and origin information have been submitted.

### Customs uncertainty
The tariff may be zero under the relevant preference, but import VAT and handling costs still apply. The case also illustrates the distinction between tariff liability and the broader cost/uncertainty of customs clearance.

## Outcome

The coffee eventually arrives successfully and clears customs. The tariff treatment is favorable, but VAT and handling charges remain. The important learning is that the nominal product price and tariff do not capture the **true cost of completing the international transaction**.

## Commercial opportunity raised at the end

The experience suggests a possible opportunity in importing Ugandan specialty coffee into Europe because:
- product quality is attractive;
- Ugandan specialty coffee may be underrepresented in the relevant premium market;
- the raw product economics appear favorable;
- preferential tariff treatment can help;
- the first transaction creates experiential knowledge.

But the same experience reveals that a scalable business would require a much more deliberate transaction architecture than an informal one-off purchase.

## Framework mapping

### Institutional theory
Formal and informal institutions shape what is easy or difficult:
- customs rules;
- payments;
- logistics infrastructure;
- documentation standards;
- informal trust networks.

### Liability of Foreignness / costs of doing business abroad
The buyer/importer faces unfamiliarity, information gaps, distance, and institutional mismatch that a domestic transaction would largely avoid.

### Uppsala / experiential learning
The first transaction generates market knowledge. If Felix repeated the activity, he would begin with more knowledge about suppliers, payments, logistics, customs, and documentation, potentially reducing uncertainty and enabling greater commitment.

### Market-entry modes / intermediaries
The case makes visible how many external parties can sit between producer and end customer. A commercial venture would need to decide which roles remain external and which should be managed more directly.

### Transaction Cost Theory
Analyze costs at each stage:
- **search:** finding a workable supplier/channel;
- **negotiation:** coordinating price, quantity, shipping, intermediary roles;
- **contracting:** specifying payment, shipment, responsibility, documentation;
- **monitoring:** payment status, parcel tracking, customs status;
- **enforcement/problem solving:** what happens if payment, delivery, valuation, or customs treatment fails.

### Internalization
If recurring arm's-length exchange remains unreliable or expensive, there may be value in internalizing parts of sourcing, export handling, import processing, inventory, or distribution.

### OLI
A scalable import/FDI decision could be framed as:
- **O:** what firm-specific advantage would Felix's business possess?
- **L:** what location advantages do Ugandan coffee supply and European premium demand create?
- **I:** which activities should be internalized rather than repeatedly purchased from unreliable or costly market intermediaries?

## Questions to use in class

1. Which transaction costs are one-off learning costs, and which would persist at scale?
2. Which intermediary adds the most value? Which creates the most dependency?
3. Would higher transaction volume reduce unit costs enough to create a viable business?
4. Which activities should be standardized, outsourced, partnered, or internalized?
5. What FSA would a new importer need to justify entering this market?
6. Does the trade preference create a real location advantage, or is it outweighed by other institutional/logistics costs?
7. How would the answer change if the business moved from 5 kg to hundreds or thousands of kilograms?
