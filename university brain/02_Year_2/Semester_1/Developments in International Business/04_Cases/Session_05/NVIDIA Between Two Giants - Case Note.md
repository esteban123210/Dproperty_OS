## Decision and study interpretation

Nvidia faces conflicting US and Chinese demands concerning semiconductor access, enforcement, ownership and technological independence.

Use the case-period facts to examine bilateral political risk. A response that satisfies one state may trigger retaliation from the other; a compliant product is not necessarily politically acceptable to both. Assess dependence, unique GPU/CUDA assets, competitors and chokepoints. Compare alignment, diversification, constrained middle-path engagement and exit, with explicit escalation triggers.

Between Two Giants 
Nvidia and the Bilateral Political Risk of Semiconductors 
 
On Monday, August 11, 2025, the White House issued a formal press statement: Nvidia 
Corporation (Nvidia) and Advanced Micro Devices (AMD) were permitted to sell artificial 
intelligence (AI) chips to China’s commercial sector subject to individual export licenses issued 
by the Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS). The approval followed 
a weeks-long campaign by Nvidia’s CEO , Jensen Huang, who had argued before Congress, 
lobbied White House AI czar David Sacks, and met with President Trump. The administration 
framed the decision as strategic: better to have Chinese AI companies “addicted to the American 
technology stack” than to cede the entire market to Huawei. 
Priya Anand, a member of Nvidia’s Global Policy Strategy team, received a message at 11 p.m. 
on August 24, 2025. The short message was from David Park, Nvidia’s Senior Vice President 
for Global Government Affairs, and read: 
Priya. Senior leadership is meeting with a delegation from the National Security Council 
and Commerce tomorrow at 2 p.m. The White House wants to discuss additional 
measures. I need a briefing memo on my desk by 9 a.m. What more might they ask of 
us? What does it mean if we say yes? How do we handle it? 
Priya read the message twice and then opened a blank document. She had ten hours. 
The Longest Week 
The price of the export-license deal was announced immediately after the press conference . 
Nvidia and AMD would remit 15% of all revenue earned from AI chip sales to China to the US 
Treasury. No such arrangement had ever been contemplated in US technology policy. 
Although the Trump administration tried to calm critics by stating that the chips in question 
were “old” and that China already had access to this technology, resistance emerged. US 
Senator Elizabeth Warren (D -MA) claimed that the deal “undercut American technological 
leadership,” and called for Jensen Huang to testify publicly and under oath. Along the same 
lines, former National Security Council (NSC) member Liza Tobin asked whether the US would 
“sell F -35s to China for a 15 % commission.” Legal scholars questioned whether the deal 
violated the Export Administration Regulations, which prohibit ed fees for export licenses. 
Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick acknowledged in a press briefing that the “legality and 
mechanics” were “still being ironed out.”

On Tuesday, Beijing responded. China’s Cyberspace Administration summoned Nvidia to a 
meeting in the Zhongguancun Science Park. Chinese officials told Nvidia’s China country team 
that the H20 chip contained unspecified “backdoor security risks,” including alleged 
capabilities to track users’ locations or to be remotely disabled by US authorities. Nvidia denied 
the allegations. However, within hours, separate reports suggested that ByteDance, Alibaba, 
and Tencent—Nvidia’s three largest Chinese customers —had received informal government 
guidance to suspend H20 purchases pending a security review. Shares in SMIC, China’s leading 
domestic chipmaker, rose 5% on expectations that restrictions on Nvidia would accelerate 
demand for domestic alternatives. 
On Wednesday, a bipartisan group of six US senators introduced emergency legislation to cap 
AI chip exports to China, and require presidential and congressional sign -offs on future sales. 
Among the critics was a former National Security Advisor, who told reporters that the deal was 
“nuts,” that the US and China were competing for leadership in AI, and that the US had just 
volunteered to solve China’s single biggest problem. 
Now it was Thursday morning, and Priya Anand had a task to complete before 9 a.m. 
Priya had joined Nvidia’s Global Policy Strategy team 14 months earlier, after finishing a 
master’s degree in international political economy at Georgetown. She was 27. Her work 
typically involved tracking regulatory developments in the EU and advising on government 
relations in Southeast Asia. The bilateral US -China chip war had always been a background 
issue she monitored, not a situation she had been asked to navigate . That changed when she 
received David’s message. 
The 2 p.m. meeting would be attended by Jensen Huang; Colette Stern, Nvidia’s Chief Financial 
Officer; Tim Teter, the company’s General Counsel ; and David Park himself. The NSC 
delegation would be led by a Deputy Assistant to the President for Technology and National 
Security, whom Priya knew only by reputation. A BIS Deputy Assistant Secretary would also 
be on the call . The subject, according to the calendar invite, was “Strategic alignment and 
forward posture—Nvidia/USG.” 
Priya had already learned enough about Washington to know that language like “forward 
posture” was rarely about the past. 
Nvidia and Its Place in the World 
From Graphics Chips to the Infrastructure of Intelligence 
Nvidia Corporation was founded in Santa Clara, California, in 1993 by Jensen Huang, Curtis 
Priem, and Chris Malachowsky, with the purpose of building graphics processing units (GPUs) 
for the gaming market. For its first 20 years, Nvidia was primarily a gaming and professional 
visualization company, and its chips powered video -game consoles, workstations, and 
Hollywood rendering pipelines. 
The transformation began around 2012, when researchers discovered that Nvidia’s parallel -
processing architecture, designed to render thousands of simultaneous pixel calculations, was 
uniquely suited for training deep neural networks. The CUDA software ecosystem, which 
Nvidia began developing in 2006 as a general-purpose parallel computing platform, became the 
de facto programming environment for machine learning. By the time deep learning emerged 
as the dominant paradigm in AI in the mid-2010s, Nvidia’s hardware and software were already 
embedded in virtually every major research lab and hyperscale data center worldwide. 
For its 2025 fiscal year (ending January 2025), Nvidia reported revenue of USD 130 billion—
a figure that would have seemed implausible even three years earlier. The company’s data -
center segment, which sold AI chips and associated networking equipment primarily to cloud

Nvidia’s flagship products for this segment (the A100, H100, and the newest Blackwell B200) 
were the most sought -after pieces of industrial hardware in the world. On August 5, 2025, 
Nvidia became the first publicly traded company in hist ory to reach a market capitalization of 
USD 4 trillion. 
The company’s top management team was led by Jensen Huang, who had served as CEO since 
he co-founded the firm. Huang, who was born in Taiwan and raised in the US, was a graduate 
of Oregon State University and Stanford . He had become the most recognizable executive in 
the semiconductor industry and a figure with unusual geopolitical visibility. His personal 
influence extended well beyond Nvidia’s boardroom—he had testified before Congress, dined 
with President Trump at Mar-a-Lago, and made multiple lobbying visits to Beijing, where he 
was received with a level of attention rarely afforded to foreign technology executives. He 
dressed exclusively in a black leather jacket , which had become something of an industry 
symbol. 
The senior leadership team also included Colette Stern (Chief Legal Officer), who had joined 
after leaving Qualcomm in 2021 and had been instrumental in navigating earlier rounds of 
export controls; Marcus Webb (CFO), who had previously served at Broadcom and was known 
for his tight management of Nvidia’s balance sheet; and David Park (S enior Vice President, 
Global Government Affairs), who had spent 15 years in US Trade Representative offices before 
moving to the private sector. 
The China Business: 90%, Then 17%, Then 0% 
Prior to October 2022, China accounted for approximately 25% of Nvidia’s annual revenue and 
Nvidia held close to 90 % of China’s AI chip market. That changed when the Biden 
administration implemented its first round of advanced chip export controls , which prohibited 
the sale of chips above specified performance thresholds measured by aggregate interconnect 
bandwidth and other metrics to Chinese customers without a license. 
Nvidia’s initial response was to develop downgraded, China -specific variants of its highest -
performing chips. The A800 and H800 were launched as performance -limited alternatives to 
the A100 and H100, and were designed specifically to fall below export control thresholds. 
When the Biden administration tightened controls in October 2023 to close this gap, Nvidia 
launched the H20, its most capable China -compliant chip, along with two lower -tier 
alternatives. 
The H20 had an uncertain start. By the time of its launch in early 2024, Huawei’s Ascend 910B 
had closed enough of the performance gap that Chinese buyers could credibly evaluate domestic 
alternatives. H20 prices fell as supply outpaced demand. Nvidia’s China revenue declined from 
roughly 25% of total revenue pre-controls to approximately 17% in the 2024 fiscal year. This 
was still a material number, but the trajectory concerned investors. 
The situation deteriorated further in December 2024 when Beijing initiated two simultaneous 
retaliatory measures. China’s State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR) opened an 
antitrust investigation into Nvidia alleging that the company had violated commitments made 
when regulators approved Nvidia’s 2020 acquisition of Mellanox Technologies. Specifically, 
SMAR questioned Nvidia’s compliance with commitments to supply Chinese customers on fair, 
reasonable, and non-discriminatory terms. Separately, four Chinese industry associations issued 
a coordinated statement advising domestic companies that US chips were “no longer safe” and 
urging a shift to domestic alternatives. Informal government guidance to prioritize Huawei’s 
chips over Nvidia’s had circulated since at least May 2024. 
By late 2024, Nvidia’s China business was shrinking rapidly. By the time Huang visited Beijing 
in July 2025 to celebrate the H20 licensing agreement, Chinese sales had effectively collapsed

The Competitive Landscape 
Nvidia’s dominant position in AI chips rested on two interlocking advantages that were difficult 
to replicate quickly. The first was hardware. Nvidia’s GPU architecture, refined over more than 
10 years of parallel investment s in gaming and AI workloads, delivered superior raw 
performance and energy efficiency for AI training and inference. The second was software. The 
CUDA ecosystem, comprising millions of lines of optimized libraries, frameworks, and 
developer tools, had grown over 15 years into a form of platform lock -in. The process of 
rewriting AI workloads for a different chip architecture was expensive, slow, and technically 
uncertain. 
Nvidia’s primary Western competitor in the AI chip space was AMD, whose MI -series GPUs 
had captured a modest but growing share of the hyperscale data -center market. Intel, once the 
world’s dominant semiconductor firm, was attempting a costly and, so far, unsuccessful pivot 
to AI chips through its Gaudi architecture. The company’s Foundry division had reported a USD 
18.8 billion loss in 2024 and was the subject of an unprecedented government rescue. Neither 
AMD nor Intel posed an immediate threat to Nvidia’s leadership in frontier AI -training 
hardware. 
The more consequential competitive dynamic was in China. Huawei’s Ascend 910C chip, 
manufactured by SMIC using repurposed deep -ultraviolet (DUV) lithography equipment 
through a multi -patterning technique, had achieved performance approaching 800 TFLOPS . 
This was still well below Nvidia’s Blackwell B200 at roughly 2,500 TFLOPS, but the chip was 
closing the gap through an innovative system-level design. Huawei’s CloudMatrix 384, a cluster 
of 384 Ascend chips connected via optical networking, could approach competitive 
performance on certain AI inference tasks but consumed four times the power of a comparable 
Nvidia system. Chinese researchers at DeepSeek had simultaneously demonstrated that 
software-level efficiency improvements could partially compensate for hardware gaps. The 
trajectory in China was not in Nvidia’s favor. 
The Value Chain: Dependence in Both Directions 
Nvidia’s business model was fabless —the company designed chips but outsourced 
manufacturing to foundries. Its primary manufacturing partner was Taiwan Semiconductor 
Manufacturing Company (TSMC), which fabricated the overwhelming majority of Nvidia’s 
advanced chips at its facilities in Hsinchu and Tainan, Taiwan, using extreme ultraviolet (EUV) 
lithography equipment available only from the Dutch firm ASML. Nvidia’s chips were then 
assembled into servers by contract manufacturers, most notably Supermicro and Foxconn, and 
packaged with high-bandwidth memory (HBM) from SK Hynix and Samsung. 
This supply chain created dependencies that ran in multiple directions. Nvidia depended on 
TSMC for fabrication; TSMC depended on ASML for equipment; ASML depended on 
components from Japan, Germany, and the United States. The entire chain was geographically 
concentrated around Taiwan. A confidential 2022 report prepared by the Semiconductor 
Industry Association and McKinsey, portions of which were leaked to the press, found that a 
naval blockade of Taiwan would produce an economic contraction worse than the Great 
Depression with an 11% decline in US GDP and a 16% drop in Chinese GDP. 
At the same time, Nvidia’s relations in China were not purely with commercial buyers. China 
controlled approximately 90% of global refining capacity for high-grade graphite, the dominant 
anode material in lithium -ion batteries used in data -center cooling and power systems. It 
controlled comparable shares of gallium, germanium, and dysprosium, rare-earth element s 
critical for the permanent magnets used in GPU cooling systems. In December 2024, following

Nvidia was embedded in a global value chain that neither the US nor China could unilaterally

The Regulatory Environment: A Moving Target 
Starting in October 2022, the US government imposed three successive rounds of advanced 
chip export controls, each tightening the performance thresholds and expanding the list of 
controlled entities. BIS administered the regime through the Export Administration 
Regulations, which required export licenses for chips above specified parameters when sold to 
Chinese customers. 
Enforcement was structurally limited. BIS employed fewer than 600 people with an annual 
budget of approximately USD 200 million, making it a small agency charged with monitoring 
one of the most technically complex global supply chains in existence. Smuggling networks 
emerged almost immediately. A January 2024 Reuters investigation found that Chinese military 
units, state-sponsored AI institutes, and elite universities, including entities on the US Entity 
List, had acquired small quantities of restricted Nvidia A100 and H100 chips through 
intermediaries in India, Taiwan, and Singapore. Multiple criminal prosecutions followed, 
including the August 2025 arrest of two Chinese nationals in California who had routed tens of 
millions of dollars in H100 chips through freight forwarders in Singapore and Malaysia. Federal 
prosecutors separately charged a Supermicro co -founder with running a USD 2.5 billion 
smuggling operation involving pass-through entities in Southeast Asia. 
The Trump administration’s approach to export controls differed substantially from its 
predecessor’s. While the Biden administration pursued a strategy of multilateral restrictions, 
coordinating controls with Japan, the Netherlands, South Korea, and other allied nations, the 
Trump administration signaled a preference for transactional bargaining. The 15 % revenue-
sharing arrangement struck with Nvidia in August 2025 had no precedent in US export-control 
law and prompted legal challenges before it was even formally implemented. 
8:47 a.m. 
By 8:47 a.m., Priya had a rough outline of the demands that Nvidia might face. She was 
confident the NSC-Commerce meeting would not be a status update. The administration wanted 
to advance what James Okafor, Nvidia’s Director of Federal Government Relations, had called 
“the partnership.” From the information she had gathered, she understood that the demands that 
might be placed on Nvidia were complex and contradictory. 
The critical point was that every demand from Washington raised a corresponding vulnerability 
for Beijing. Priya had constructed a rough map over her second cup of coffee and she found it 
clarifying in a way she had not expected. If Nvidia conceded to a US demand, China would 
respond with allegations of security backdoors or unfair competition, and might even retaliate 
with trade sanctions, as had happened in the case of Nexperia. 
None of this was certain. Beijing had its own reasons for wanting the best available AI chips, 
and the domestic alternative was not yet a perfect substitute. However, China had repeatedly 
demonstrated its willingness to bear economic costs in order to impose geopolitical costs on its 
counterparts. The Nexperia reversal notwithstanding, the trajectory was toward more 
willingness to use supply chain leverage, not less. Moreover, the government in Beijing applied 
a long-term perspective, one that went beyond the US electoral cycle. 
Priya wrote a single line at the top of her page of notes and circled it:

Every “yes” to Washington is a reason for Beijing to say “no.” Every “yes” to Beijing is a 
reason for Washington to say no. 
The question was not whether to manage bilateral political risk. It was whether Nvidia could 
afford to meet either government’s demands without triggering an escalating response from the 
other. If it could not, how could it navigate the space between them? 
Priya saved her draft and leaned back in her chair. Through the glass wall o n the open-plan 
floor, she saw David Park’s assistant setting up the conference room for a pre -brief. In 73 
minutes, the memo would need to be on David’s desk. In five hours, Jensen Huang, Colette 
Stern, and Marcus Webb would sit across a table from the National Security Council. 
She read through what she had written. The likely demands were clear. The implications were, 
she thought, well -characterized. The third section on managing the demands was the hardest 
and she was not sure she had it right. She had sketched out three broad orientations that Nvidia’s 
leadership might consider. 
She labeled the first “full alignment”—cooperate with the administration’s demands, accept the 
enforcement role, commit to home -shoring, and explore governance arrangements. The logic 
was that Nvidia’s long -run success depended on its standing in the US, and that the Chinese 
market, while valuable, was structurally at risk regardless of Nvidia’s choices. Huawei would 
close the gap, and Washington’s protection was worth more than Beijing’s goodwill. 
The second was “strategic non -compliance”—accept the minimum demands necessary to 
maintain export licenses, avoid anything that could be characterized as active enforcement 
participation, and preserve optionality with China. The logic was that Nvidia’s leverage derived 
from its indispensability to both sides . More specifically, neither government could afford to 
lose all access to Nvidia’s technology. That leverage required remaining genuinely dual-use as 
a company that neither government could credibly threaten to lose. 
The third was “boundary-led engagement”—a deliberate, public articulation of what Nvidia 
would and would not do, basically defining the perimeter of its cooperation before the 
administration did so, as Sofia Marchetti ,a senior partner at a Washington law and lobbying 
firm who counted both Nvidia and several allied governments among her client s, had advised. 
This approach required constructing a framework that could be presented to both governments 
as principled rather than political: here is where commercial and security interests align, here 
is where they do not, and the line does not move based on who is asking. 
Each approach had its merits and costs. Each would be received differently in Washington and 
Beijing. 
Priya looked at the clock: 8:51 a.m. She had nine minutes. 
She thought about what Jensen Huang had said at the Beijing press conference in July when a 
reporter asked how he balanced US and Chinese expectations: “Nvidia serves the world’s 
researchers, engineers, and scientists. That is who we are.” It was a good line. It was also, she 
now realized, a response that left every hard question unanswered. 
She put her hands back on the keyboard. 
As Priya prepared to finalize her memo, the calendar alert on her screen refreshed. The 2:00 
p.m. meeting had been moved up. It was now scheduled for 10:30 a.m.
