On a cold afternoon in early January 2023, Matthew Mace, chief scientific officer and director of Sales and 
Marketing at Swedish technology start-up Acorai AB (Acorai), arrived at his office in the technology hub 
at Helsingborg, a coastal city in the south of Sweden. Mace was experiencing mixed feelings : It was the 
best of times, and it was the worst of times. 
Being one of the four co-founders of the start-up, Mace was on a high because the interim results of an ongoing 
clinical study in Sweden had just come in and the company’s heart monitor prototype, the subject matter of the 
study, had demonstrated its equivalence to the prevailing gold standard of cardiac pressure measurement, called 
invasive blood pressure (IBP). IBP was the best-in-class measurement of cardiac pressure not only because it 
was accurate but also because it provided continuous, beat-by-beat measurement. Since it was invasive, however, 
the technique required accessories like an intra-arterial cannula, fluid-filled tubing, a pressure transducer, and a 
microprocessor with a display.1 The clinical evidence of its readings, being on par with the ruling benchmark, 
carried the promise of a bright future for the non-invasive prototype that Acorai’s co-founders were planning to 
soon usher into an assembly line for manufacturing. 
But Mace was also on a low because the company was running out of money. The monthly cash burn was 
around €85,0002 for the seven-employee start-up (see Exhibit 1). Having just paid the salaries for the month 
of December, the company had less than €50,000 in its cash account at the bank. The company was not 
eligible for credit because the three-and-half-year-old start-up did not yet have a regular revenue stream. It 
had been chugging along, from month to month, fuelled by piecemeal donations from both high-net-worth 
individuals (HNWIs) among friends and well -wishers of the co -founders and the occasional grants from 
agencies like the European Innovation Fund and European Institute of Technology. 
It was a time of mixed emotions also because the clinical evidence, even if interim, had placed the co -
founders at the cusp of powering a renaissance in the world of hemodynamics, as the study of human 
anatomy dealing with the flow of blood through the cardiovascular system was known. Once the company’s 
faltering momentum picked up, Acorai would have a first -mover advantage in a nascent segment of the 
competitive market for heart monitors.

MEDICAL DEVICES INDUSTRY 
The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) defined a medical device as “an instrument, 
apparatus, implement, machine, contrivance, implant, in vitro reagent, or accessory which does not achieve 
its primary intended purposes through chemical action within the body and which is not dependent upon 
being metabolized for the achievement of its primary intended purposes.”3 
There were about two million types of medical devices in the world market, categorized into more than 
7,000 generic device groups, according to the World Health Organization. 4 Ranging from adhesive 
bandages and hearing aids to insulin pumps and pacemakers, they were used in diverse settings—in homes, 
clinics, and advanced medical facilities. There were, however, six broad product categories in the medical 
devices industry in the U nited States: electromedical cardiovascular devices (comprising 21.7 per cent by

HEART FAILURE 
Heart failure was defined by the Heart Failure Society of America as “a clinical syndrome with symptoms 
and/or signs caused by a structural and/or functional cardiac abnormality and corroborated by elevated 
natriuretic peptide levels and/or objective evidence of pulmonary or systemic congestion.”9 
When the heart could not pump sufficient blood for circulation in the human body, it led to a disorder 
known as heart failure. Its early sign was an increase in the pressure inside the heart , called pulmonary 
capillary wedge pressure (PCWP). The pressure was an important indicator of an individual’s risk 
propensity for an existing or imminent heart failure. The normal PCWP was between 4 mmHg (millimetres 
of mercury) and 12 mmHg. 10 Elevated levels of the pressure showed up in noticeable symptoms like 
shortness of breath and fatigue in the short term, and swollen feet/weight gain in the medium term. 
Notwithstanding the classic symptoms of breathlessness and listlessness, heart failure presented a 
diagnostic challenge for cardiac care professionals because the symptoms were not only nuanced but 
surfaced after heart failure had already set in. The litmu s test was therefore to identify heart failure well 
before the external symptoms could start showing up. The only tool used in a cardiac care environment to 
measure PCWP was the pulmonary wedge catheter (PWC), which had become the gold standard for 
cardiologists worldwide.11 Manufactured by companies like GE HealthCare, the PWC was a balloon-tipped, 
multi-lumen tube inserted through a central vein to be advanced into a branch of the pulmonary artery. 
Once inside, the balloon was inflated, blocking the pulmonary artery and providing, in turn, a pressure 
reading equivalent to the pressure of the left atrium of the heart. 12 PWC offered accurate, real -time heart 
hemodynamic data and facilitated individualized management strategies.

PRODUCT 
The original idea was for Acorai to develop a remote monitoring tool for individual patients in tune with 
the trend of consumerization of devices, popular among members of the venture capitalist community. 
However, the founders soon realized that the immediate demand was for a tool that could be deployed in 
hospital settings. This was also an untapped category of medical diagnostics with little competition. 
Cardiologists and cardiac nurses were seeking a device that could help them manage patients who were 
already in the hospital. The foun ders realized that a proof of concept in an ICU would set the stage for 
taking the device out of hospitals and into outpatient rooms, and then, in due course, into home-care settings. 
They also saw a higher value proposition in a device that could measure the cardiac pressure in an ICU 
several times a day so that clinicians could see the trend of heart pressure readings over time. 
Robin Lee, a product developer at Acorai, explained, 
The product development team conceptualized a hand-held unit of roughly the size and shape of a 
smartphone. In-house research into non-invasive detection of fluid pressures led us to a recognition 
of sensors as the medium with which to pick up vibrations corresponding to the circulation of blood. 
We also looked at non -medical industries for best practices in engine diagnostics and fluid 
dynamics. Having chanced upon the oil and gas sector for its sensor stack and technology stack, 
we used them as benchmarks in designing a heart monitor prototype. 
The team discovered that it had to use not one, but four types of sensors to detect the vibrations on the surface 
of a patient’s chest: a combination of seismic, acoustic, visual, and electrical sensors. This led to the acronym 
SAVE, which the founders patented. The decision to include a range of sensors in the hardware was meant to 
eliminate the risk of bias in measurement and ensure the device could adapt outputs to the type of patient it was 
evaluating. For example, visual sensors were less effective in making a final estimate of a patient with darker 
skin pigment, and the device had to have reinforcements incorporated into it internally to make up for it.

The SAVE Sensor System thus comprised (1) seismocardiography, which was a technique for measuring 
the mechanical vibrations generated by the heart and for isolating wave patterns related to cardiovascular 
hemodynamics; (2) audio -phonocardiography, which collected audible signals from heart sounds using 
digital microphones targeted to specific frequencies ; (3) photoplethysmography, which derived the pulse 
wave transit time holding an inverse relationship with pressure ; and (4) electrocardiography , which 
provided timing to other sensing modalities and carried AI enhancements. The data was collected just by 
holding the device against the heart for a few minutes. It identified signs of heart failure progression, within 
seconds, by the machine learning engine in the device. The cardiologist could thus manage the risk factors 
associated with heart failure without elaborate surgery by simply adjusting medication. 
Its unique hardware architecture and ability to analyze data with AI models —built entirely in-house—set 
the Acorai heart monitor prototype apart ( see Exhibit 2). It could detect impending heart failure 
hospitalizations 30 days before they would actually happen. The device could stop people from developing 
recurrent heart failure symptoms and filling up emergency rooms. 
The team soon realized that an accurate wedge pressure monitor could be used in many other settings in 
operating theatres. For example, interventionist physicians, valve physicians, and even non -cardiologists 
like anesthetists could find applications for the device.

THE WAY FORWARD 
The Acorai co-founders were aware that building a company from scratch required not only entrepreneurial 
grit and willingness to take risks but also a dose of luck. Developing a med -tech device was particularly 
complex; notwithstanding that, in addition to reaching the gold standard with its prototype, the Acorai heart 
monitor was entering the virgin territory of non-invasive monitoring. Funding was a constraint. A cheque for 
€200,000 from an HNWI well-wisher was on its way and would help the company survive for two months. 
Staying local would make sense in light of the prevailing cash crunch. For Acorai, growth meant going 
beyond the comfort zone of Sweden into new and expanding markets. The founders could postpone the 
decision to grow outside of Sweden for only so long. The y felt pressure, in light of the favo urable clinical 
results in January 2023, to expand to new countries sooner rather than later. 
Staying Local 
Acorai was an unknown company with a low profile in the health care ecosystem, even within Sweden. It 
was not easy for the founders to get commitment from hospitals to conduct clinical trials and register cardiac 
patients. It did not have the product ready, and convincing them that Acorai was a real company was tough. 
All it had were two prototypes that could serve the needs of two ongoing clinical trials, albeit slowly. Yet 
in Sweden the cost was “cheap,” as the founders recognized it, at €100 per patient. The Swedish study 
would be less expensive but would last two years to generate all the evidence needed to apply for regulatory 
approval by the EU under MDR. 
Continuing as a small research organization in Sweden for the next two years with a monthly cash burn of 
about €85,000 was a sustainable proposition, as regulatory approval by the EU under MDR (based on two-
year data ) would open doors for expansion within Europe. The company could set itself on a growth 
trajectory before the decade was out and embed itself as a European company.

The role of key opinion leaders among cardiologists was two-fold. The first was to independently appraise 
the technology, make sure that it worked in clinical trials, and then put it through stress tests to demonstrate 
that it worked in all the patient groups regardless of the underlying etiology of their cardiac condition. The

through peer-reviewed publications, would trigger their second role , of recommending funding from the 
relevant government agencies for either making the device through grants or using the device through 
reimbursements. It was a different level of conversation altogether involving representations to the FDA 
and the payer community that they should make the funding available for making/using the product.
