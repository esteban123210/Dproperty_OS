# Ghemawat (2012) - Actually the World Isn't Flat

**Author:** Pankaj Ghemawat  
**Format:** TEDGlobal talk / transcript  
**Session:** 1 - What is International Business? Why do MNEs exist?  
**Role:** Required reading  
**Course use:** Globalization, semi-globalization, home bias, cross-border frictions, evidence discipline

## Core proposition

The world is substantially less integrated across national borders than popular discourse suggests. Cross-border flows are important but far from the levels that would be expected in a genuinely borderless or "flat" world. Ghemawat argues that exaggerated perceptions of globalization distort business and public-policy decisions. He calls this tendency **globaloney**.

## Transcript / content

### Opening: how globalized are we?

Ghemawat opens by saying he wants to discuss how globalized the world is, how globalized it is not, and why accuracy in judging globalization matters. He identifies a dominant view—held both by many advocates and critics of globalization—that national borders matter very little, cross-border integration is close to complete, and the world functions as one integrated space.

He emphasizes that this is not a new idea. Similar claims appeared in the nineteenth century; he cites David Livingstone writing in the 1850s that the railroad, steamship, and telegraph were integrating East Africa with the rest of the world. The recurring mistake is to infer from new technologies that borders have ceased to matter.

### 01:54 - Measure actual cross-border shares

His preferred way to test the "flat world" claim is empirical: examine activities that can occur either domestically or across borders and calculate the cross-border component as a share of the total.

He focuses on four categories:
- information flows;
- people flows;
- capital flows;
- trade in products and services.

### 02:45 - Telephone calls

Of all voice-calling minutes in the world, roughly **2%** were international. Including internet telephony might raise the figure to roughly **6-7%**, still far below typical intuitive estimates.

### 03:21 - Migration

First-generation immigrants represented roughly **3%** of the world's population. Again, cross-border integration is real but much smaller than many people assume.

### 03:48 - Foreign direct investment

Of total real investment worldwide in 2010, **less than 10%** was accounted for by foreign direct investment.

### 04:06 - Trade and the problem of double counting

Official export-to-GDP statistics typically showed a figure above 30%. Ghemawat warns that gross trade statistics can double- or triple-count components that cross several borders during production. He gives the example of a Japanese component exported to China, incorporated into an iPod, and then exported again to the United States.

He reports that Pascal Lamy, then Director-General of the WTO, estimated that exports as a percentage of GDP would likely be **below 20%** after correcting for such multiple counting.

### 05:12 - The world is far from the no-border benchmark

Ghemawat compares these observed internationalization levels with a hypothetical no-border-effect benchmark in which internationalization might reach roughly **85-95%**. The observed world is nowhere near that level.

The conclusion is not that globalization is insignificant. It is that claims of near-complete integration are greatly overstated.

### 06:13 - Globaloney

Ghemawat reports surveys in which respondents systematically overestimated internationalization. For four quantities whose actual average was below 10%, respondents often guessed values three or four times as high.

He labels the gap between perceived and actual globalization **globaloney**.

This matters because people may design strategies, policies, or arguments around a world that does not actually exist.

### 07:19 - Why people overestimate globalization

He proposes three main explanations.

#### 1. A dearth of data

Much public debate about globalization relies on narratives rather than measurement. Ghemawat recounts criticism from Thomas Friedman, whose *The World Is Flat* became emblematic of the borderless-world argument, and stresses the need to look for data rather than relying on inherited claims.

The lesson is methodological: **do not accept globalization narratives without checking the relevant flows and denominators.**

#### 2. Peer pressure

Talking about a single integrated world has become intellectually fashionable. Questioning that view can make someone appear outdated, creating social pressure to accept the borderless-world narrative.

#### 3. "Techno-trances"

People often assume that new technology will quickly eliminate cultural, political, and geographic barriers. Ghemawat argues that this is an exaggerated inference.

### 10:08 - Facebook as an example

Facebook theoretically makes a friendship halfway around the world technologically as easy as one with someone nearby. Yet only roughly **10-15%** of users' Facebook friends were in other countries.

The reason is that technology operates on top of an existing network of relationships. Technology can reduce some communication costs but does not automatically erase geography, culture, language, institutions, or pre-existing social ties.

This is an important Session 1 principle:

> Lower technological communication costs do not imply the disappearance of cross-border distance.

### 12:15 - Why accuracy matters: room for more integration

If the globalization "glass" is only roughly 10-20% full, there may still be large gains available from additional international integration. If decision-makers mistakenly believe the world is already fully integrated, they may fail to recognize those opportunities.

### 13:22 - Why accuracy matters: globalization fears

Overestimating globalization can also intensify political fears.

Ghemawat gives the example of France, where survey respondents estimated immigrants at roughly 24% of the population while the actual figure he cites was about 8%.

He also cites a U.S. survey in which people estimated that roughly 30% of the federal budget went to foreign aid, compared with an actual level around 1%. When people were shown the actual data, some became more willing to support higher foreign-aid spending.

The general principle is that **misperceptions about international exposure can distort preferences and policy judgments.**

### 15:11 - Home bias and foreign aid

Ghemawat broadens the argument to the idea of **home bias**: most economic and social activity remains disproportionately domestic.

Using calculations attributed to Branko Milanovic, he notes that OECD countries spent vastly more per poor domestic resident than per poor person in poorer countries—on the order of a 30,000-to-1 ratio.

His point is not that complete cosmopolitan equality is immediately realistic. Even incremental reductions in home bias could generate substantial benefits.

### 16:31 - Closing

Ghemawat concludes that radical openness may be desirable, but because the world remains far from fully open, even **incremental openness** can matter significantly.

## Concepts generated by the talk

### Semi-globalization
The world is neither closed nor borderless. Cross-border flows are significant but systematically constrained by borders and distance.

### Globaloney
The systematic tendency to overestimate how internationalized the world actually is.

### Home bias
Economic and social interactions are disproportionately concentrated within countries or nearby environments rather than distributed randomly across the world.

### Technology does not erase distance
Digital technologies can reduce particular transaction and communication costs without eliminating cultural, institutional, political, geographic, and relational distance.

## Managerial implications

- Do not build international strategy around a generic assumption that "the world is global."
- Measure the actual cross-border intensity relevant to the firm's industry and activity.
- Expect borders and distance to continue creating costs and frictions.
- At the same time, limited integration implies potential opportunities for firms capable of overcoming those frictions.
- Separate technological accessibility from actual behavioral, institutional, and commercial integration.

## Connection to other Session 1 readings

### Ghemawat -> Altman et al. (2024)
Altman et al. update the empirical globalization debate. Ghemawat warns against exaggerating globalization; Altman et al. warn against making the opposite error of assuming that deglobalization is already occurring. Together they support a **semi-globalized, empirically grounded** view.

### Ghemawat -> Zaheer (1995)
If borders and national differences still matter, foreign firms face additional costs relative to local firms. Zaheer conceptualizes those costs as the **liability of foreignness**.

### Ghemawat -> Bean of Africa
The coffee case shows the micro-level frictions behind home bias: payment problems, logistics, customs uncertainty, information gaps, institutional differences, and reliance on intermediaries.

## Exam retrieval cue

**Use Ghemawat when a question assumes that technology/globalization has eliminated borders.** Answer by challenging the flat-world assumption, introducing semi-globalization and home bias, and then identifying which cross-border frictions still matter for the focal firm.
