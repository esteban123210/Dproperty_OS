## Core thesis

The traditional two-party MNE–host-government model is insufficient; FDI bargaining is a dynamic two-tier and multi-party process.

## Mechanism and evidence

Tier 1 consists of bargaining among home and host governments and multilateral institutions, producing macro rules on FDI. Tier 2 consists of firm-level negotiations constrained by those rules. Classical obsolescing bargaining predicts that an investor loses leverage after sunk investment, but changing macro institutions and competing parties can alter that trajectory.

## Application and limits

Do not attribute the simple classical model as the papers final conclusion: its contribution is to revise it. Treaty constraints and institutional credibility vary by country pair and industry. Compare Session 5 weakening supranational constraints with the institutional protection of micro bargains.

THE OBSOLESCING 'BARGAININGMODEL'? 
In this article I puzzle through the rea- 
sons why host governments in develop- 
ing countries have become so much 
more MNC-friendly in the 1990s. I con- 
clude that this shift cannot be explained 
within the framework of traditional bar- 
gaining models, because MNCs-host coun- 
try relations can no longer be viewed as a 
static, two-party negotiation, but rather 
must be viewed as a dynamic, two-tier, 
multi-party bargaining process. Tier-1 
bargaining occurs between host develop- 
ing countries and home (industrialized) 
countries, and takes place bilaterally or 
through multilateral institutions like the 
IMF, the World Bank, and the WTO. 
These negotiations produce macro rules 
or principles governing FDI, anchored 
in bilateral or multilateral agreements, 
which then constrain micro negotiations 
in tier-2 between individual MNCs and 
host governments. At the risk of oversim- 
plification, I would argue that the indus- 
trialized countries have used tier-1 bar- 
gaining to weaken the hand of host gov- 
ernments in tier-2 bargaining, while 
strengthening that of their MNCs, and 
that they have been more successful 
at doing this in some countries and sec- 
tors than in others. The article con- 
cludes with suggestions on how the two- 
tier bargaining model might be tested 
empirically.

New Development Strategy 
There is much evidence that develop- 
ing countries have voluntarily adopted a 
more open stance towards FDI because 
of a change in development strategy. Dis- 
illusioned with closed, import-substitut- 
ing industrialization, many countries 
turned in the 1980s and 1990s to a 
more open, market-friendly develop- 
ment strategy. As part of this shift, they 
lowered tariffs, eliminated quotas, freed 
up exchange rates, relaxed price controls 
and deregulated domestic markets. With 
economic distortions thus reduced, pri- 
vate profit corresponded more closely 
with social profit, obviating the need for 
FDI screening on a case-by-case basis as 
was necessary when distortions were 
high (Wells 1998: 104). 
Vernon (1998: 70-71) argues in the 
Latin American context that the liberal- 
ization of FDI was also triggered by the 
presence of a new generation of well- 
trained, often US-educated, civil service 
with more faith in markets and private 
ownership than its preceding genera- 
tions. It has also been suggested that as 
policy makers in developing countries 
gained experience dealing with MNCs, 
their confidence in negotiating with such 
firms has grown. Another positive factor 
has been the increased diversity of 
MNCs, because in many industries 
American MNCs are no longer the dom- 
inant players they once were; instead, 
they have been joined by European, Jap- 
anese, and, occasionally, Third World 
multinationals. Finally, the local private 
sector in many developing countries is 
stronger today than it was two decades 
ago, which may also have made host gov- 
ernments less fearful of MNCs. In addi- 
tion, some of these local firms have 
themselves become multinationals: in 
1997, outward FDI flows were 10-30 per- 
cent of inward FDI flows in countries 
like Singapore, Korea, Taiwan, Brazil, 
and Mexico; therefore these countries 
were developing a vested interest in lib- 
eral FDI policies. If FDI screening and 
case-by-case negotiation with MNCs are 
no longer desirable or necessary from the 
host country's point of view, the bargain- 
ing model ceases to be a useful paradigm 
for thinking about MNC-host govern- 
ment relations. 
I believe it would erroneous to carry 
this line of reasoning to the point of re- 
jecting the bargaining model out of 
hand-at least as yet. First, although FDI 
policies in developing countries have 
changed from being restrictive to being 
more liberal, there are still significant 
barriers to trade and inward FDI in de- 
veloping countries, e.g., in the case of 
large projects or in sectors such as ser- 
vices. Second, at any given time, the FDI 
policies of some developing countries 
will be less liberal than those of others, 
China being the prime example of a 
country that successfully courted FDI in 
the 1980s and 1990s despite quite re- 
strictive FDI policies. It is therefore more 
helpful to view the FDI policies of devel- 
oping countries as being in various tran- 
sitory stages between the highly restric-

TIER-1 BARGAINING 
I begin the discussion of Tier-1 bargain-

Bilateral Negotiations 
Two developments in bilateral rela- 
tions between developing countries and 
industrialized countries have pro- 
foundly affected FDI policies in the 
former. One is the cutback in aid offered 
by industrialized countries to develop- 
ing countries, and the other is the more 
aggressive approach taken by industrial- 
ized countries, such as the United States, 
to liberalize FDI policies in developing 
countries through unilateral actions and 
bilateral negotiations. 
In the 1980s and 1990s, official capital 
flows to developing countries either stag- 
nated or declined. Grants from rich 
countries to developing countries, as a 
share of capital flows to developing 
countries, fell from 30 percent in 1990 to 
8.3 percent in 1997 (all statistics in this 
paragraph are derived from World Bank 
1998). Official bilateral loans to develop- 
ing countries fell sharply from $11.6 bil- 
lion in 1990 to a net outflow of $ 7.2 
billion in 1996 and an inflow of only 1.8 
billion in 1997. At the same time, multi- 
lateral development finance (consisting 
mainly of IMF and World Bank loans) 
stagnated at $10-15 billion, and their 
share in capital flows fell from 16 per- 
cent to less than 6 percent. In all, official 
development finance fell from $56 bil- 
lion in 1990 to $44 billion in 1997. Fol- 
lowing the debt crisis of 1982, commer- 
cial bank lending to developing coun- 
tries also declined sharply. By 1997, 
private capital accounted for 85 percent 
of capital flows into developing coun- 
tries, about half of which was FDI. Thus, 
MNCs once again became important as 
sources of not only technology or mar- 
keting skills but also of capital, as had 
been the case in the 1950s and 1960s. 
The other important development was 
the signing of bilateral investment trea- 
ties (BITs) that pried away FDI restric- 
tions in developing countries "BIT by 
BIT," as one observer put it (Salacuse, 
1990). A U.S. government document de- 
scribes unabashedly the objectives of its 
BIT program as "promoting U.S. exports 
and enhancing the international compet- 
itiveness of U.S. companies" (US Trade 
Representative's Office, 1999). U.S. BITs 
assured U.S. MNCs the better of national 
or MFN treatment at the time of entry 
and thereafter established clear limits on 
expropriation, with prompt and fair 
compensation. They guaranteed U.S. in- 
vestors the right to transfer funds into 
and out of the country at market ex- 
change rates, prohibited host countries 
from imposing performance require-

quotas, secured the right of MNCs to en-

choice, and provided for international 
arbitration of disputes, with no require-

foreigners to participate in the privatiza- 
tion of large public enterprises in key 
industries, liberalized the real estate 
market and offered tax and other incen- 
tives to foreign investors (United Na- 
tions, 1998: 341). Similar FDI liberaliza- 
tion followed after the Asian financial 
crisis in countries like Indonesia, Malay- 
sia, and the Philippines, and Thailand 
(Ibid). The only developing countries 
among the top-10 recipients of FDI that 
hadn't signed BITs with the United 
States nor sought emergency financing 
from multilateral institutions were 
China and Singapore. 
One might wonder if countries that 
liberalized FDI following a structural ad- 
justment loan reverted to restrictive pol- 
icies after the economic crisis passed; 
after all, conditions imposed by multilat- 
eral agencies do not have the perma- 
nency of bilateral or multilateral agree- 
ments. The evidence shows, at least until 
1999, that this was not the case. One 
reason was that when FDI was liberal- 
ized, the new rules had a multi-year or 
indefinite validity. But the more impor- 
tant reason was that many of these coun- 
tries went back for a second or third 
round of emergency funding from the 
IMF and the World Bank (e.g. Argentina, 
Brazil, Mexico, Venezuela), in which 
case they had to liberalize FDI policies 
even further. In the case of Mexico, for 
instance, after the 1994 peso crisis, addi- 
tional sectors, such as financial services 
and electricity, were opened up to FDI. 
To forestall a negative contagion effect of 
the peso crisis, Argentina liberalized FDI 
policies even further in 1995, especially 
in banking and financial services. 
Privatization of large SOEs also re- 
quired host countries to liberalize FDI 
policies. Between 1989-1993, 30 percent 
of the privatization revenues in Latin 
America and 57 percent of the privatiza- 
tion revenues in Europe and Central Asia 
was raised from foreign investors, two- 
thirds of which came as direct invest- 
ment and one-third of which came as 
portfolio investment (Sader, 1995: 34 
and 42). In the transition economies, FDI 
linked to privatization accounted for 
43.4 percent of total FDI inflows into the 
bloc from 1988-1993, while the corre- 
sponding number for Latin America was 
14.9 percent (Sader, 1995: 17). A partic- 
ularly important consequence of the pri- 
vatization trend was that sectors in 
which FDI had previously been highly 
restricted, such as public utilities and 
other services, were suddenly opened up 
to foreign investment. 
As a result of these trends, competi- 
tion among host countries for FDI pro- 
gressively increased, with each host try- 
ing to be more FDI-friendly than the 
other. Because FDI liberalization oc- 
curred simultaneously in so many coun- 
tries, the bargaining power of MNCs was 
further enhanced, despite incentives and 
liberal host-country policies. Many of 
the early privatizations in developing 
and transitional economies, for instance, 
often attracted only one or two qualified 
foreign bidders each, and sometimes not 
even that (for examples from airlines and 
telecommunications in Latin America, 
see Ramamurti 1996). The heightened 
competition among host countries for 
FDI is confirmed by the reduction in the 
share of the top-10 developing country 
hosts of FDI (excluding China)-from 82 
percent in 1984-89 to 73 percent in 1997 
(calculated from data in United Nations, 
1998: 361-5 and United Nations 1996: 
227-231). In the same period, five new 
countries joined the list of top-10 hosts 
of FDI in the developing world, three of

Multilateral Trade Agreements/Insti- 
tutions. I turn finally to FDI liberaliza- 
tion brought about through the Uruguay 
Round of trade talks. On the one hand, 
this round saw a cut in tariffs imposed 
by developing countries, which at the 
margin reduced the incentive for MNCs 
to undertake market-seeking FDI. But, 
more importantly, the Uruguay Round 
included for the first time several provi- 
sions that affected developing countries' 
FDI policies, although always in the 
guise of addressing trade-relatedissues. 
These included the TRIPS, TRIMS, and 
GATS agreements. In addition, all agree- 
ments were now enforceable through the 
WTO's dispute settlement provisions. 
The Uruguay Round set important pre- 
cedents for raising FDIissues within the 
WTOframework. 
The TRIPS provisions strengthen the 
ability of MNCs to leverage their tradi- 
tional strengths, i.e. intangible assets like 
technology, know-how, brand names, 
and trademarks. This is a boon for MNCs 
in sectors such as entertainment, soft- 
ware, publishing, drugs, and pharmaceu- 
ticals. The TRIMS agreement took away 
the right of host countries to impose lo- 
cal content or trade balancing require- 
ments, although it still allows FDI 
screening and such things as export re- 
quirements, technology transfer, and 
staffing requirements. The agreement 
also created a TRIMScommittee under 
the WTO's Council for Trade in Goods, 
and required the latter to "consider 
whether the Agreement should be com- 
plemented with provisions on invest- 
ment policy and competition policy" (ar- 
ticle 9, TRIMS). In September 1996, 
working groups were formed to study 
these questions. 
GATS has potentially important rami- 
fications for developing countries, inas- 
much as services have typically been 
closed to imports and often require di- 
rect investment to serve local markets. 
However, it did not require countries to 
provide market access or guarantee na- 
tional treatment in services, and permits 
different degrees of opening in each 
mode of service delivery. But an impor- 
tant footnote to Article XVI of GATS 
notes that "if a member undertakes a 
market-access commitment in relation to 
the supply of a service [requiring com- 
mercial presence] and if the cross-border 
movement of capital is an essential part 
of the service itself, that member is 
thereby committed to allow such move- 
ment of capital." (WTO, GATS, Part III, 
footnote 8). The same clarification is 
made with respect to services provided 
by natural persons. Thus, the thin edge 
of the FDI wedge was introduced into the 
GATS deal. 
At the same time that the WTO came 
into being, the OECD began negotiations 
on a Multilateral Agreement on Invest- 
ment (MAI), which developing countries 
were to be free to join if they wished. 
Some observers saw in this the risk that 
developing countries might join MAIon 
the "apprehension that a non-signatory 
developing country will be at a disad- 
vantage in competing for FDI as com- 
pared to a signatory one" (Ganesan 1998: 
2). Although the MAI negotiations ran 
aground in late 1998, developing coun- 
tries' FDI policies are likely to come un- 
der renewed scrutiny in future trade 
talks, even if little progress was made at 
the December 1999 Seattle meeting.

bargaining power of MNCs, while weak- 
ening the bargaining power of host coun- 
tries and limiting their ability to exercise 
whatever power they do have (see Figure 
1). MNCs' ability to protect and profit 
from technology and other intangible as- 
sets (brand name, trademarks) has been 
bolstered. Their ability to bring capital 
into developing countries has assumed 
greater importance as official capital 
flows have declined and private flows 
have increased. Competition amongst 
host developing countries has increased 
the ability of MNCs to play these coun- 
tries against one another, especially for 
export-oriented investments. 
The formation of free-trade areas has 
further improved the bargaining power 
of MNCs, because they can make coun- 
tries within the trade bloc vie for FDI. 
Once a free-trade area has been formed, 
countries within the bloc lose their abil- 
ity to use access to the bloc as a bargain- 
ing chip with MNCs-unless they coor- 
dinate their FDI policies, which is gen- 
erally not the case. Thus, one of the 
unintended consequences of opening up 
the home market to intra-bloc trade has 
been that of also opening it up to extra- 
bloc direct investment. 
At the same time, the bargaining 
power of host developing countries has 
weakened because of falling tariffs, and 
the emergence of several new FDI hosts 
in the developing and transitional econ- 
omies. The ability of host countries to pit 
MNCs against one another has weakened 
in the last decade, because of mergers 
and acquisitions among MNCs, e.g. in 
industries such as autos, pharmaceuti- 
cals, telecommunications, banking, com- 
puters, energy, and oil. Bilateral invest- 
ment treaties with countries such as the 
United States, structural adjustment 
loans from institutions such as the 
World Bank and the IMF, and multilat- 
eral deals such as the WTO have also 
constrained host countries' ability to 
limit foreign ownership, impose perfor- 
mance requirements, require domestic 
dispute settlement, or regulate financial 
transactions. 
TESTABLE PROPOSITIONS 
I conclude the article with suggestions 
for empirically testing the two-tier 
model. To recapitulate, the basic argu- 
ment of this article has been that, 
through Tier-1 bargaining, home govern- 
ments of MNCs have systematically 
weakened the ability of host developing 
countries to extract concessions from in- 
dividual MNCs in tier-2 bargaining. One 
implication is that, given the same 
sources of bargaining power as in the 
past, MNCs ought to enjoy higher bar- 
gaining power today than before, while 
the opposite ought to be true for host 
countries. In other words, if past empir- 
ical studies of MNC-host government 
bargaining were repeated with data from 
the late 1980s or 1990s, the coefficients 
of variables reflecting the bargaining 
power of MNCs should be higher, while 
those reflecting the bargaining power of 
host countries should be lower. For in- 
stance, such a study might replicate the 
methods and samples of Fagre & Wells 
(US investments in Latin America) or 
Lecraw (US, European, and Japanese 
MNCs in ASEAN nations), and test if the 
coefficients are significantly different in 
the predicted direction. 
Pi: Any given source of bargaining 
power for MNCs, such as technology 
or brand name, ought to carry more 
weight in the 1990s than it did in the 
1960s and 1970s. The opposite should 
be true for host countries' sources of 
bargaining power.

difficulties of replicating past studies, it

indirect confirmation of the two-tier 
model, because P1 does not speak to the 
causes of the hypothesized shift in bar- 
gaining power. None of the country-level 
variables proposed in the two-tier model 
appear in P1. Other variables, not in- 
cluded in the two-tier model, could con- 
ceivably cause the hypothesized shift in 
coefficients; for example, the market 
power of MNCs may have increased in 
the interim years, or host countries may 
have voluntarily opened up their econo- 
mies to FDI as they grew richer, and so 
on. 
A more meaningful test of the two-tier 
model would require hypotheses in 
which Tier-1 country-level variables fig- 
ure explicitly. According to the two-tier 
model, countries that had signed a BIT 
with the United States, that had sought 
emergency financing from the IMF/ 
World Bank, or that were members of the 
WTO, would be expected to have less 
bargaining power in Tier-2 negotiations 
than those that did not. This leads to the 
following proposition: 
P2: Country-level macro variables, 
such as those proposed in Tier-1 of the 
two-tier model, will reduce the bar- 
gaining power of host countries (or, 
equivalently, increase that of MNCs), 
after controlling for the micro-level 
variables that determine bargaining 
power according to the traditional bar- 
gaining model. 
P2 can be tested, for instance, through 
a cross-sectional analysis of American 
multinationals' investments in a number 
of developing countries. After control- 
ling for the micro-level variables em- 
ployed in traditional bargaining models, 
the host country dummy variables 
would be expected to have negative co- 
efficients. 
Alternatively, the traditional bargain- 
ing model can be tested in a pair of coun- 
tries that differ sharply in the country- 
level macro variables that appear in the 
two-tier model. The prediction then 
would be that the coefficients of the vari- 
ables measuring MNCs' bargaining 
power would be lower in the country 
that had made fewer country-level con- 
cessions in Tier-1 bargaining. As an il- 
lustration, American multinationals' in- 
vestments in Argentina and China in the 
early 1990s might be compared. Argen- 
tina had signed a BIT with the United 
States, had sought emergency financial 
assistance from the IMF/World Bank 
multiple times in the prior years, and 
was a member of the WTO. In contrast, 
China was a large, important, and pow- 
erful player in the world economy that 
had not signed a BIT with the United 
States, that had not sought structural ad- 
justment loans from the IMF/World 
Bank, and that was not a member of the 
WTO (at least as of mid-2000). I would 
predict that the coefficients of the MNC 
variables would be higher in Argentina 
than in China. 
A third, and more stringent, test of the 
two-tier model would require finding a 
setting in which its predictions actually 
contradict those of the traditional bar- 
gaining model. Then, because the evi- 
dence could not be consistent with both 
models, one or the other model would 
receive empirical confirmation. One 
such research design would compare the 
bargaining power of "strong" MNCs from 
"weak" home countries with those of 
"weak" MNCs from "strong" home coun- 
tries.2 The traditional bargaining model 
would predict that the strong MNC from 
the weak country would have more bar- 
gaining power than the weak MNC from

the strong home country, because an 
MNC's home country is irrelevant in the 
traditional model. But in the two-tier 
model, home-country-host-country re- 
lations matter. Specifically, Tier-2 bar- 
gaining should be almost irrelevant in 
host countries that have conceded most 
of their FDI policy discretion in Tier-1 
bargaining. Therefore, the two-tier bar- 
gaining model would predict that a weak 
MNC from a strong country should have 
more bargaining power than a strong

model, a strong MNC from a weak 
home country would have more bar-

a strong home country, but under the 
two-tier model, the opposite would be 
true. 
To test P3, one might, for instance, 
compare the bargaining power of U.S. 
MNCs in Argentina versus those of Swiss 
and/or Dutch MNCs in China, the as- 
sumption being that the former represent 
MNCs from a strong home country and 
the latter MNCs from a weak home coun- 
try. (Note that "weak" and "strong" are 
defined in relation to a particular dyad of 
countries, and that the illustrative re- 
search design deliberately picks polar 
cases.) The traditional bargaining model 
of the Fagre & Wells type can be run 
separately in each setting. Since the host 
country is the same for all observations 
within each case, only MNC variables, 
such as their R&D- or advertising-inten- 
sity, would be used in the analysis. As- 
suming that the two samples are other- 
wise similar, one prediction consistent 
with P2 is that the coefficients of the 
MNC variables would be higher in the 
US-Argentina sample than in the Swiss/ 
Dutch-China sample. But to test P3 spe- 
cifically, a hypothetical "weak" US-MNC 
in Argentina would have to be con- 
structed by, say, taking the first quartile 
value for each of the MNC variables in 
the model; this firm's bargaining power 
in Argentina can be estimated using the 
regression equation. Similarly, a hypo- 
thetical "strong" Swiss/Dutch-MNC in 
China can be constructed by taking the 
third quartile value for each of the MNC 
variables. P3 will be confirmed if the 
hypothetical weak American MNC has 
higher bargaining power in Argentina 
than the hypothetical strong Swiss/Dutch 
MNC does in China. Note, however, that 
P3 is a stringent test of the two-tier model 
because it asserts that the upper tier is 
"more important" than the lower tier in 
determining relative bargaining power. Its 
rejection would repudiate only this partic- 
ular formulation, without prejudicing less 
stringent interpretations, such as the view 
that variables in both tiers determine rela- 
tive bargaining power. 
Several practical issues will arise in 
testing P1-P3, including the important 
question of how to measure bargaining 
power. A popular measure in past stud- 
ies has been MNC ownership in host- 
country subsidiaries, but the underlying 
premise that ownership mirrors control 
is not entirely valid. An alternative mea- 
sure is the MNC's "effective control" 
over its subsidiary, as adopted in Lecraw 
(1984). A third possibility is to use a 
combination of the above measures. One 
of the strengths of the old bargaining 
model was its robustness-in the sense 
that its predictions held up well when 
tested in alternative ways and with 
"noisy" data. Hopefully, the two-tier bar- 
gaining model will turn out to be equally 
robust when tested empirically.

Relations between MNCs and host 
governments in developing countries in

the 1960s and 1970s could be analyzed 
in terms of each side's bargaining power. 
However, that static two-party bargain- 
ing model has become less relevant in 
the 1990s, because another level of bar- 
gaining-between host governments and 
the home governments of MNCs-has 
become far more important in shaping 
host countries' FDI policies. Accord- 
ingly, in this article I have proposed a 
two-tier bargaining model, whose lower 
tier is the original bargaining model and 
whose upper tier involves country-to- 
country bargaining through bilateral or 
multilateral negotiations. Such a model 
is more useful for understanding and ex- 
plaining MNC-host country relations in 
today's circumstances than the tradi- 
tional bargaining model. 
However, the two-tier model pre- 
sented here still has several shortcom- 
ings. First, it does not recognize the 
increasingly important role of actors 
such as non-governmental organizations 
(NGOs) in shaping the context of MNC- 
host country bargaining. Similarly, na- 
tional associations of MNCs from one or 
more countries have emerged as new 
lobbying groups, targeting both host and 
home governments. Second, the two-tier 
model does not adequately cover the im- 
portant case of service sectors, in which 
FDI liberalization has been less pro- 
nounced than manufacturing. Although 
IMF- and World Bank-imposed reform 
programs have affected FDI policies gov- 
erning services, the Uruguay Round re- 
forms, which affect many more coun- 
tries, were quite limited in this regard. 
When privatizing services, host develop- 
ing countries have conducted case-by- 
case negotiations with MNCs, reminis- 
cent of the traditional bargaining model. 
Like mining concessions of the past, 
these privatizations have entailed con- 
cession or licensing agreements featur- 
ing technology transfer, ownership re- 
strictions, and performance require- 
ments (Ramamurti, 1996; Wells and 
Gleason, 1995). Third, although develop- 
ing countries have liberalized FDI at the 
entry stage, some of them seem to be 
compensating by tightening regulations 
at the operational stage (Luo 2000), in a 
pattern akin to the proliferation of non- 
tariff trade barriers that followed the 
lowering of tariff barriers. 
The two-tier model has important pol- 
icy implications. For MNCs, it draws at- 
tention to the additional leverage that 
home governments can provide them by 
tying the hands of host countries through 
Tier-1 bargaining. The model highlights 
the bilateral and multilateral paths 
through which home governments can 
influence host country FDI policy, in- 
cluding the back-door approach through 
the IMF, World Bank, and the WTO. 
MNCs should continue to lobby for a 
Multilateral Investment Agreement that 
includes not just OECD countries but de- 
veloping nations as well, and through 
which the FDI policy concessions ex- 
tracted piecemeal by industrialized 
countries can be consolidated. Pending 
such a deal, however, MNCs would be 
well advised to continue lobbying their 
home governments to sign more bilateral 
investment treaties with developing 
countries, and to continue to strengthen 
their own bargaining power through re- 
search, advertising, horizontal mergers, 
and lobbying for stricter enforcement of 
intellectual property laws. 
For their part, developing countries 
must work in the opposite direction, un- 
less they are convinced that FDI liberal- 
ization is in fact in their own best inter- 
est-which it probably is in many cases. 
They should realize that Tier-1 bargain- 
ing is chipping away at their FDI policy 
discretion, and that by working with
