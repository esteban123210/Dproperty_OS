# Altman et al. (2024) - Challenging the Deglobalization Narrative

**Authors:** Steven A. Altman, Caroline R. Bastian, Davis Fattedad  
**Journal:** *Journal of International Business Policy*, 7, 416-439  
**Published online:** 7 October 2024  
**DOI:** 10.1057/s42214-024-00197-0  
**Role:** Supplementary Session 1 reading  
**License stated in article:** Creative Commons Attribution 4.0

## Abstract-level claim

The authors challenge the popular narrative that the world has already entered a period of **deglobalization**. Their conclusion is more precise:

> **Deglobalization is still a risk rather than a current general reality.**

Using the DHL Global Connectedness Index and related flow data, they argue that:

- international flows have not generally decreased relative to domestic activity;
- there is no clear ongoing shift from global to regional business;
- geopolitically driven changes remain concentrated around countries at the center of current conflicts;
- globalization levels and trends vary substantially across countries;
- mistaken beliefs about deglobalization can themselves encourage costly policy and strategy errors.

# 1. Why the paper was written

The authors argue that a new myth has emerged. In the 1990s and 2000s, many people made the mistake of assuming that borders and distance were becoming irrelevant. Today, decision-makers increasingly make the **opposite** mistake: they assume a new era of deglobalization has already arrived.

The paper therefore asks what has actually changed in the size and geography of international flows after successive shocks including:

- Brexit;
- the election of Donald Trump and subsequent U.S. trade conflicts;
- the COVID-19 pandemic;
- Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine;
- worsening U.S.-China geopolitical tensions.

# 2. Four empirical questions

The paper is organized around four questions:

1. **Are international flows decreasing relative to domestic activity?**
2. **Are more international flows taking place within rather than between geographic regions?**
3. **Are international flows fragmenting along geopolitical lines?**
4. **How do globalization levels and trends vary across countries?**

The evidence on the first three challenges the claim that broad deglobalization is already underway. The fourth demonstrates why global averages are often insufficient for policy or strategy.

# 3. What counts as deglobalization?

The authors define deglobalization as a **decline in the integration or interdependence of countries around the world**.

This means the concept should be tested with actual interactions among countries—not merely attitudes, policy announcements, corporate statements, or forecasts.

# 4. Five strands of the deglobalization narrative

One of the article's most useful analytical distinctions is its separation of five strands that are often conflated.

## 1. Preferences

Evidence may show:
- declining public support for globalization;
- political backlash;
- populist or anti-globalization movements;
- specialists revising their views of globalization.

## 2. Public policy

Evidence may show:
- trade restrictions;
- industrial subsidies;
- investment screening;
- data-flow restrictions;
- weakening of international institutions;
- new sanctions and strategic trade measures.

## 3. Plans

Companies increasingly discuss:
- reshoring;
- nearshoring;
- friendshoring;
- supply-chain redesign;
- footprint changes.

## 4. Predictions

Analysts and scholars forecast:
- more fragmentation;
- further policy restrictions;
- reductions or geographic redirection in cross-border activity.

## 5. Actual patterns of international activity

This is the article's focus:
- are trade, capital, information, and people flows actually becoming less international?
- are they becoming systematically more regional?
- are they splitting into geopolitical blocs?

The authors' position is:

- strands **1-4** contain many legitimate developments and indicate an **elevated risk** of deglobalization;
- strand **5** does **not yet** show a clear generalized deglobalization pattern.

This distinction is one of the most exam-useful points in the article.

# 5. Why false narratives matter

The authors warn that deglobalization rhetoric can become a **self-fulfilling prophecy**.

If governments and firms behave as if globalization has already collapsed, they may create the fragmentation they fear.

Potential costs cited include:
- an IMF estimate that bifurcating trade into two rival blocs could reduce world GDP by as much as roughly **7%**;
- potentially larger losses from technological fragmentation;
- weaker capacity to address climate change;
- inflationary effects;
- reduced economic resilience.

# 6. Literature context

The article reviews scholarship arguing both for and against current deglobalization.

## Literature emphasizing deglobalization / fragmentation

Themes include:
- Brexit and anti-globalization backlash;
- weaker trade/FDI growth after the global financial crisis;
- protectionism;
- geopolitical rivalry;
- reshoring and supply-chain restructuring;
- weakening multilateral institutions.

The authors note that major academic conferences and policy discussions increasingly treat deglobalization as an established fact.

## Literature pushing back

Other scholars argue that:
- weaker post-2008 flows partly reflect cyclical or compositional factors;
- globalization is changing rather than ending;
- global activity remains resilient;
- reconfiguration should not be confused with reversal;
- geopolitical tensions have not yet produced systemic retreat across all flows.

## Connection to longstanding IB work

The article explicitly places itself in the tradition of correcting misperceptions about globalization.

- **Rugman:** much multinational activity is regionally concentrated.
- **Ghemawat:** distance still matters; the world is **semi-globalized**; people overestimate cross-border integration.
- **DHL Global Connectedness Index:** measures both the size and geographic reach of international flows.

# 7. Measuring globalization: depth and breadth

The DHL Global Connectedness Index is especially important because it distinguishes two dimensions.

## Depth / intensity

How large are cross-border flows **relative to domestic activity**?

A higher depth measure means international flows account for a larger share of the relevant activity.

## Breadth / extensity

How widely are international flows distributed across countries/geographic space?

A system can therefore be:
- highly international but concentrated among a few partners;
- less international in aggregate but geographically diversified;
- deep but regionally concentrated;
- broad but shallow.

The paper's analysis focuses mainly on **intensity** and **extensity** rather than the speed or impact of flows.

# 8. Intensity of international flows

The central empirical conclusion is that there is **no general shift from international toward domestic activity**.

## DHL Global Connectedness depth index

The overall global depth index combines:
- trade - **35% weight**;
- capital - **35%**;
- information - **15%**;
- people - **15%**.

The overall index reached a **new record high in 2022**, with projections suggesting a similar level in 2023.

## Trade pillar

The trade pillar includes trade in value-added terms relative to world GDP.

Findings:
- trade intensity rose to record levels in 2022 in the DHL measure;
- the familiar gross exports-to-GDP ratio recovered strongly in 2021-22;
- in 2022 it was roughly back near its 2008 peak;
- IMF projections suggested only small subsequent changes.

### Why gross exports/GDP can mislead

The paper notes that stagnation or declines in gross exports/GDP can reflect:
- changing composition of the global economy;
- growth of less trade-intensive countries;
- greater consumption of services, which are traded less intensively than goods;
- commodity-price changes;
- region-specific trends.

Services trade intensity has continued increasing even when some goods indicators weakened.

Conclusion: declining exports/GDP after 2008 should not automatically be interpreted as deglobalization.

# 9. Reshoring evidence

The authors find only limited evidence of broad U.S. reshoring.

Examples discussed:
- more imports of upstream manufactured goods could be consistent with reshoring of later production stages;
- the magnitude remains modest;
- manufacturing employment did not show a general reshoring boom;
- much U.S. manufacturing-construction growth was concentrated in electronics and influenced by subsidies;
- the Kearney Reshoring Index improved recently but remained below earlier levels.

The message is not that reshoring never happens. It is that **isolated or sector-specific reshoring does not establish a general deglobalization trend**.

# 10. Capital flows and FDI

FDI flows relative to gross fixed capital formation are below historic peaks and recently showed declines.

The authors argue that this indicator alone is insufficient because FDI flows:
- are highly volatile;
- often peak during M&A waves;
- can be distorted by corporate inversions and conduit flows;
- exclude contractual internationalization modes;
- miss foreign activity financed with host-country capital;
- can be heavily influenced by unusual transactions in financial centers.

The article notes that when Luxembourg's large 2022 transaction is excluded, FDI intensity actually increased rather than fell.

## Why FDI stocks matter

The authors defend FDI stocks as a meaningful indicator of continuing interdependence because foreign ownership creates long-lived production and organizational linkages.

They connect this to internalization theory: FDI involves transferring **firm-specific advantages (FSAs)** across borders in imperfect markets.

The collapse of automotive production after foreign MNEs halted operations in Russia is used as an example of how foreign-owned production creates real interdependence beyond the original investment flow.

# 11. Broader indicators of multinational activity

Figure 5 expands the analysis beyond FDI flows. Indicators include:

- inward FDI stock as % of GDP;
- inward FDI stock relative to stock-market capitalization;
- announced greenfield FDI value;
- announced greenfield FDI project counts;
- cross-border M&A value;
- cross-border M&A counts;
- international share of M&A transactions;
- MNE foreign-affiliate output as a share of world output;
- MNE foreign-affiliate employment relative to the world working-age population;
- international payments for use of intellectual property;
- foreign revenue shares of listed companies.

Taken together, the indicators do not support a broad retreat of multinational enterprise activity.

# 12. Information flows

The information pillar remained around a **record high** in 2022 after large increases over the prior two decades.

Global indicators include:
- international scientific-research collaboration;
- international patenting;
- payments for intellectual property;
- related cross-border knowledge flows.

At country level, the index also considers measures such as international internet bandwidth and foreign news traffic.

# 13. People flows

The people pillar remained below its 2019 peak in 2022.

The authors attribute this primarily to international travel's slow recovery from COVID-19 rather than to a structural retreat from internationalization.

The pillar includes:
- migrant stocks;
- international travel;
- international tertiary education.

Tourism was expected to return to or exceed pre-pandemic levels.

# 14. Geographic regionalization

A major narrative says firms are moving from global networks to shorter, more regional ones via:
- nearshoring;
- regional sourcing;
- local production;
- supply-chain shortening.

The article tests whether actual flows support that claim.

## Corporate responses to disruption

Many firms increased resilience through:
- digitization;
- supplier diversification;
- dual sourcing;
- redundancy;
- inventory changes;

rather than simply moving production closer to home.

Temporary pandemic-era shipping costs also made nearby supply more attractive for a period, but these costs later declined.

## Intra-regional trade

Some earlier work suggested an increase in intra-regional trade beginning around 2012.

Altman et al. show:
- the result depends on how regions are defined;
- the apparent increase did not continue after about 2016;
- after COVID, intra-regional trade shares actually declined under several alternative region classifications.

## Average distance traveled by flows

The paper also evaluates average geographic distance traversed by international flows.

This avoids arbitrary decisions about where regional boundaries should be drawn.

If international activity were systematically regionalizing, average distance should decline. The data do not show a broad, persistent fall consistent with generalized regionalization.

### Geographic conclusion

There is **no clear current macro-level shift from global to regional flows**.

Specific industries, firms, or country relationships may regionalize, but that cannot be generalized to all International Business.

# 15. Geopolitical fragmentation

The article separately analyzes **geoeconomic fragmentation**.

This refers to policy-induced changes in sources and destinations of cross-border flows driven by strategic considerations such as:
- national security;
- economic security;
- sovereignty;
- autonomy;
- geopolitical alignment.

The central concern is whether the world is splitting into rival U.S.-aligned and China-aligned blocs.

# 16. U.S.-China decoupling

The paper finds **real weakening in U.S.-China ties**.

Across 11 types of trade, capital, information, and people flows:
- China's share of U.S. flows declined in **9 of 11** categories;
- the U.S. share of China's flows declined in **8 of 10** categories for which the comparison is reported;
- average U.S. flow share involving China fell from roughly **9.4% to 6.7%**;
- average Chinese flow share involving the U.S. fell from roughly **17.7% to 12.9%**.

So bilateral decoupling is not merely rhetoric.

# 17. Four criteria for geopolitically driven decoupling

The authors caution that not every bilateral decline is caused by geopolitics.

They propose four criteria:

1. **A recent decline exists** in the relevant bilateral flow share.
2. **The decline is unusual** relative to the previous trend.
3. **The timing aligns** with policy changes or a substantial geopolitical-risk increase capable of affecting decisions.
4. **The decline is not simply global**—the partner must not be declining similarly in its interactions with all countries.

Merchandise imports provide one of the clearest cases in which all four criteria support geopolitically driven U.S.-China decoupling.

# 18. Russia

Russia's international relationships were much more dramatically reorganized after the 2022 invasion of Ukraine.

Sanctions and geopolitical realignment redirected:
- trade;
- investment;
- finance;
- technology;
- other international flows.

Russia therefore provides a strong case of severe geopolitical reconfiguration.

# 19. Is the world splitting into rival blocs?

Despite U.S.-China decoupling and Russia's reorientation, the paper does **not** find a generalized worldwide pattern of rival geopolitical blocs systematically severing ties with one another.

The strongest geopolitical fragmentation remains concentrated around the countries directly involved in major present conflicts.

Key distinction:

> **Bilateral decoupling and selective geopolitical fragmentation are real; generalized global deglobalization is not yet established.**

# 20. Country-level variation

Globalization levels differ much more **across countries** than they typically change **over short business-planning horizons**.

Relevant drivers include:
- country size;
- geography;
- logistics/infrastructure;
- openness;
- regional integration;
- institutions;
- sector structure;
- investment regimes;
- geopolitical alignment.

The article notes that many countries' international flows are highly concentrated in just a few partners.

For the average country in 2022:
- more than half of merchandise exports went to just **three** destination countries;
- more than half of FDI inflows came from just **two** origin countries.

Canada and Mexico are examples where more than 70% of exports go to a single partner, the United States.

Therefore global averages can be a poor guide for national policy or firm strategy.

# 21. Reconfiguration is not deglobalization

A central conceptual distinction is between **reconfiguration** and **reversal**.

Example:
- direct U.S.-China trade can decline;
- trade may be rerouted through third countries;
- supply chains may change configuration;
- the activity may still remain international rather than returning home.

The authors treat such adaptation as evidence of the resilience of international business under constraints.

Changing routes does not necessarily mean the underlying activity becomes domestic.

# 22. Policy implications

The paper cautions governments against assuming deglobalization is inevitable or already complete.

Implications include:
- preserve international openness where possible;
- distinguish targeted security risks from broad retreat;
- avoid unnecessarily costly fragmentation;
- consider diversification and resilience rather than simple autarky;
- monitor actual flow data rather than policy rhetoric alone;
- use granular country/partner analysis.

The article notes rising interest in **de-risking** rather than blanket decoupling and discusses the broader idea of **reglobalization** as a policy agenda combining openness with resilience and security.

# 23. Research implications

Future IB research should:
- test globalization perceptions with hard data;
- examine why international activity remains resilient despite a more hostile policy environment;
- improve measures of geopolitical alignment beyond UN voting alone;
- distinguish firm-level responses from aggregate outcomes;
- continue monitoring whether current deglobalization risks eventually translate into actual flow declines.

# 24. Main conclusion

The article concludes:

- pressures against globalization are real;
- anti-globalization rhetoric and policy barriers have increased;
- corporate plans increasingly reference reshoring, friendshoring, and de-risking;
- U.S.-China decoupling is real in important areas;
- Russia's external relationships have been substantially redirected;
- nevertheless, aggregate international flows remain resilient;
- no generalized shift from international to domestic activity is established;
- no generalized shift from global to regional activity is established;
- no comprehensive worldwide geopolitical-bloc split is established;
- **deglobalization remains a risk rather than a current macro-level reality**.

# 26. Managerial implications

1. **Do not confuse rhetoric with realized flows.** Political statements and corporate plans can shift much faster than underlying networks.
2. **Avoid binary globalize/deglobalize thinking.** Networks can reconfigure while remaining international.
3. **Analyze the relevant flow.** Trade, FDI, talent, information, IP, and travel can evolve differently.
4. **Analyze the relevant country pair.** U.S.-China dynamics are not automatically representative of every relationship.
5. **Build resilience without assuming retreat is optimal.** Diversification, dual sourcing, digitization, and network redesign can preserve international benefits.
6. **Expect selective fragmentation.** Some sectors and bilateral ties can decouple sharply while aggregate globalization remains resilient.
7. **Use granular country data.** Global averages are often a poor input for a specific investment decision.

# 27. Connection to Ghemawat

**Ghemawat (2012):** the world is **less globalized than people think**.  
**Altman et al. (2024):** the world is **not deglobalizing as broadly as people increasingly claim**.

Combined lesson:

> International Business requires empirical discipline. Reject both hyperglobalization and simplistic deglobalization narratives.

# 28. Connection to Session 1 theory

The article reinforces several Session 1 concepts:

- **semi-globalization:** borders still matter even when international activity is resilient;
- **FDI:** flows, stocks, and foreign affiliate activity must be interpreted carefully;
- **FSAs:** FDI creates persistent interdependence because firms transfer capabilities across borders;
- **distance/geopolitics:** flow patterns remain structured by geography and political relationships;
- **strategic context:** firms should not treat a global narrative as a substitute for country-specific analysis.

# 29. Exam retrieval cue

**Use Altman et al. when an exam question assumes globalization is reversing, reshoring is universal, or geopolitical fragmentation means all MNEs should retreat home.**

Response structure:
1. Separate **preferences/policy/plans/predictions** from **actual flow patterns**.
2. Test **depth**: are flows becoming less international relative to domestic activity?
3. Test **breadth/geography**: are flows actually becoming more regional?
4. Test **geopolitical fragmentation**: is decoupling bilateral/selective or system-wide?
5. Move to the focal country/industry before recommending strategy.

Literature review
Recent international business (IB) literature has highlighted 
shifts in the international environment and discussed how 
they could contribute to a reversal of globalization, address-
ing multiple strands of the deglobalization narrative summa-
rized in Table  1. Kobrin (2017) is an early and particularly 
far-reaching contribution, discussing the UK’s exit from the 
EU, the election of Donald Trump to the presidency in the 
U.S., anti-globalization backlashes in many countries, and 
the revival of conflict between major states and declines in 
trade and foreign direct investment (FDI) flows after the 
2008 “great recession.” Meyer (2017), another early contri-
bution, focuses on societal and political movements oppos-
ing globalization and marks “the apparent onset of an era 
of anti-globalization” (p. 78). Aguilera et al. (2019) look 
at measures of international trade and investment, along 
with indicators highlighting the rise of populism and armed 
conflict, seeing in these developments an “era of global 
flux” marked, in popular commentaries, by a “trend toward 
deglobalization, or ‘slowbalization’” (p. 61). Petricevic and 
Teece (2019) cite the discussion of deglobalization in Live-
sey (2018) in their argument that a structural reshaping of 
globalization requires “fundamental shifts in international 
business scholarship and management practice” (p. 1487).
In this context, it is important to recognize that IB schol-
ars, along with historians of globalization from other disci-
plines, have long viewed levels of globalization as subject 
to both increases and decreases over time [see, for example, 
James (2020); Jones (2005); Meyer (2017)], contrary to 
characterizations by many political leaders in the 1990s and 
2000s of globalization as both irreversible and subject to 
inevitable further increases (Livesey, 2018). The historical 
record, thus, supports the value of contemporary research 
considering whether present conditions suggest that such a 
reversal could be on the horizon.
As noted earlier, this paper challenges the view that 
deglobalization is already underway and that governments 
and firms should treat this as a historical fact rather than a 
potential scenario (strand 5 in Table  1). Witt (2019) goes 
beyond providing a useful perspective on potential drivers of 
deglobalization grounded in international relations theories 
of liberalism and realism (strand 4) to argue that deglobali-
zation “seems to have been in progress for several years, at 
least with respect to the dimensions most directly relevant 
to International Business, trade and FDI” (pp. 1054–1055) 
(strand 5). Building on that contribution, Witt et al. (2023) 
argue that deglobalization (along with U.S.–China decou-
pling) has been underway for more than a decade. Likewise, 
van Bergeijk (2019) cites developments in trade and capi-
tal flow data, along with shifts in public policy and other 
spheres, to support the view that “a reversal of the globaliza-
tion process has occurred” (and discusses historical paral-
lels). Rajan (2023) also takes the view that “deglobalization 
is well underway,” based on trade and FDI trends, along with 
policy shifts and political developments.
Programs of major conferences in business academia also 
suggest that many scholars have begun to treat deglobaliza-
tion as a foregone conclusion. The Academy of Manage-
ment’s 2023 annual conference theme linked “declining 
globalization…and a shift towards ‘re-shoring,’” to “a per -
fect storm of increasingly severe talent shortages and inten-
sified labor activism.” In 2018, the Strategic Management 
Society’s annual conference theme was “Strategies in the 
era of de-globalization.” Recent Academy of International 
Business (AIB) conferences have also featured several ses-
sions on deglobalization. Likewise, the presumption that 
deglobalization is underway features prominently in public 
policy discourse on supply chain resilience (Thakur-Weigold 
& Miroudot, 2023), in the media (e.g., Economist (2019)), 
in publications by think tanks (Garjewski, 2022), and even 
in some analyst reports from prominent investment banks 
(e.g., Joynson et al. (2024)).
In response, several scholars have pushed back against 
the notion that deglobalization is underway. Bordo (2017), 
in a paper titled, “The second era of globalization is not yet 
over: a historical perspective,” points to cyclical macroeco-
nomic factors as the main cause of weaker global flows after 
the 2008 global financial crisis. Aguilera et al. (2019) view 
recent trends in global economic integration as “in some 
respects less negative than is often perceived in the con-
ventional wisdom or that is implied by the recent focus on 
deglobalization in the academic literature” (p. 61). O’Rourke 
(2019) argues that the first task of economic historians today 
“should perhaps be to calm talk of the imminent demise of 
globalization” (p. 357). Steger and James (2020) warn to 
“avoid the common mistake of confusing global reconfigura-
tion with deglobalization,” and conclude that “globalization 
is far from finished” (p. 200). Evenett (2022) argues that 
the deglobalization narrative has “the flimsiest foundation in 
fact” (p. 350). Grosse et al. (2022), titled, “De-globalization 
is a myth,” points not only to the resilience of international 
trade and investment but also to the limited extent of recent 
increases in trade barriers. Luo (2022) sees globalization 
changing rather than reversing, arguing that we are entering 
an era of “interfered globalization” (more complex and regu-
lated) and “fragmented globalization” (marked by tensions 
and divisions between blocs of countries). Cevik (2023) con-
cludes, based on a gravity model incorporating geopolitical 
alignment that, “there is no systemic retreat in trade glo-
balization due to geopolitical developments” (p. 15). Setser 
(2024), titled, “The Dangerous Myth of Deglobalization”

This paper also adds to a substantial body of IB research 
correcting misperceptions about globalization. For decades, 
IB scholars, drawing upon their understanding of the chal-
lenges that firms encounter doing business in distant and 
different environments, have shown that perceptions about 
globalization are often far removed from reality. Alan Rug-
man, in a (2000) book titled, The End of Globalization, made 
the case – not that globalization was over – but that it mainly 
involves firms expanding within their home regions, setting 
the stage for the publication of Rugman and Verbeke (2004) 
and hundreds of subsequent publications on regionalization. 
Pankaj Ghemawat (2001, 2003) drew the field’s attention to 
how multiple dimensions of distance constrain international 
activity, characterizing the world as ‘semiglobalized.’ His 
subsequent work also highlighted how most managers and 
members of the general public believe international flows are 
much larger than they really are and how such mispercep-
tions can distort business and public policy decision-making 
(Ghemawat, 2018).
The DHL Global Connectedness Index, the first five 
editions of which were co-authored by Pankaj Ghemawat, 
drew upon IB insights on regionalization and semiglobaliza-
tion to introduce the first globalization index with parallel 
treatment, wherever possible, of the size (“depth”) and geo-
graphic reach (“breadth”) of countries’ international flows. 
This index, thus, is uniquely suited to addressing present 
questions about the state and trajectory of globalization, as 
concerns have shifted from a primary focus on the pace of 
globalization’s expansion to whether patterns of flows are 
shifting in ways that reflect either a geographic tilt (toward 
more regionalized flows) or a geopolitical tilt (toward a split 
between rival blocs of countries). The empirical material 
that follows draws heavily from the current DHL Global 
Connectedness Index and from the dataset compiled to cal-
culate it (Altman & Bastian, 2024).
Our analysis of international flows covers the first two 
of four types of measures proposed by Held et al. (1999): 
intensity and extensity, as discussed in Ghemawat and Alt-
man (2017). Our depth measures examine the intensity of 
international flows by comparing (scaling) them relative to 
benchmarks of domestic activity, and our breadth measures 
track the extensity of international flows in various ways, as 
discussed later in this paper. The third and fourth types of 
measures proposed by Held et al. (1999) are velocity and 
impact. We do not attempt to measure the velocity of inter-
national flows (how fast they move between countries) due 
both to data limitations and the sense that there is very little 
variation along this dimension over the focal period of our 
main analysis (since 2001). Likewise, we exclude measures 
of the impact of international flows, to focus on measures 
of globalization itself, which can be related to measures of 
globalization’s enablers and consequences in policy analysis.
As a further connection to the literature on measuring 
globalization, it bears mention that our analysis focuses 
solely on flows taking place between countries, the primary 
type of evidence also employed in the IB literature on deglo-
balization and in the de facto sub-index of the KOF Index 
of Globalization (Gygli et al., 2019). Measures of interna -
tional flows are also used extensively in the Asian Develop-
ment Bank’s Global Integration Index (ADB, 2023). These 
indexes, like the DHL Global Connectedness Index, show 
declines in 2020 followed by increases in 2021. 2 The most 
prominent alternative approach to measuring globalization is 
to look at indicators of its consequences, such as measures of 
price dispersion across markets (see, for example, O’Rourke 
and Williamson (2002)). While price-based measures are 
also valuable, conceptual and data availability constraints 
make it infeasible to employ them across the range of types 
of activity that we include in our research.

macro-level measures show stable or rising levels of inter -
nationalization. For a broad overview, the depth dimension 
of the DHL Global Connectedness Index (Altman & Bastian, 
2024) reports global aggregate measures of the intensity of 
international trade, capital, information, and people flows 
relative to domestic activity (see Fig.  2). These measures 
are shown on a scale running from 0% (meaning no flows at 
all take place across national borders) to 100% (a friction-
less hypothetical with no border or distance effects, mak -

Starting with trade, the global ratio of the value of (gross) 
exports of goods and services to world GDP is the most 
widely used measure of globalization, and many publications 
on deglobalization cite declines in this ratio after the 2008 
global financial crisis as a key piece of evidence. However, 
this ratio rebounded in 2021 and 2022, roughly matching in 
2022 the record high level set in 2008 (see Fig. 3). While the 
2021 and 2022 exports-to-GDP ratios were elevated due to 
high commodity prices, forecasts from the IMF’s October 
2023 World Economic Outlook imply only a small decline 
in this ratio in 2023 and no change in 2024. Thus, national 
economies are on track to remain roughly as connected as 
they ever have been via trade.

After trade intensity, the next most prominent cross-bor-

direct investment (FDI) flows, typically examined relative to

declining trend. However, it would be a mistake to conclude 
from this trend alone that multinational firms are retreating 
from international markets. FDI flows systematically under-

excluding contractual modes and capital raised in host coun-
tries (Beugelsdijk et al., 2010; Lundan, 2018). Additionally,

in Vujanović et al. (2021)). Furthermore, purely financial

involved) can distort these trends. As shown in Fig. 4, when 
excluding Luxembourg (where one large financial transac -

in 2022, rather than decreased.8

Extensity from geographic perspective 
(regionalization)
Measures of the extensity of international flows focus on 
the extent to which countries’ international flows are spread 
out around the world or are more narrowly focused with 
particular sets of partner (origin/destination) countries. In 
this section, we examine extensity from a geographic per -
spective, focusing on the question of whether international 
flows are becoming more regionalized. In the next section, 
we revisit extensity from a geopolitical perspective, consid-
ering whether a rising proportion of international activity is 
happening between geopolitically friendly countries.
Surveys and textual analysis of earnings calls show a 
spike in business interest in supply chain regionalization 
(“nearshoring”) at the beginning of the COVID-19 pan-
demic, followed by another spike as the war in Ukraine 
ramped up (Aiyar, Presbitero, et al., 2023; Alfaro & Chor, 
2023). Many drivers of a potential increase in regionaliza-
tion – especially with respect to trade – have been cited, 
including efforts to boost supply chain resilience, responses 
to rising geopolitical tensions, the development of technolo-
gies such as 3D printing that could potentially favor more 
distributed production networks, the proliferation of regional 
trade agreements, and commitments to reduce transporta-
tion-related greenhouse gas emissions (see, for example, 
Enderwick and Buckley (2020), Lund et al. (2019)). 12
Set against the many reasons business activity might 
become more regionalized, less attention has been paid to 
factors that militate against a substantial increase in region-
alization. As IB scholarship has long emphasized, business 
activity is already highly regionalized, implying less scope 
to boost regionalization than would otherwise be the case 
(Rosa et al., 2020; Rugman & Verbeke, 2004). On average, 
roughly half of international trade, capital, information, and 
people flows already happen within roughly continent-sized 
regions, three times more than would be expected if these 
flows were not dampened by distance and cross-country 
differences (Altman & Bastian, 2021). Additionally, many 
companies have chosen to invest in digitization and diversifi-
cation/dual-sourcing to boost resilience, instead of nearshor-
ing (Alicke et al., 2021). Moreover, unusually high shipping

Extensity from geopolitical perspective 
(fracturing/fragmentation)
Rising tensions between the world’s two largest economies, 
the U.S. and China, along with the global ramifications of 
Russia’s 2022 full-scale invasion of Ukraine, have raised the 
prospect of what the IMF refers to as geoeconomic fragmen-
tation, defined as “policy-induced changes in the sources 
and destinations of cross-border flows, often guided by stra-
tegic considerations, such as national and economic secu-
rity, sovereignty, autonomy” (Gopinath et al., 2024). While 
geoeconomic fragmentation could, in principle, unfold in 
various ways, current research and policy debates focus on 
the possibility of a separation between rival U.S.-aligned and 
China-aligned blocs of countries (Aiyar, Presbitero, et al., 
2023). We do see a clear weakening of ties between the U.S. 
and China, along with a profound reorientation of Russia’s 
international flows. Nonetheless, we do not – at least so 
far – find evidence of a general pattern of rival geopolitical 
blocs cutting ties with each other.
U.S.–China decoupling
Consider first the development of U.S.–China ties, after 
which we will turn briefly to Russia, and then to the rest of 
the world. The term decoupling, which Witt et al. (2023) 
define as “the process of weakening interdependence 
between two nations or blocs of nations,” is widely used to 
describe the weakening of U.S.–China ties, so we will use 
that term here. 15 To align our work with the IMF’s defini-
tion of geoeconomic fragmentation, we use the term geo -
politically driven decoupling for the subset of decoupling 
episodes that are driven either by actual policy changes 
introduced due to geopolitical tensions or by related expecta-
tions or uncertainty regarding future policy changes (Jakubik 
& Ruta, 2023).
Figure  8 presents a broad overview of changes in the 
shares of 11 types of trade, capital, information, and people 
flows taking place between the U.S. and China between 2016 
and the most recent year with data available (2023 for most 
flows). The share of U.S. flows that were to or from China 
fell in nine out of 11 categories, declining on average from 
9.4% to 6.7%, while the share of China’s flows involving 
the U.S. declined in eight out of ten categories, falling, on 
average, from 17.7% to 12.9%.16
While Fig.  8 shows a clear pattern of U.S.–China decou-
pling, additional context is required to identify the subset of 
reductions in U.S.–China flow shares that appear to reflect 
geopolitically driven decoupling.17 We propose four criteria 
(illustrated in Fig.  9) to identify plausible episodes of geo-
politically driven decoupling, and we apply them (using the 
left side of Fig.  10) to selected flows between the U.S. and 
China. The first two criteria assess whether a decline has 
taken place, while the remaining two criteria assess whether 
a decline appears to be driven by geopolitically related pol-
icy developments or risks.

interactions involving the rival country (or bloc); (2) if such 
a decline is outside of the normal pattern of variation in the

Thus, there is a broad pattern of U.S.–China decoupling, 
and clear evidence of geopolitically driven decoupling in at 
least some areas. It is important, however, not to overstate 
the extent of this phenomenon, for three reasons. First, the 
U.S. and China continue to be connected by larger trade, 
capital, information, and people flows than almost every 
other pair of countries worldwide. 19 Second, the U.S. and 
China had an unusually high level of connectedness before 
the recent declining trend began and continue to have unu-
sually strong ties in some areas. Panel A of Fig.  10 shows 
that the share of U.S. imports coming from China was much 
higher than the share of the rest of the world’s imports com-
ing from China before recent declines and has fallen only 
to about the same level. More strikingly, panel C shows 
that – even after recent declines – the shares of publications 
that researchers in the U.S. developed with colleagues in 
China and vice versa were still twice as high as the shares of 
publications that scholars from the rest of the world devel-
oped with counterparts from either the U.S. or China. 20 
Third, recent declines in the share of U.S. imports coming 
from China overstate how much the U.S. has reduced its 
reliance on goods from China. This is clear from both the 
growth of indirect exports from China to the U.S. via other 
countries (Alfaro & Chor, 2023; Qiu et al., 2023) and from 
the large gap that has opened up between China’s reported 
exports to the U.S. and the U.S.’s reported imports from 
China (while U.S. data show Mexico as the largest source 
of U.S. merchandise imports in 2023, China still reported 
more exports to the U.S. than Mexico did). 21 Thus, even as 
U.S.–China ties stand diminished, these two countries are 
far from decoupled.22
Russia’s reorientation
Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 demonstrated 
what a true decoupling looks like – and that it can happen 
under the right conditions. Business ties formerly viewed 
as critical, such as energy exports from Russia to West-
ern Europe, have declined dramatically. While a detailed 
examination of Russia’s reorientation away from interactions 
with Western countries (and toward greater ties with China 
and other non-Western economies) is beyond the scope 
of this paper, developments in announced greenfield FDI 
and merchandise trade illustrate the magnitude of Russia’s 
reorientation.

sia collapsed from 157 in 2021 to just 21 in 2023, and the 
sources of this investment shifted dramatically. In 2021, 59%

Wider geoeconomic fragmentation?
Looking beyond the fraying of direct ties between the U.S. 
and China and the break between Russia and Western-
aligned countries, the preponderance of the evidence so far 
fails to demonstrate a meaningful pattern of global flows 
fracturing between rival blocs of countries (a deglobaliza-
tion scenario that many studies have warned could be very 
costly (Aiyar et al., 2023)). As a first indication, the charts 
on the right side of Fig.  10 show that close allies of the U.S. 
and China (as classified in Evans-Pritchard and Williams 
(2023)), excluding Russia, do not show similar patterns of 
decoupling as we saw between the U.S. and China them-
selves (left side of the same figure). In 2023, the share of 
U.S. close allies’ imports coming from China and its close 
allies (panel B) was roughly the same as its pre-pandemic 
(2019) level, after having risen during the pandemic. Mean-
while, the share of China’s allies’ imports coming from the 
U.S. and its allies ticked up in 2023, after a long-term declin-
ing trend. Data on scientific research collaboration (panel D) 
also show no clear geopolitically driven decoupling between 
blocs of allies, and there are no recent breaks from prior 
trends for outward announced greenfield FDI and inward 
announced M&A transactions (Panels F and H). (Refer to 
Fig. A1 in the Online Appendix for additional flows and 
a version including Russia in the trends for China’s close 
allies.)

Before moving on from geoeconomic fragmentation, we 
should note that our results do not challenge narrower find-
ings about geopolitically driven realignments of activity in 
specific sectors. Unsurprisingly, there have been larger shifts 
toward within-bloc trade in strategic sectors since Russia’s 
full-scale invasion of Ukraine (IMF, 2024). There are espe-
cially forceful policy efforts underway to shift the geography 
of semiconductor production and trade (Luo & Van Assche, 
2023). Nonetheless, it is important to keep in mind that elec-
tronic integrated circuits (including semiconductors) com-
prised less than 5% of global exports in 2022. 30 While it is 
natural for the dramatic realignments of activity in selected 
industries to garner substantial attention, the results we have 
shown here caution against presuming that such trends will 
be mirrored in overall patterns across sectors.
Thus, while there is a clear pattern of (partial) decoupling 
between the U.S. and China, and Russia’s international flows 
have shifted dramatically, the preponderance of the evidence 
so far does not indicate a clear pattern of decoupling between 
rival blocs of countries. This contrast highlights the much 
larger shifts in international flows provoked by an active 
military conflict (Russia–Ukraine) and from sharp increases 
in tariffs and other policy interventions (U.S.–China) relative 
to the still modest macro-level shifts that have resulted from 
more limited de-risking efforts in other parts of the world. It 
also spotlights how leaders in many countries have strongly 
opposed pressures to align decisively with a particular geo-
political bloc and raises the question of whether – and under 
what conditions – such non-aligned or multi-aligned policy 
approaches will continue to remain viable moving forward.
Variation across countries
Before turning to implications for policy and future research, 
we briefly shift the level of analysis from the world to indi-
vidual countries, to highlight the cross-country variation 
that should be considered in policymaking. The variation 
across countries in levels of globalization (e.g., the contrast 
between Antigua and Barbuda’s 1% merchandise exports to 
GDP ratio and Singapore’s 111%) is far larger than recent 
changes over time in the same ratio at the global level (which 
has remained within the 20–25% range since 2004). For 
additional evidence of cross-country variation, refer to the 
Country Profiles in Altman and Bastian (2024), which report 
up to 42 country-level globalization indicators for 181 coun-
tries and territories.
It is even more important in the present context to rec -
ognize that there is only a very weak relationship between 
changes in global and country-level globalization measures. 
To illustrate this point, Fig.  14 plots annual percent changes 
in countries’ exports-to-GDP ratios (on the vertical axis) 
versus annual percent changes in the world’s exports-to-
GDP ratio (on the horizontal axis) (over the 2001 to 2022 
period). Even in the year with the fastest global growth in 
export intensity (9.8% in 2021), 17% of countries reported 
declines, and in the year with the fastest global decline 
(-15% in 2009), 14% of countries reported increases.
Globalization levels and trends, thus, vary widely across 
countries. As we will discuss in the next section, this 
implies that policymakers should be very cautious about 
acting based only on global trends in globalization patterns.

Implications for policy and future research
We propose four implications of the material presented here 
for policy, followed by three implications for future research. 
First, and most importantly, policymakers and other public 
leaders should avoid mistaking the elevated risk of deglo-
balization for actual deglobalization, to avoid contributing 
on false premises to a costly race toward national self-suf-
ficiency. The policy implications of actual deglobaliza-
tion are different from those of the risk of deglobalization, 
mainly because each country’s international opportunities 
depend on how open other countries are to international 
flows. National policy choices about levels of openness 
must consider both the current benefits associated with a 
country’s international flows and expected future benefits 
and risks – which depend on likely policies adopted by key 
partner countries, including partner country responses to the 
focal country’s own policy choices (if partners reciprocate 
the focal country’s openness or retaliate in response to new 
restrictions).
Were deglobalization already underway, individual 
countries’ benefits from international exchange would have 
already diminished and the risk of further deglobaliza-
tion would loom especially large. If such trends could not 
be reversed, they would support a case for policymakers 
to focus on boosting self-sufficiency, potentially even via 
policies likely to provoke retaliation by partner countries. 
However, since our analysis indicates that deglobalization 
is still a risk rather than a current reality, appropriate policy 
responses are quite different. In most areas, countries still 
enjoy substantial benefits from international openness and 
have strong incentives to preserve those benefits by avoiding 
policy changes that could lead to costly retaliation. At the 
same time, in areas where the potential harm from deglo-
balization is too great (e.g., loss of access to critical materi-
als), the risk of deglobalization may still demand carefully 
targeted efforts to boost self-sufficiency (domestically or 
with allied countries). Since policy uncertainty is itself a 
barrier to international flows, the boundaries of what U.S. 
policymakers have called a “small yard, high fence” strat-
egy31 should be defined as clearly as possible. Ultimately, 
the key policy implication of the evidence we have presented 
is that it is still possible – and worthwhile – to avoid policy 
choices that would contribute to a self-fulfilling prophecy 
of deglobalization.
The same logic also applies in the realm of policy-related 
rhetoric. Leaders and scholars, especially those affiliated 
with international institutions such as the UN, IMF, and 
WTO, have played valuable roles warning policymakers 
about the potential costs of deglobalization. However, such 
warnings should be balanced with sufficient emphasis on 
the resilience of international flows to make clear that the 
appropriate policy response, in most areas, is to preserve 
and improve international ties – see next point – rather than 
to set countries on a race to minimize their vulnerability 
to deglobalization. Otherwise, “scare tactics” (Gourinchas, 
2023: 143) could contribute to precisely the sort of harmful 
policy developments that they are intended to prevent.
Second, policies intended to shift patterns of international 
flows must consider the substantial capacity of firms and 
countries to adapt to new constraints in ways that preserve 
or expand the benefits they derive from globalization. It 
is striking, for example, that the intensity of international 
trade was at a record high in 2022, despite an unprecedented 
proliferation of sanctions on trade and other international 
activity (see Fig.  15). This creates both opportunities and 
challenges for policymakers. It highlights how global value 
chains are more resilient than many have presumed (Thakur-
Weigold & Miroudot, 2023). But it also demonstrates the dif-
ficulty of imposing targeted restrictions on interactions with 
a geopolitical adversary, as illustrated by recent increases in 
indirect trade between the U.S. and China (Alfaro & Chor, 
2023; Gopinath et al., 2024). This suggests the importance 
of designing de-risking policies, where required, based on 
a holistic view of global value chains, and pursuing them 
cooperatively, wherever possible, rather than via unilateral 
policy changes. While such cooperation is most natural 
among geopolitically aligned countries, there can also be 
value in communicating and coordinating with adversar -
ies to reduce uncertainty and minimize cycles of tit-for-tat 
retaliation.
Third, the resilience of international activity should moti-
vate policymakers to redouble efforts at addressing the root 
causes of opposition to globalization. Since deglobalization 
is not underway, efforts to address public concerns about 
globalization are still highly relevant. While a detailed 
discussion of how to expand the benefits of globalization 
while better managing its challenges lies beyond the scope 
of this paper, substantial research and policy focus has been 
devoted to this topic in recent years. See, for example, mate-
rial on how to expand benefits of trade and better support 
adjustment to trade shocks (IMF et al., 2017), how to boost 
contributions of FDI to sustainable development (OECD, 
2022), and how trade policies could be adjusted to support 
efforts at improving gender equity (World Bank & WTO, 
2020). Many institutions have recently adopted the term

Fourth, it is essential for policymakers to look beyond 
global trends in levels of globalization to understand changes 
in the openness of their own countries’ key partners in 
international exchange. As highlighted in the previous sec-
tion, there is wide variation across countries in globaliza-
tion levels and trends. This is especially important because 
most countries do not have substantial interactions with all 
countries around the world. For an average country in 2022, 
more than half of the country’s merchandise exports went 
to just three destination countries, and more than half of its 
FDI inflows came from just two origin countries. 32 Thus, 
for example, while global changes in trade intensity hold 
substantial relevance for policymakers in China (the country 
with the most globally distributed exports), they are not very 
relevant for policymakers in most countries, where trends for 
key trade partners are much more important. This is espe-
cially true for small countries, but it is also salient for some 
large countries, such as Canada and Mexico (which send 
more than 70% of their exports to just one country, the U.S.).
Turning to future research, the first implication of the 
material presented here is the importance of carefully check-
ing perceptions about globalization with hard data. While 
misperceptions can arise in any subject area, globalization 
levels appear to be subject to especially large mispercep-
tions due to a variety of cognitive biases articulated in Ghe-
mawat (2018). Thus, related empirical work can contribute 
to future IB research in the many areas where deglobaliza-
tion is already being considered, including core theoreti-
cal approaches (Luo, 2024; Tung et al., 2023), global strat-
egy (Cui et al., 2023 ), MNE subsidiaries (Andrews et al., 
2023), global business ethics (Yao et al., 2023), and so on. 
A recent illustration of the need for such empirical research 
is provided by Evenett and Pisani (2023), which highlighted 
the relatively small proportion of Western multinationals 
that fully divested from Russia during the early stages of 
the war in Ukraine. Such findings, along with the mate-
rial we have presented in this paper, also suggest the need 
for future research examining why and how IB activity has 
remained so resilient even as the public policy environment 
has become more adverse. Research examining firm-level 
responses to the present turbulence could help inform related 
policymaking.
Second, research on the effects of geopolitical tensions 
on globalization would benefit from advances in measur -
ing countries’ levels of geopolitical alignment. The most 
widely used measures are based on countries’ voting patterns 
in the UN General Assembly. However, these data provide 
only a narrow view of countries’ complex and multifaceted 
relationships (Baturo et al., 2017). Note, for example, that 
based on the Bailey et al. (2017) dataset we used to gener -
ate Fig.  12, Vietnam – a country widely viewed as a key 
beneficiary of “friend-shoring” by U.S. firms – is actually 
more geopolitically distant from the U.S. than China is. This 
highlights the value of broader approaches, such as the work 
of Evans-Pritchard and Williams (2023), which incorpo-
rates additional factors such as military alliances, territorial 
disputes, and public opinion data. It would be valuable for 
future research to employ new measures to clarify the dis-
tinction between geopolitically driven shifts in patterns of 
international activity and shifts due to other factors, building 
on distinctions we made here between geopolitically driven 
decoupling and decoupling related to countries’ changing 
overall levels of participation in international flows (along 
with effects of exogenous shocks such as the COVID-19 
pandemic).
Third, the analysis of trade regionalization presented here 
suggested that widely reported increases in the intra-regional 
share of trade between 2012 and 2016 using one region clas-
sification – which turned out not to be robust to the use of 
alternative region classifications – contributed to mispercep-
tions of a rising trend in trade regionalization. This prompted 
us to use the average distance over which countries interact 
as an alternative measure of regionalization, which we rec-
ommend for consideration in future research. The same les-
son is likely to apply to the fast-growing body of research 
focused on geopolitical distance and the possibility of a frac-
turing between rival blocs of countries. The fact that differ-
ent studies have employed vastly different bloc definitions, 
with one study alone (Gopinath et al., 2024) considering 
both a China-centric bloc comprised of only seven countries 
(Belarus, China, Eritrea, Mali, Nicaragua, Russia, and Syria) 
and another China-centric bloc with one-quarter of all coun-
tries, highlights the lack of agreement on relevant definitions 
of geopolitical blocs. We see especially great promise in the 
development of new (composite) continuous measures of 
geopolitical distance incorporating many of the underlying 
variables employed in Evans-Pritchard and Williams (2023) 
and calibrating their relevance in different IB contexts.
Conclusion
International business activity has proven remarkably resil-
ient in the face of rising geopolitical tensions and an increas-
ingly adverse public policy environment, challenging the 
notion that the world has entered a period of deglobaliza-
tion. There has not been a clear shift from international to 
domestic activity, nor is there a clear pattern of international

direct to indirect trade between the U.S. and China – should 
not be viewed as evidence of deglobalization. To the con-
trary, such reconfiguration illustrates the resilience of inter-
national activity and how firms and countries tend to adapt 
to new constraints to preserve benefits they derive from 
globalization.
Deglobalization, thus, is still a risk and not a current 
reality. Moving forward, it will be important to continue 
monitoring – and improving – globalization measures to 
see if this conclusion continues to hold. The salience of 
such future research is underscored by evidence showing 
an unusually high level of flux in the geographic patterns 
of trade and announced greenfield FDI, along with a sus-
tained increase in business statements about deglobaliza -
tion, reshoring, and related terms (Gopinath et al., 2024). 
Recent policy developments such as further escalation of 
tariffs on China’s exports by various countries and proposed 
additional tariff increases also underscore the importance of 
continuing to monitor whether the factors contributing to an 
elevated risk of deglobalization (strands 1–4 of Table 1) will 
ultimately lead to actual deglobalization (strand 5).
Finally, it is essential to keep in mind that globalization 
levels vary far more across countries than over time, at least 
over reasonable business planning horizons. This implies 
that even as debates about global trends in levels of interna-
tional integration continue, national policy choices should 
be driven by much more granular perspectives. Sweeping 
global narratives about deglobalization, even if they were 
supported by the data, would still form a poor basis for most 
countries’ national policymaking.
