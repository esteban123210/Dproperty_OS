# Altman et al. (2024) - Challenging the Deglobalization Narrative

**Authors:** Steven A. Altman, Caroline R. Bastian, Davis Fattedad  
**Journal:** *Journal of International Business Policy*, 7, 416-439  
**Published online:** 7 October 2024  
**DOI:** 10.1057/s42214-024-00197-0  
**Role:** Supplementary Session 1 reading  
**License stated in article:** Creative Commons Attribution 4.0

## Abstract-level claim

The authors challenge the popular narrative that the world has already entered a period of **deglobalization**. Their conclusion is more precise:

> **Deglobalization is still a risk rather than a current general reality.**

Using the DHL Global Connectedness Index and related flow data, they argue that:

- international flows have not generally decreased relative to domestic activity;
- there is no clear ongoing shift from global to regional business;
- geopolitically driven changes remain concentrated around countries at the center of current conflicts;
- globalization levels and trends vary substantially across countries;
- mistaken beliefs about deglobalization can themselves encourage costly policy and strategy errors.

# 1. Why the paper was written

The authors argue that a new myth has emerged. In the 1990s and 2000s, many people made the mistake of assuming that borders and distance were becoming irrelevant. Today, decision-makers increasingly make the **opposite** mistake: they assume a new era of deglobalization has already arrived.

The paper therefore asks what has actually changed in the size and geography of international flows after successive shocks including:

- Brexit;
- the election of Donald Trump and subsequent U.S. trade conflicts;
- the COVID-19 pandemic;
- Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine;
- worsening U.S.-China geopolitical tensions.

# 2. Four empirical questions

The paper is organized around four questions:

1. **Are international flows decreasing relative to domestic activity?**
2. **Are more international flows taking place within rather than between geographic regions?**
3. **Are international flows fragmenting along geopolitical lines?**
4. **How do globalization levels and trends vary across countries?**

The evidence on the first three challenges the claim that broad deglobalization is already underway. The fourth demonstrates why global averages are often insufficient for policy or strategy.

# 3. What counts as deglobalization?

The authors define deglobalization as a **decline in the integration or interdependence of countries around the world**.

This means the concept should be tested with actual interactions among countries—not merely attitudes, policy announcements, corporate statements, or forecasts.

# 4. Five strands of the deglobalization narrative

One of the article's most useful analytical distinctions is its separation of five strands that are often conflated.

## 1. Preferences

Evidence may show:
- declining public support for globalization;
- political backlash;
- populist or anti-globalization movements;
- specialists revising their views of globalization.

## 2. Public policy

Evidence may show:
- trade restrictions;
- industrial subsidies;
- investment screening;
- data-flow restrictions;
- weakening of international institutions;
- new sanctions and strategic trade measures.

## 3. Plans

Companies increasingly discuss:
- reshoring;
- nearshoring;
- friendshoring;
- supply-chain redesign;
- footprint changes.

## 4. Predictions

Analysts and scholars forecast:
- more fragmentation;
- further policy restrictions;
- reductions or geographic redirection in cross-border activity.

## 5. Actual patterns of international activity

This is the article's focus:
- are trade, capital, information, and people flows actually becoming less international?
- are they becoming systematically more regional?
- are they splitting into geopolitical blocs?

The authors' position is:

- strands **1-4** contain many legitimate developments and indicate an **elevated risk** of deglobalization;
- strand **5** does **not yet** show a clear generalized deglobalization pattern.

This distinction is one of the most exam-useful points in the article.

# 5. Why false narratives matter

The authors warn that deglobalization rhetoric can become a **self-fulfilling prophecy**.

If governments and firms behave as if globalization has already collapsed, they may create the fragmentation they fear.

Potential costs cited include:
- an IMF estimate that bifurcating trade into two rival blocs could reduce world GDP by as much as roughly **7%**;
- potentially larger losses from technological fragmentation;
- weaker capacity to address climate change;
- inflationary effects;
- reduced economic resilience.

# 6. Literature context

The article reviews scholarship arguing both for and against current deglobalization.

## Literature emphasizing deglobalization / fragmentation

Themes include:
- Brexit and anti-globalization backlash;
- weaker trade/FDI growth after the global financial crisis;
- protectionism;
- geopolitical rivalry;
- reshoring and supply-chain restructuring;
- weakening multilateral institutions.

The authors note that major academic conferences and policy discussions increasingly treat deglobalization as an established fact.

## Literature pushing back

Other scholars argue that:
- weaker post-2008 flows partly reflect cyclical or compositional factors;
- globalization is changing rather than ending;
- global activity remains resilient;
- reconfiguration should not be confused with reversal;
- geopolitical tensions have not yet produced systemic retreat across all flows.

## Connection to longstanding IB work

The article explicitly places itself in the tradition of correcting misperceptions about globalization.

- **Rugman:** much multinational activity is regionally concentrated.
- **Ghemawat:** distance still matters; the world is **semi-globalized**; people overestimate cross-border integration.
- **DHL Global Connectedness Index:** measures both the size and geographic reach of international flows.

# 7. Measuring globalization: depth and breadth

The DHL Global Connectedness Index is especially important because it distinguishes two dimensions.

## Depth / intensity

How large are cross-border flows **relative to domestic activity**?

A higher depth measure means international flows account for a larger share of the relevant activity.

## Breadth / extensity

How widely are international flows distributed across countries/geographic space?

A system can therefore be:
- highly international but concentrated among a few partners;
- less international in aggregate but geographically diversified;
- deep but regionally concentrated;
- broad but shallow.

The paper's analysis focuses mainly on **intensity** and **extensity** rather than the speed or impact of flows.

# 8. Intensity of international flows

The central empirical conclusion is that there is **no general shift from international toward domestic activity**.

## DHL Global Connectedness depth index

The overall global depth index combines:
- trade - **35% weight**;
- capital - **35%**;
- information - **15%**;
- people - **15%**.

The overall index reached a **new record high in 2022**, with projections suggesting a similar level in 2023.

## Trade pillar

The trade pillar includes trade in value-added terms relative to world GDP.

Findings:
- trade intensity rose to record levels in 2022 in the DHL measure;
- the familiar gross exports-to-GDP ratio recovered strongly in 2021-22;
- in 2022 it was roughly back near its 2008 peak;
- IMF projections suggested only small subsequent changes.

### Why gross exports/GDP can mislead

The paper notes that stagnation or declines in gross exports/GDP can reflect:
- changing composition of the global economy;
- growth of less trade-intensive countries;
- greater consumption of services, which are traded less intensively than goods;
- commodity-price changes;
- region-specific trends.

Services trade intensity has continued increasing even when some goods indicators weakened.

Conclusion: declining exports/GDP after 2008 should not automatically be interpreted as deglobalization.

# 9. Reshoring evidence

The authors find only limited evidence of broad U.S. reshoring.

Examples discussed:
- more imports of upstream manufactured goods could be consistent with reshoring of later production stages;
- the magnitude remains modest;
- manufacturing employment did not show a general reshoring boom;
- much U.S. manufacturing-construction growth was concentrated in electronics and influenced by subsidies;
- the Kearney Reshoring Index improved recently but remained below earlier levels.

The message is not that reshoring never happens. It is that **isolated or sector-specific reshoring does not establish a general deglobalization trend**.

# 10. Capital flows and FDI

FDI flows relative to gross fixed capital formation are below historic peaks and recently showed declines.

The authors argue that this indicator alone is insufficient because FDI flows:
- are highly volatile;
- often peak during M&A waves;
- can be distorted by corporate inversions and conduit flows;
- exclude contractual internationalization modes;
- miss foreign activity financed with host-country capital;
- can be heavily influenced by unusual transactions in financial centers.

The article notes that when Luxembourg's large 2022 transaction is excluded, FDI intensity actually increased rather than fell.

## Why FDI stocks matter

The authors defend FDI stocks as a meaningful indicator of continuing interdependence because foreign ownership creates long-lived production and organizational linkages.

They connect this to internalization theory: FDI involves transferring **firm-specific advantages (FSAs)** across borders in imperfect markets.

The collapse of automotive production after foreign MNEs halted operations in Russia is used as an example of how foreign-owned production creates real interdependence beyond the original investment flow.

# 11. Broader indicators of multinational activity

Figure 5 expands the analysis beyond FDI flows. Indicators include:

- inward FDI stock as % of GDP;
- inward FDI stock relative to stock-market capitalization;
- announced greenfield FDI value;
- announced greenfield FDI project counts;
- cross-border M&A value;
- cross-border M&A counts;
- international share of M&A transactions;
- MNE foreign-affiliate output as a share of world output;
- MNE foreign-affiliate employment relative to the world working-age population;
- international payments for use of intellectual property;
- foreign revenue shares of listed companies.

Taken together, the indicators do not support a broad retreat of multinational enterprise activity.

# 12. Information flows

The information pillar remained around a **record high** in 2022 after large increases over the prior two decades.

Global indicators include:
- international scientific-research collaboration;
- international patenting;
- payments for intellectual property;
- related cross-border knowledge flows.

At country level, the index also considers measures such as international internet bandwidth and foreign news traffic.

# 13. People flows

The people pillar remained below its 2019 peak in 2022.

The authors attribute this primarily to international travel's slow recovery from COVID-19 rather than to a structural retreat from internationalization.

The pillar includes:
- migrant stocks;
- international travel;
- international tertiary education.

Tourism was expected to return to or exceed pre-pandemic levels.

# 14. Geographic regionalization

A major narrative says firms are moving from global networks to shorter, more regional ones via:
- nearshoring;
- regional sourcing;
- local production;
- supply-chain shortening.

The article tests whether actual flows support that claim.

## Corporate responses to disruption

Many firms increased resilience through:
- digitization;
- supplier diversification;
- dual sourcing;
- redundancy;
- inventory changes;

rather than simply moving production closer to home.

Temporary pandemic-era shipping costs also made nearby supply more attractive for a period, but these costs later declined.

## Intra-regional trade

Some earlier work suggested an increase in intra-regional trade beginning around 2012.

Altman et al. show:
- the result depends on how regions are defined;
- the apparent increase did not continue after about 2016;
- after COVID, intra-regional trade shares actually declined under several alternative region classifications.

## Average distance traveled by flows

The paper also evaluates average geographic distance traversed by international flows.

This avoids arbitrary decisions about where regional boundaries should be drawn.

If international activity were systematically regionalizing, average distance should decline. The data do not show a broad, persistent fall consistent with generalized regionalization.

### Geographic conclusion

There is **no clear current macro-level shift from global to regional flows**.

Specific industries, firms, or country relationships may regionalize, but that cannot be generalized to all International Business.

# 15. Geopolitical fragmentation

The article separately analyzes **geoeconomic fragmentation**.

This refers to policy-induced changes in sources and destinations of cross-border flows driven by strategic considerations such as:
- national security;
- economic security;
- sovereignty;
- autonomy;
- geopolitical alignment.

The central concern is whether the world is splitting into rival U.S.-aligned and China-aligned blocs.

# 16. U.S.-China decoupling

The paper finds **real weakening in U.S.-China ties**.

Across 11 types of trade, capital, information, and people flows:
- China's share of U.S. flows declined in **9 of 11** categories;
- the U.S. share of China's flows declined in **8 of 10** categories for which the comparison is reported;
- average U.S. flow share involving China fell from roughly **9.4% to 6.7%**;
- average Chinese flow share involving the U.S. fell from roughly **17.7% to 12.9%**.

So bilateral decoupling is not merely rhetoric.

# 17. Four criteria for geopolitically driven decoupling

The authors caution that not every bilateral decline is caused by geopolitics.

They propose four criteria:

1. **A recent decline exists** in the relevant bilateral flow share.
2. **The decline is unusual** relative to the previous trend.
3. **The timing aligns** with policy changes or a substantial geopolitical-risk increase capable of affecting decisions.
4. **The decline is not simply global**—the partner must not be declining similarly in its interactions with all countries.

Merchandise imports provide one of the clearest cases in which all four criteria support geopolitically driven U.S.-China decoupling.

# 18. Russia

Russia's international relationships were much more dramatically reorganized after the 2022 invasion of Ukraine.

Sanctions and geopolitical realignment redirected:
- trade;
- investment;
- finance;
- technology;
- other international flows.

Russia therefore provides a strong case of severe geopolitical reconfiguration.

# 19. Is the world splitting into rival blocs?

Despite U.S.-China decoupling and Russia's reorientation, the paper does **not** find a generalized worldwide pattern of rival geopolitical blocs systematically severing ties with one another.

The strongest geopolitical fragmentation remains concentrated around the countries directly involved in major present conflicts.

Key distinction:

> **Bilateral decoupling and selective geopolitical fragmentation are real; generalized global deglobalization is not yet established.**

# 20. Country-level variation

Globalization levels differ much more **across countries** than they typically change **over short business-planning horizons**.

Relevant drivers include:
- country size;
- geography;
- logistics/infrastructure;
- openness;
- regional integration;
- institutions;
- sector structure;
- investment regimes;
- geopolitical alignment.

The article notes that many countries' international flows are highly concentrated in just a few partners.

For the average country in 2022:
- more than half of merchandise exports went to just **three** destination countries;
- more than half of FDI inflows came from just **two** origin countries.

Canada and Mexico are examples where more than 70% of exports go to a single partner, the United States.

Therefore global averages can be a poor guide for national policy or firm strategy.

# 21. Reconfiguration is not deglobalization

A central conceptual distinction is between **reconfiguration** and **reversal**.

Example:
- direct U.S.-China trade can decline;
- trade may be rerouted through third countries;
- supply chains may change configuration;
- the activity may still remain international rather than returning home.

The authors treat such adaptation as evidence of the resilience of international business under constraints.

Changing routes does not necessarily mean the underlying activity becomes domestic.

# 22. Policy implications

The paper cautions governments against assuming deglobalization is inevitable or already complete.

Implications include:
- preserve international openness where possible;
- distinguish targeted security risks from broad retreat;
- avoid unnecessarily costly fragmentation;
- consider diversification and resilience rather than simple autarky;
- monitor actual flow data rather than policy rhetoric alone;
- use granular country/partner analysis.

The article notes rising interest in **de-risking** rather than blanket decoupling and discusses the broader idea of **reglobalization** as a policy agenda combining openness with resilience and security.

# 23. Research implications

Future IB research should:
- test globalization perceptions with hard data;
- examine why international activity remains resilient despite a more hostile policy environment;
- improve measures of geopolitical alignment beyond UN voting alone;
- distinguish firm-level responses from aggregate outcomes;
- continue monitoring whether current deglobalization risks eventually translate into actual flow declines.

# 24. Main conclusion

The article concludes:

- pressures against globalization are real;
- anti-globalization rhetoric and policy barriers have increased;
- corporate plans increasingly reference reshoring, friendshoring, and de-risking;
- U.S.-China decoupling is real in important areas;
- Russia's external relationships have been substantially redirected;
- nevertheless, aggregate international flows remain resilient;
- no generalized shift from international to domestic activity is established;
- no generalized shift from global to regional activity is established;
- no comprehensive worldwide geopolitical-bloc split is established;
- **deglobalization remains a risk rather than a current macro-level reality**.

# 26. Managerial implications

1. **Do not confuse rhetoric with realized flows.** Political statements and corporate plans can shift much faster than underlying networks.
2. **Avoid binary globalize/deglobalize thinking.** Networks can reconfigure while remaining international.
3. **Analyze the relevant flow.** Trade, FDI, talent, information, IP, and travel can evolve differently.
4. **Analyze the relevant country pair.** U.S.-China dynamics are not automatically representative of every relationship.
5. **Build resilience without assuming retreat is optimal.** Diversification, dual sourcing, digitization, and network redesign can preserve international benefits.
6. **Expect selective fragmentation.** Some sectors and bilateral ties can decouple sharply while aggregate globalization remains resilient.
7. **Use granular country data.** Global averages are often a poor input for a specific investment decision.

# 27. Connection to Ghemawat

**Ghemawat (2012):** the world is **less globalized than people think**.  
**Altman et al. (2024):** the world is **not deglobalizing as broadly as people increasingly claim**.

Combined lesson:

> International Business requires empirical discipline. Reject both hyperglobalization and simplistic deglobalization narratives.

# 28. Connection to Session 1 theory

The article reinforces several Session 1 concepts:

- **semi-globalization:** borders still matter even when international activity is resilient;
- **FDI:** flows, stocks, and foreign affiliate activity must be interpreted carefully;
- **FSAs:** FDI creates persistent interdependence because firms transfer capabilities across borders;
- **distance/geopolitics:** flow patterns remain structured by geography and political relationships;
- **strategic context:** firms should not treat a global narrative as a substitute for country-specific analysis.

# 29. Exam retrieval cue

**Use Altman et al. when an exam question assumes globalization is reversing, reshoring is universal, or geopolitical fragmentation means all MNEs should retreat home.**

Response structure:
1. Separate **preferences/policy/plans/predictions** from **actual flow patterns**.
2. Test **depth**: are flows becoming less international relative to domestic activity?
3. Test **breadth/geography**: are flows actually becoming more regional?
4. Test **geopolitical fragmentation**: is decoupling bilateral/selective or system-wide?
5. Move to the focal country/industry before recommending strategy.
