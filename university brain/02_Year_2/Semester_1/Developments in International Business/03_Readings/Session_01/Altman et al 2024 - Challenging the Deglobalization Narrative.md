# Altman et al. 2024 - Challenging the Deglobalization Narrative

**Authors:** Steven A. Altman, Caroline R. Bastian, Davis Fattedad  
**Journal:** Journal of International Business Policy, 7(4), 416-439  
**Status:** Supplementary Session 1 reading

## Core thesis
The popular claim that the world has already entered a period of broad **deglobalization** is not supported by the aggregate evidence. Deglobalization is a meaningful **risk**, but not yet a general empirical reality.

The paper distinguishes rhetoric, policy pressure, corporate plans, predictions, and **actual patterns of international activity**. Its main concern is the last category: what international flows are actually doing.

## Five strands of the deglobalization narrative
1. **Preferences** - public or expert sentiment shifts against globalization.
2. **Public policy** - more barriers, industrial policy, screening, restrictions, weaker multilateral institutions.
3. **Plans** - firms talk about reshoring, nearshoring, friendshoring, or changing footprints.
4. **Predictions** - analysts expect fragmentation or declining international flows.
5. **Actual patterns of international activity** - trade, FDI, people, information and other flows actually become less international.

The authors accept that the first four contain real signals of pressure but argue that they should not automatically be treated as proof of the fifth.

## Main empirical findings

### 1. No general shift from international to domestic activity
Using the DHL Global Connectedness Index and related measures, the authors find that aggregate international activity remained at or near record levels after the pandemic recovery.

The index examines four broad categories:
- trade;
- capital;
- information;
- people.

Trade and capital reached record-high levels in 2022; information remained at a record high; the people pillar lagged mainly because international travel had not yet fully recovered from COVID-19.

### 2. No clear general shift toward regionalization
Despite widespread discussion of nearshoring, actual flow data do not show a robust worldwide move from global to regional trade. Results depend heavily on how regions are defined, and recent data do not show a consistent trend toward shorter-distance international flows.

### 3. Geopolitical fragmentation is real but concentrated
The paper does identify important cases of geopolitical decoupling:
- partial U.S.-China decoupling in some flows;
- a dramatic reorientation of Russia's flows after the invasion of Ukraine.

However, these shifts do **not** yet amount to a broad global split into rival blocs.

### 4. Country-level variation matters enormously
Global averages can hide very different national experiences. Countries vary greatly in both their level and trajectory of globalization.

The strategic implication is that managers should avoid using a global headline such as 'deglobalization' as a substitute for country, sector, and flow-specific analysis.

## Depth and breadth
A useful conceptual contribution is the distinction between:

- **Depth:** how large international flows are relative to domestic activity.
- **Breadth / extensity:** how geographically dispersed those international flows are.

A country or firm can be highly internationally connected but concentrated in only a few partners, or less intensely connected but broadly diversified.

## Relationship to Ghemawat
This paper updates the same intellectual tradition as Ghemawat's semi-globalization argument.

Ghemawat challenges **hyperglobalization**: people overestimate cross-border integration.

Altman et al. challenge a newer overcorrection: people may now be exaggerating **deglobalization**.

Together:

> Cross-border integration is substantial and resilient, but still incomplete and highly structured by distance, institutions, geopolitics, and country-specific conditions.

## Managerial implications
1. Do not redesign an international footprint merely because 'deglobalization' is fashionable language.
2. Distinguish announced intentions from realized flows.
3. Analyze the relevant type of flow separately: trade, capital, information, people, technology, etc.
4. Analyze geography and geopolitics at the country-pair and industry level.
5. Consider diversification and resilience without assuming that nearshoring is automatically optimal.
6. Avoid global averages when making a country-specific investment decision.

## Useful exam contrast
### Hyperglobalization error
**Claim:** borders barely matter anymore.  
**Counter:** Ghemawat - actual cross-border shares are much lower than people think.

### Deglobalization error
**Claim:** globalization is broadly reversing.  
**Counter:** Altman et al. - aggregate international flows remain resilient and often near historical highs.

### Better view
**Semi-globalized / selectively fragmented world:** large international flows coexist with persistent distance, country differences, and targeted geopolitical decoupling.

## Exam-ready version
**Core claim:** Deglobalization is currently more a risk and narrative than a general empirical reality.  
**Framework:** Separate preferences/policy/plans/predictions from actual flow patterns; evaluate depth and breadth of flows.  
**Use when:** A case assumes reshoring, friendshoring, or geopolitical fragmentation is universal.  
**Managerial implication:** diagnose the specific flow, country, industry, and relationship before changing geographic strategy.  
**Best comparison:** Ghemawat (2012) on overestimating globalization.
