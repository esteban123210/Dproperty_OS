# Zaheer (1995) - Overcoming the Liability of Foreignness

**Author:** Srilata Zaheer  
**Journal:** *Academy of Management Journal*, 38(2), 341-363  
**Role:** Supplementary Session 1 reading / foundational framework  
**Core concepts:** liability of foreignness, firm-specific advantages, local isomorphism, administrative heritage, integration vs responsiveness

> This is a comprehensive structured extraction for search, study, and exam retrieval. It preserves the paper's theory, hypotheses, empirical design, measures, main results, implications, and caveats without reproducing the copyrighted article verbatim.

# 1. Research problem

International Business theory assumes that multinational enterprises operating abroad face extra costs simply because they are foreign. Zaheer's paper asks two linked questions:

1. **Does a measurable liability of foreignness actually exist even in a highly global industry?**
2. **If it does, what helps a foreign subsidiary overcome it: importing the parent firm's distinctive organizational practices or becoming more like successful local firms?**

The paper therefore brings together:
- classic theory of the multinational enterprise;
- the resource-based view / firm-specific advantage logic;
- institutional theory and local isomorphism;
- international strategy's integration-responsiveness problem.

# 2. Liability of Foreignness (LOF)

## Definition

LOF is the set of **additional costs a firm operating in a foreign market incurs that a comparable local firm would not incur**.

Zaheer identifies four broad, potentially overlapping sources.

## 2.1 Spatial-distance costs

Costs directly associated with physical/geographic separation, such as:
- travel;
- transport;
- communication;
- coordination over distance;
- coordination across time zones.

## 2.2 Firm-specific unfamiliarity costs

Costs arising because the foreign firm:
- lacks roots in the local environment;
- has less experience with local institutions;
- lacks local relationships;
- is less familiar with customers and market practices;
- needs to learn how the host market works.

## 2.3 Host-country costs

Costs created by the environment in which the foreign subsidiary operates, such as:
- weak legitimacy;
- discrimination against foreign firms;
- economic nationalism;
- stakeholder preference for domestic firms;
- host-country institutional barriers.

## 2.4 Home-country costs

Constraints originating in the MNE's home country that affect overseas activity.

Example discussed in the article:
- restrictions on sales of high-technology products to certain countries imposed on U.S.-owned MNEs.

## Context dependence

The importance of each LOF source varies across:
- industry;
- firm;
- host country;
- home country.

Whatever the source, the theory implies that, all else equal, foreign firms should have:
- lower profitability than local firms;
- potentially lower probability of survival.

# 3. Horizontal versus vertical/networked MNEs

Zaheer suggests LOF may be especially acute in **simple, market-seeking horizontal MNEs**.

A horizontal MNE's subsidiaries largely replicate one another across countries and compete on a local-for-local basis.

By contrast:
- a **vertical MNE** places different stages of an integrated value chain in different countries;
- a **networked MNE** assigns differentiated roles to subsidiaries.

Vertical/networked structures may partly offset LOF through:
- global scale;
- scope economies;
- specialized subsidiary roles;
- integrated value creation.

# 4. Why foreign-exchange trading is a strong empirical test

The study examines foreign-exchange trading rooms of major Western and Japanese banks in **New York and Tokyo**.

This is an especially demanding setting in which to demonstrate LOF because:
- FX is highly global;
- currencies are undifferentiated products;
- major banks use broadly similar technology;
- trading systems have global reach;
- many traditional product-level advantages are weak.

If foreignness still creates disadvantages here, the finding strongly supports the idea that cross-border operation carries persistent costs.

# 5. Why local firms may still have an advantage in FX

Although speculative currency trading crosses borders, customer business remains substantially local.

Local trading rooms can be better embedded in:
- customer networks;
- local information flows;
- central-bank relationships;
- policy networks;
- local expectations about interest rates and currency movements.

Because information is critical in FX trading, this local embeddedness can produce a material performance advantage.

# 6. Hypothesis 1 - existence of LOF

**H1:** Foreign trading rooms will be less profitable than local trading rooms in the same location, all else equal.

This is the direct empirical test of liability of foreignness.

# 7. How can the MNE overcome LOF?

The paper contrasts two strategic responses.

## 7.1 Import firm-specific advantage from home

Classic MNE and resource-based theory suggests the parent transfers resources/capabilities that locals cannot easily replicate.

Potential FSAs include:
- scale or scope economies;
- brand;
- differentiated products;
- technology;
- cost advantages;
- organizational routines;
- managerial skills;
- learning capability;
- knowledge-transfer capability.

In the FX setting, traditional product/technology advantages are limited, so **organizational and managerial capabilities** are especially important.

## 7.2 Adapt to / imitate local firms

Institutional theory predicts that foreign subsidiaries face pressure to resemble the host environment.

Three forms of isomorphism are relevant:

### Coercive isomorphism
Adoption of local practices because of regulation or formal host-country requirements.

### Normative isomorphism
Convergence because professions, educational systems, or accepted standards impose common practices.

### Mimetic isomorphism
Imitation of organizations seen as successful local models.

The key puzzle is whether a foreign subsidiary performs better by:
- preserving/importing its parent firm's distinctive practices; or
- becoming more like successful host-country competitors.

# 8. Hypotheses 2a and 2b

## Hypothesis 2a - imported FSA

Foreign trading rooms whose organizational practices more closely resemble their own firm's **home-country trading room** should show less LOF.

## Hypothesis 2b - local isomorphism

Foreign trading rooms whose organizational practices more closely resemble **local host-country trading rooms** should show less LOF.

These predictions pull the subsidiary in different directions.

# 9. Organizational practices selected for the study

Zaheer needed practices that:
- differed substantially between local Western rooms in New York and local Japanese rooms in Tokyo;
- plausibly affected performance.

Formal structure and physical layout were mostly too similar to provide a useful comparison.

Two control-system dimensions showed strong differences.

# 10. Market controls

Western trading rooms in New York relied more heavily on market-like controls, including:
- performance-based compensation;
- recruiting experienced traders from the external labor market;
- higher labor turnover.

## Performance logic

FX room profits depend heavily on traders' individual ability and effort.

Performance-linked incentives can:
- attract higher-quality traders;
- motivate effort;
- connect individual outcomes to organizational performance.

Zaheer therefore expects stronger market control to be associated with better performance in this setting.

# 11. Bureaucratic microcontrols

Japanese trading rooms in Tokyo used tighter detailed restrictions on:
- intraday open positions;
- overnight open positions;
- limits by currency;
- limits by trader.

These are called **bureaucratic microcontrols**.

## Why they may reduce performance

Successful speculative trading requires:
- rapid interpretation of market information;
- immediate decisions;
- discretion by the trader closest to the market.

Strict central rules can prevent traders from exploiting time-sensitive opportunities.

The paper therefore expects bureaucratic microcontrol to be negatively associated with performance in this specific context.

# 12. Research design

## Exploratory phase

The study began with observation and interviews in eight U.S. and Japanese bank trading rooms in New York and Tokyo to identify meaningful differences in organizational practice.

## Full sample

The full sample contained **28 trading rooms**:
- 13 in New York;
- 15 in Tokyo;
- belonging to eight Western and eight Japanese banks.

Survey responses were obtained from **198 traders**, a 79% response rate overall.

## Matched sample used for core tests

The main tests of local isomorphism versus imported FSA use a matched sample of **24 trading rooms**:
- 12 New York;
- 12 Tokyo;
- six Western parent banks;
- six Japanese parent banks.

Each bank contributes a home and foreign room, making direct parent-to-subsidiary comparison possible.

The matched-sample survey includes **174 traders**.

Subgroup counts reported:
- Japanese rooms in Tokyo: 51 trader respondents;
- Japanese rooms in New York: 31;
- Western rooms in Tokyo: 53;
- Western rooms in New York: 39.

# 13. Data collection

Zaheer combines:
- observation;
- interviews;
- trader questionnaires;
- separate questionnaires/interviews with trading-room heads.

Trader responses measure organizational practices.

Heads of rooms provide performance measures.

Using different respondent groups helps reduce common-method bias.

# 14. Measuring liability of foreignness

For each foreign room, LOF is measured as the difference between:

**average profits per trader of local rooms in the same city - profits per trader of the focal foreign room.**

Example logic:
- compare a Japanese-owned trading room in New York with the average performance of Western/local rooms in New York.

Higher positive values = greater LOF.

The analysis also uses a perceptual performance measure built from trader assessments of:
- quality of traders;
- profitability relative to other rooms;
- total room profit;
- profit per trader.

# 15. Measuring local isomorphism

For each organizational practice, the paper measures how far the foreign room is from the **average local practice in the host city**.

Practices measured:
- market control;
- bureaucratic microcontrol.

Both **signed distance** and **absolute distance** are calculated.

Why signed distance matters:
- not only the degree of difference, but the **direction** of deviation can influence performance.

Smaller absolute distance from local rooms = greater local isomorphism.

# 16. Measuring imported firm-specific advantage

The paper compares each foreign room with its paired **home-country room in the same parent bank**.

Again, the analysis calculates:
- signed distance from home practice;
- absolute distance from home practice.

Smaller distance from the home room = stronger replication/transfer of the parent firm's organizational practice.

# 17. Control variables

## Trading-room age

Used to distinguish LOF from a simple **liability of newness**.

## Trading-room size

Measured by number of traders.

## Actual risk-taking

FX profitability is strongly related to risk.

Risk is captured through:
- open-position behavior;
- size of positions;
- loss-taking behavior.

This allows the analysis to avoid attributing profit differences simply to foreign rooms taking less risk.

# 18. Descriptive patterns

Reported average profits per trader ranged from approximately:
- **$0.95 million** in Japanese trading rooms in New York;
- up to **$1.88 million** in Japanese trading rooms in Tokyo;
- with Western rooms in New York/Tokyo in between.

The subsamples differed significantly on:
- market control;
- age;
- size.

Bureaucratic control differences were marginally significant.

Western rooms in New York were generally the oldest.

Japanese rooms in Tokyo were considerably larger on average.

# 19. Result: LOF exists

After controlling for age and size, local rooms were significantly more profitable than foreign rooms.

The analysis reports a significant difference supporting **Hypothesis 1**.

Neither age nor size adequately explains the foreign-local profitability gap.

This is important because the study finds LOF even in:
- a highly global industry;
- an undifferentiated product market;
- technologically standardized operations.

Zaheer interprets this as strong support for Hymer's classic claim that there are persistent costs to doing business abroad.

# 20. Local isomorphism versus imported FSA - main result

The relationship is complex rather than one universal rule.

Overall, the findings give stronger support to the importance of **imported firm-specific organizational capabilities / administrative heritage** than to the claim that subsidiaries should simply imitate local firms.

For the practices studied:
- greater similarity to the parent/home organizational system can reduce LOF;
- local imitation does not consistently improve performance;
- in some cases, greater distance from local practice is associated with better performance;
- effects vary by practice.

This undermines a simple "copy the successful locals" prescription.

# 21. Why local imitation is difficult

Organizational practices are not isolated modules.

They are embedded in:
- routines;
- incentive systems;
- skills;
- history;
- organizational culture;
- complementary practices.

A foreign subsidiary may observe a local competitor's successful practice without possessing the broader organizational system that makes the practice work.

This connects the paper to resource-based/evolutionary arguments about **imperfect imitability**.

# 22. Administrative heritage

A central implication is the importance of **administrative heritage**.

Organizations accumulate historically developed:
- routines;
- management practices;
- control systems;
- knowledge;
- skills;
- ways of coordinating people.

When the firm's main FSA resides in those organizational capabilities, foreign subsidiaries may be better off preserving/transferring them than replacing them with host-country practices the parent does not know how to implement effectively.

# 23. But the paper is NOT a "never localize" argument

Zaheer explicitly cautions against overgeneralization.

If the firm's main FSA is embodied in:
- technology;
- brand;
- scale;
- a differentiated product;
- another non-organizational resource;

then localization of organizational practices may not damage—and may even improve—subsidiary performance.

Some practices may need local adaptation because of:
- regulation;
- professional norms;
- physical constraints;
- local cost conditions;
- legitimacy requirements.

# 24. Multiple sources of isomorphism

A subsidiary may resemble others for different reasons.

## Local isomorphism
Host-country pressure or local efficiency.

## Home-company isomorphism
Replication of parent routines / administrative heritage.

## Global-industry isomorphism
Some practices converge worldwide because industry efficiency demands a common model.

Example logic from the paper:
- trading-desk structures can be globally standardized because customers and counterparties expect familiar functional roles.

## Economic constraint rather than imitation

Some practices look locally similar simply because all firms face the same local economics.

Example:
- expensive Tokyo real estate encourages tightly packed office layouts regardless of a firm's home-country style.

# 25. Integration versus responsiveness

The study offers a nuanced way to approach the classic MNE design problem.

Instead of asking only:

**Should the subsidiary be globally integrated or locally responsive?**

Ask for each practice/activity:

1. Is this activity a source of firm-specific advantage?
2. How strongly is it embedded in the firm's administrative heritage?
3. Does the host environment force or strongly reward adaptation?
4. Is there a global industry norm?
5. Can the local practice actually be imitated effectively?

Different activities within the same subsidiary can therefore require different levels of integration and local responsiveness.

# 26. Caveats / limitations

Zaheer stresses several limitations:

- small number of organizational units;
- one industry;
- only the U.S. and Japan;
- performance measured at one point in time;
- specific organizational practices may have different effects in other industries;
- LOF sources vary by firm/country/industry;
- findings based on matched cross-national subunits should be viewed as suggestive rather than universal.

At the same time, the measures aggregate many individual trader responses, and the matched-pair design offers unusually strong control over parent-company differences.

# 27. Main theoretical contributions

The paper:

1. provides empirical evidence that **liability of foreignness exists**;
2. shows LOF even in a highly global and apparently friction-light industry;
3. connects MNE theory to **firm-specific advantage** and the resource-based view;
4. tests institutional-theory predictions about local isomorphism;
5. shows that copying successful local practices can be difficult or counterproductive;
6. demonstrates the role of **administrative heritage**;
7. adds nuance to global integration versus local responsiveness;
8. helps connect theories of why MNEs exist with theories of how MNEs should be organized.

The coffee case demonstrates LOF-like frictions before a formal MNE even exists:
- unfamiliar institutions;
- weak information;
- distance;
- logistics;

- customs uncertainty;
- dependence on local relationships/intermediaries.

A professional importer would need capabilities that systematically reduce those disadvantages.

# 31. Exam retrieval cue

**Use Zaheer when asked why a foreign company might underperform local competitors or how an MNE should respond to host-country differences.**

Answer structure:

1. Identify the relevant **LOF sources**: spatial, unfamiliarity, host-country legitimacy/institutional, home-country constraints.
2. Identify the firm's **FSA**.
3. Ask whether the FSA depends on preserving home-country routines/administrative heritage.
4. Separate activities that should remain integrated from activities that should be localized.
5. Do not assume copying local firms automatically improves performance.

 OVERCOMING THE LIABILITY OF FOREIGNNESS 
 SRILATA ZAHEER 
 University of Minnesota 
 This study addressed the question of whether firms in a competitive, 
 globally integrated environment face a "liability of foreignness" and to 
 what extent either importing home-country organizational capabilities 
 or copying the practices of successful local firms can help them over- 
 come this liability. Predictions were tested with a paired sample of 24 
 foreign exchange trading rooms of major Western and Japanese banks 
 in New York and Tokyo. Results support the existence of a liability of 
 foreignness and the role of a firm's administrative heritage in providing 
 competitive advantage to its multinational subunits. They also highlight 
 the difficulty firms face in copying organizational practices from other 
 firms. 
 Researchers in international business have long theorized that multina- 
 tional enterprises (MNEs) doing business abroad face costs (Hymer, 1976; 
 Kindleberger, 1969) arising from the unfamiliarity of the environment, from 
 cultural, political, and economic differences, and from the need for coordi- 
 nation across geographic distance, among other factors. This liability of for- 
 eignness has been the fundamental assumption driving theories of the mul- 
 tinational enterprise (Buckley & Casson, 1976; Caves, 1982; Dunning, 1977; 
 Hennart, 1982). Further, it has been argued that to overcome the liability of 
 foreignness and compete successfully against local firms, MNEs need to 
 provide their overseas subunits with some firm-specific advantage, often in 
 the form of organizational or managerial capabilities (Buckley & Casson, 
 1976; Caves, 1982; Dunning, 1977; Hennart, 1982). Resource-based views of 
 strategy (Barney, 1991; Lippman & Rumelt, 1982; Winter, 1991) have also 
 stressed the importance of firm-specific resources and organizational capa- 
 bilities in providing sustainable competitive advantage to firms. These theo- 
 ries suggest that multinationals' subunits will try to overcome the liability of 
 foreignness by importing capabilities embodied in the organizational prac- 
 tices of their parent enterprises, particularly if the subunits are competing in 
 an undifferentiated product market in which other sources of imported com- 
 petitive advantage, such as a brand name, a superior technology, or factor- 
 cost advantages, have little role to play.

 that foreign firms will have lower profitability than local firms, all else being
 equal, and perhaps even a lower probability of survival.

 Firm-Specific Advantage Versus Local Isomorphism
 To overcome the liability of foreignness and compete with local firms, a
 multinational enterprise needs to either bring to its foreign subunit resources
 or capabilities specific to the firm (firm-specific advantages) or attempt to
 mimic the advantages of successful local firms. The costs that contribute to
 creating a liability of foreignness do not directly point to which of these
 options an MNE might prefer. An MNE might attempt to reduce the costs of
 coordination directly by giving total autonomy to a foreign subunit allowing
 it to behave like a local firm by, for instance, performing all value-adding
 stages in the foreign location. Or the parent might attempt to compensate for
 distance-related costs through scale economies or the premium attached to a
 brand name imported from the home country. Researchers studying inter-

 Prahalad & Doz, 1987) have suggested a range of industry-specific factors

 MNE subunit, which in turn could affect the subunit's degree of similarity to
 local firms. Rosenzweig and Singh (1991) suggested that MNE subunits in
 multidomestic industries might be much more prone to local isomorphism

 Writers in international organization theory (Arias & Guillen, 1991; Powell &
 DiMaggio, 1991; Rosenzweig & Nohria, 1994; Rosenzweig & Singh, 1991)
 have argued that MNE subunits are most likely to attend to the demands of
 their local environments. Others (Westney, 1988, 1993; Zaheer, 1992) have
 argued for the existence of multiple isomorphic pulls on MNE subunits, and
 Rosenzweig and Nohria (1994) suggested a number of factors that, across
 industries or practices, might moderate the extent of local isomorphism. The
 assumption behind the arguments for local isomorphism in these models is
 that an MNE subunit operating in a particular local environment (say, a
 subsidiary of an American multinational in Germany) will tend to follow
 local practices either because of coercive isomorphism (caused by the re-
 quirements of German regulations, for example), normative isomorphism
 (caused by professionally imposed requirements), or mimetic isomorphism
 (imitation caused by the success of local exemplars; DiMaggio & Powell,
 1983). Mimetic isomorphism is likely to be particularly important in areas of
 free and unregulated economic competition, where firms will try to adopt
 the practices of others that appear to be the most successful in a given
 environment. 
 These arguments suggest that if local firms are generally more profitable 
 than foreign firms in an industry (that is, if Hypothesis 1 is supported), the

 MARKET AND BUREAUCRATIC CONTROLS 
 Two criteria were used in selecting the organizational practices to be 
 studied. First, the chosen practices had to be very different in the two sets of 
 local trading rooms-those in the Western banks in New York and the Jap- 
 anese banks in Tokyo-as only in that case would I be able to separate the 
 effects of local isomorphism from the effects of imported firm-specific ad- 
 vantage. In addition, the practices needed to have some influence on trading 
 room performance. 
 An exploratory phase of the study eliminated several organizational 
 practices and pointed toward others. For example, formal structure was 
 identical in all the trading rooms in New York and Tokyo and was therefore 
 inappropriate for the purposes of this study. Every trading room had inter- 
 bank traders dealing with currency pairs (dollar-mark traders and dollar-yen 
 traders, for example), a smaller number of customer traders who executed 
 orders from corporate clients, and a back office that confirmed deals and 
 wired payments. Even the physical layout of trading desks tended to be fairly 
 similar in both sets of local rooms, although a few of the Western banks in 
 New York had island-shaped layouts rather than the less space-consuming 
 straight-line layouts common in both New York and Tokyo. I eliminated 
 both formal structure and physical layout as organizational practices worth 
 comparing. 
 The one area in which the Western trading rooms in New York differed 
 markedly from the Japanese trading rooms in Tokyo was in their control 
 systems, which can be viewed as made up of market and bureaucratic con- 
 trols.2

 the Japanese banks in Tokyo. Again, other researchers studying Japanese 
 organizations have commented on the tight bureaucratic control exercised in 
 Japanese organizations (Beechler, 1990).

 and of bureaucratic microcontrols, were examined to test the alternative

 separately for each practice as I expected them to influence performance in 
 different directions, with market controls having a positive impact on profits 
 per trader and bureaucratic controls having a negative impact. Not just the 
 absolute distance, but the direction in which a particular trading room's 
 practices differed from local or from home country practices, was likely to 
 make a difference to its performance.

 METHODS 
 An initial exploratory study, which consisted of observation and inter- 
 views conducted at eight foreign exchange trading rooms of U.S. and Japa-

 York and Japanese trading rooms in Tokyo. This initial phase was followed 
 by two surveys: the first, given to all foreign exchange traders in each room, 
 asked about room-level practices (the independent variables); the second, 
 given to the head of each trading room, assessed the dependent variable, 
 trading room performance. Use of these two respondent groups was de- 
 signed to eliminate common method bias.

 RESULTS 
 Caveats 
 Some caveats about interpreting the results of the data analysis are in 
 order. Although the sample is small, the measures reflect an aggregation of 
 the responses of 174 traders, so the reported correlations are ecological (Hof- 
 stede, 1980), or correlations of means. Regression analyses of such measures 
 tend to have high explanatory power because of the robustness of the un- 
 derlying measures and their low variance. Second, the sample consists of 
 carefully matched pairs and contains a large proportion of the population I 
 sought to represent (the nine U.S. and ten Japanese banks that had full- 
 fledged currency-trading operations in both New York and Tokyo). 
 The problem of small numbers will affect nearly any study that attempts 
 to look in depth at cross-national matched pairs of subunits of the same firm 
 in any single industry. Without a doubt, one should treat findings based on 
 small numbers as suggestive rather than definitive. But comparing local 
 isomorphism with imported firm-specific advantage requires studies that 
 control for industry and firm because specific organizational practices differ

 In results for the signed difference measures, the differing effects on the 
 liability of foreignness of market controls and of microcontrols is more 
 marked. The higher the extent of market control compared to local norms, 
 the lower a trading room's liability of foreignness. Thus, whether a trading 
 room is in New York or in Tokyo, strong market-based controls relative to the 
 local average are likely to lead to better performance. The opposite appears 
 to be the case with microcontrols, whose extensive use appears to depress 
 performance. 
 As for imported firm-specific advantage, the closeness (the absolute 
 distance) to home practices does not have a significant relationship to the 
 liability of foreignness for bureaucratic or for market control, but the direc- 
 tion in which the extent of market control differs from practices at home 
 does have an effect: the higher the extent of market control in a focal unit 
 compared to practices at home, the greater the liability of foreignness. 
 The combination of that finding with the earlier finding, that high mar- 
 ket control compared to local practice is linked to better performance, has 
 some very subtle implications regarding firm-specific advantage. Although it 
 is clear that high use of market-based control relative to local practice en- 
 hances performance, subunits of firms whose home trading rooms do not use 
 high levels of market control (and whose foreign rooms have higher levels of 
 market control than their counterparts at home) suffer in performance. This 
 result may occur because the firms lack the internal expertise needed to 
 implement market controls. This finding reinforces the idea that firms find 
 it difficult to implement practices with which they are unfamiliar and that

 IMPLICATIONS 
 The reported results imply that, when the major source of firm-specific 
 advantage lies in organizational capabilities, foreign subunits may be better 
 off sticking with routines imported from home than attempting to com- 
 pletely mimic local practices with which their parent organizations have 
 little experience. This result supports the role of administrative heritage in 
 providing competitive advantage to multinational subunits (Bartlett & 
 Ghoshal, 1989) and offers evidence of the difficulty firms face in copying 
 organizational routines from other firms (Lippman & Rumelt, 1982; Nelson & 
 Winter, 1982). 
 Although the results generally support the role of firm-specific advan- 
 tage as embodied in imported organizational practices over local isomor- 
 phism as an effective way for MNE subunits to overcome the liability of 
 foreignness, this finding needs to be interpreted with caution. In particular, 
 it is possible that in industries in which firm-specific advantage is embodied 
 in technology, brand name, scale, or some other resource, rather than in 
 organizational capabilities, local isomorphism in organizational practices 
 may not hurt and may even help subunit performance. 
 Overall, the influence of isomorphism and of firm-specific advantage on 
 the organizational practices of multinational subunits as they try to over- 
 come their liability of foreignness is complex and often practice-dependent. 
 Certain practices-for example, the formal structure of a trading room-are 
 globally isomorphic, driven by practicality and efficiency considerations: 
 any caller from anywhere in the world wanting to trade can ask for the 
 dollar-mark trading desk and be assured that there will be one. Deviation 
 from such global norms would likely be a source of competitive disadvan- 
 tage as such a structure would ignore the demands of the global environment 
 (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967). Again, certain practices may be isomorphic with 
 those of the local environment, but not because of the regulatory influences 
 of coercive isomorphism, or because of the professional norms of normative 
 isomorphism, or because of deliberate mimetic behavior, but because of 
 economic considerations alone. For instance, even those Western trading 
 rooms in Tokyo whose home offices in New York have island layouts have 
 little choice but to adopt a tightly packed, straight-line layout, given the high 
 price of real estate in the downtown Ohtemachi district of Tokyo. Some 
 theorists, notably Westney (1988, 1993), have begun to deal with some of the 
 complexity in the issue of isomorphism in multinational subunits by dis- 
 cussing the pressures for isomorphism from the multiple "organizational 
 fields" (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983) to which a multinational subunit belongs. 
 The results of this study also suggest a different way of looking at the 
 issue of integration versus responsiveness (Prahalad & Doz, 1987) for differ- 
 ent elements of value-adding activity in multinational enterprises and may 
 offer a way to integrate the theory of multinational enterprise (Buckley & 
 Casson, 1976; Dunning, 1977; Hymer, 1976) with theories of multinational 
 strategy and organization (Bartlett & Ghoshal, 1989; Prahalad & Doz, 1987).

 For instance, one could speculate that value-adding activities that provide a
 multinational corporation with its firm-specific advantage are best carried
 out in a globally integrated manner (whether the integration happens
 through centralization or through coordination or through the systematic
 replication of the source of the advantage across all of the MNE's subunits)
 but that other value-adding activities, those in which the multinational has
 no particular advantage over local firms (or even faces a disadvantage), are
 best left to the discretion of the subunit. Of course, an MNE may have
 different advantages over its competitors in various markets, a factor that
 shifts the choice from the simple one of whether a particular activity should
 be managed in an integrated or responsive fashion, to the more complex
 issue of the transnational management (Bartlett & Ghoshal, 1989) of net-
 works of subunits. Perhaps groups of subunits that share similar advantages
 over their competitors could be managed as an integrated cluster. Cross-
 subunit learning could be encouraged if a particular subunit develops ex-
 pertise that may be valuable for another subunit facing a different type of
 competitor in another local market. If, for example, the Japanese trading
 rooms in New York gradually became more adept in implementing market
 controls, they might be in a position to transfer their skill in this area to their
 sister rooms in say, France, where such skill might provide a competitive
 advantage over French trading rooms.
 This study suggests several possible directions for future research. The
 issue of the liability of foreignness itself opens up a range of possible re-
 search questions and suggests a need for both longitudinal and cross-
 sectional empirical work in different industries with different types of firms.
 How does the liability of foreignness behave in different industries and over
 time? As a foreign firm gains experience in a particular location, does its
 liability of foreignness decline? As industries globalize, does foreignness
 continue to carry costs? What aspect of foreignness matters most: a unit's
 ownership, the location of its head office, or the perception that its parent is
 foreign? Does variation in the legitimacy of foreign firms in different coun-
 tries influence the liability of foreignness?
 The study also suggests a need to empirically compare strategic choice-
 based theories of competitive advantage to institutional theories, even in
 nonforeign situations. Further, researchers need to understand what influ-
 ences successful intra- and interfirm replication of organizational practices.
 CONCLUSIONS 
 This study established that a liability of foreignness exists in a compet- 
 itive industry, foreign exchange trading, and examined whether local iso- 
 morphism or imported capabilities better explained the difference in the 
 profitability of local and foreign trading rooms in international banks. The 
 results suggest that firm-specific advantage, as embodied in imported organ- 
 izational practices, may be a more effective way for multinational enter- 
 prises' subunits to overcome the liability of foreignness than imitation of
