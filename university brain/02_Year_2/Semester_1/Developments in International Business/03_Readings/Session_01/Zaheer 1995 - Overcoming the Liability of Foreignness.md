# Zaheer (1995) - Overcoming the Liability of Foreignness

**Author:** Srilata Zaheer  
**Journal:** *Academy of Management Journal*, 38(2), 341-363  
**Role:** Supplementary Session 1 reading / foundational framework  
**Core concepts:** liability of foreignness, firm-specific advantages, local isomorphism, administrative heritage, integration vs responsiveness

> This is a comprehensive structured extraction for search, study, and exam retrieval. It preserves the paper's theory, hypotheses, empirical design, measures, main results, implications, and caveats without reproducing the copyrighted article verbatim.

# 1. Research problem

International Business theory assumes that multinational enterprises operating abroad face extra costs simply because they are foreign. Zaheer's paper asks two linked questions:

1. **Does a measurable liability of foreignness actually exist even in a highly global industry?**
2. **If it does, what helps a foreign subsidiary overcome it: importing the parent firm's distinctive organizational practices or becoming more like successful local firms?**

The paper therefore brings together:
- classic theory of the multinational enterprise;
- the resource-based view / firm-specific advantage logic;
- institutional theory and local isomorphism;
- international strategy's integration-responsiveness problem.

# 2. Liability of Foreignness (LOF)

## Definition

LOF is the set of **additional costs a firm operating in a foreign market incurs that a comparable local firm would not incur**.

Zaheer identifies four broad, potentially overlapping sources.

## 2.1 Spatial-distance costs

Costs directly associated with physical/geographic separation, such as:
- travel;
- transport;
- communication;
- coordination over distance;
- coordination across time zones.

## 2.2 Firm-specific unfamiliarity costs

Costs arising because the foreign firm:
- lacks roots in the local environment;
- has less experience with local institutions;
- lacks local relationships;
- is less familiar with customers and market practices;
- needs to learn how the host market works.

## 2.3 Host-country costs

Costs created by the environment in which the foreign subsidiary operates, such as:
- weak legitimacy;
- discrimination against foreign firms;
- economic nationalism;
- stakeholder preference for domestic firms;
- host-country institutional barriers.

## 2.4 Home-country costs

Constraints originating in the MNE's home country that affect overseas activity.

Example discussed in the article:
- restrictions on sales of high-technology products to certain countries imposed on U.S.-owned MNEs.

## Context dependence

The importance of each LOF source varies across:
- industry;
- firm;
- host country;
- home country.

Whatever the source, the theory implies that, all else equal, foreign firms should have:
- lower profitability than local firms;
- potentially lower probability of survival.

# 3. Horizontal versus vertical/networked MNEs

Zaheer suggests LOF may be especially acute in **simple, market-seeking horizontal MNEs**.

A horizontal MNE's subsidiaries largely replicate one another across countries and compete on a local-for-local basis.

By contrast:
- a **vertical MNE** places different stages of an integrated value chain in different countries;
- a **networked MNE** assigns differentiated roles to subsidiaries.

Vertical/networked structures may partly offset LOF through:
- global scale;
- scope economies;
- specialized subsidiary roles;
- integrated value creation.

# 4. Why foreign-exchange trading is a strong empirical test

The study examines foreign-exchange trading rooms of major Western and Japanese banks in **New York and Tokyo**.

This is an especially demanding setting in which to demonstrate LOF because:
- FX is highly global;
- currencies are undifferentiated products;
- major banks use broadly similar technology;
- trading systems have global reach;
- many traditional product-level advantages are weak.

If foreignness still creates disadvantages here, the finding strongly supports the idea that cross-border operation carries persistent costs.

# 5. Why local firms may still have an advantage in FX

Although speculative currency trading crosses borders, customer business remains substantially local.

Local trading rooms can be better embedded in:
- customer networks;
- local information flows;
- central-bank relationships;
- policy networks;
- local expectations about interest rates and currency movements.

Because information is critical in FX trading, this local embeddedness can produce a material performance advantage.

# 6. Hypothesis 1 - existence of LOF

**H1:** Foreign trading rooms will be less profitable than local trading rooms in the same location, all else equal.

This is the direct empirical test of liability of foreignness.

# 7. How can the MNE overcome LOF?

The paper contrasts two strategic responses.

## 7.1 Import firm-specific advantage from home

Classic MNE and resource-based theory suggests the parent transfers resources/capabilities that locals cannot easily replicate.

Potential FSAs include:
- scale or scope economies;
- brand;
- differentiated products;
- technology;
- cost advantages;
- organizational routines;
- managerial skills;
- learning capability;
- knowledge-transfer capability.

In the FX setting, traditional product/technology advantages are limited, so **organizational and managerial capabilities** are especially important.

## 7.2 Adapt to / imitate local firms

Institutional theory predicts that foreign subsidiaries face pressure to resemble the host environment.

Three forms of isomorphism are relevant:

### Coercive isomorphism
Adoption of local practices because of regulation or formal host-country requirements.

### Normative isomorphism
Convergence because professions, educational systems, or accepted standards impose common practices.

### Mimetic isomorphism
Imitation of organizations seen as successful local models.

The key puzzle is whether a foreign subsidiary performs better by:
- preserving/importing its parent firm's distinctive practices; or
- becoming more like successful host-country competitors.

# 8. Hypotheses 2a and 2b

## Hypothesis 2a - imported FSA

Foreign trading rooms whose organizational practices more closely resemble their own firm's **home-country trading room** should show less LOF.

## Hypothesis 2b - local isomorphism

Foreign trading rooms whose organizational practices more closely resemble **local host-country trading rooms** should show less LOF.

These predictions pull the subsidiary in different directions.

# 9. Organizational practices selected for the study

Zaheer needed practices that:
- differed substantially between local Western rooms in New York and local Japanese rooms in Tokyo;
- plausibly affected performance.

Formal structure and physical layout were mostly too similar to provide a useful comparison.

Two control-system dimensions showed strong differences.

# 10. Market controls

Western trading rooms in New York relied more heavily on market-like controls, including:
- performance-based compensation;
- recruiting experienced traders from the external labor market;
- higher labor turnover.

## Performance logic

FX room profits depend heavily on traders' individual ability and effort.

Performance-linked incentives can:
- attract higher-quality traders;
- motivate effort;
- connect individual outcomes to organizational performance.

Zaheer therefore expects stronger market control to be associated with better performance in this setting.

# 11. Bureaucratic microcontrols

Japanese trading rooms in Tokyo used tighter detailed restrictions on:
- intraday open positions;
- overnight open positions;
- limits by currency;
- limits by trader.

These are called **bureaucratic microcontrols**.

## Why they may reduce performance

Successful speculative trading requires:
- rapid interpretation of market information;
- immediate decisions;
- discretion by the trader closest to the market.

Strict central rules can prevent traders from exploiting time-sensitive opportunities.

The paper therefore expects bureaucratic microcontrol to be negatively associated with performance in this specific context.

# 12. Research design

## Exploratory phase

The study began with observation and interviews in eight U.S. and Japanese bank trading rooms in New York and Tokyo to identify meaningful differences in organizational practice.

## Full sample

The full sample contained **28 trading rooms**:
- 13 in New York;
- 15 in Tokyo;
- belonging to eight Western and eight Japanese banks.

Survey responses were obtained from **198 traders**, a 79% response rate overall.

## Matched sample used for core tests

The main tests of local isomorphism versus imported FSA use a matched sample of **24 trading rooms**:
- 12 New York;
- 12 Tokyo;
- six Western parent banks;
- six Japanese parent banks.

Each bank contributes a home and foreign room, making direct parent-to-subsidiary comparison possible.

The matched-sample survey includes **174 traders**.

Subgroup counts reported:
- Japanese rooms in Tokyo: 51 trader respondents;
- Japanese rooms in New York: 31;
- Western rooms in Tokyo: 53;
- Western rooms in New York: 39.

# 13. Data collection

Zaheer combines:
- observation;
- interviews;
- trader questionnaires;
- separate questionnaires/interviews with trading-room heads.

Trader responses measure organizational practices.

Heads of rooms provide performance measures.

Using different respondent groups helps reduce common-method bias.

# 14. Measuring liability of foreignness

For each foreign room, LOF is measured as the difference between:

**average profits per trader of local rooms in the same city - profits per trader of the focal foreign room.**

Example logic:
- compare a Japanese-owned trading room in New York with the average performance of Western/local rooms in New York.

Higher positive values = greater LOF.

The analysis also uses a perceptual performance measure built from trader assessments of:
- quality of traders;
- profitability relative to other rooms;
- total room profit;
- profit per trader.

# 15. Measuring local isomorphism

For each organizational practice, the paper measures how far the foreign room is from the **average local practice in the host city**.

Practices measured:
- market control;
- bureaucratic microcontrol.

Both **signed distance** and **absolute distance** are calculated.

Why signed distance matters:
- not only the degree of difference, but the **direction** of deviation can influence performance.

Smaller absolute distance from local rooms = greater local isomorphism.

# 16. Measuring imported firm-specific advantage

The paper compares each foreign room with its paired **home-country room in the same parent bank**.

Again, the analysis calculates:
- signed distance from home practice;
- absolute distance from home practice.

Smaller distance from the home room = stronger replication/transfer of the parent firm's organizational practice.

# 17. Control variables

## Trading-room age

Used to distinguish LOF from a simple **liability of newness**.

## Trading-room size

Measured by number of traders.

## Actual risk-taking

FX profitability is strongly related to risk.

Risk is captured through:
- open-position behavior;
- size of positions;
- loss-taking behavior.

This allows the analysis to avoid attributing profit differences simply to foreign rooms taking less risk.

# 18. Descriptive patterns

Reported average profits per trader ranged from approximately:
- **$0.95 million** in Japanese trading rooms in New York;
- up to **$1.88 million** in Japanese trading rooms in Tokyo;
- with Western rooms in New York/Tokyo in between.

The subsamples differed significantly on:
- market control;
- age;
- size.

Bureaucratic control differences were marginally significant.

Western rooms in New York were generally the oldest.

Japanese rooms in Tokyo were considerably larger on average.

# 19. Result: LOF exists

After controlling for age and size, local rooms were significantly more profitable than foreign rooms.

The analysis reports a significant difference supporting **Hypothesis 1**.

Neither age nor size adequately explains the foreign-local profitability gap.

This is important because the study finds LOF even in:
- a highly global industry;
- an undifferentiated product market;
- technologically standardized operations.

Zaheer interprets this as strong support for Hymer's classic claim that there are persistent costs to doing business abroad.

# 20. Local isomorphism versus imported FSA - main result

The relationship is complex rather than one universal rule.

Overall, the findings give stronger support to the importance of **imported firm-specific organizational capabilities / administrative heritage** than to the claim that subsidiaries should simply imitate local firms.

For the practices studied:
- greater similarity to the parent/home organizational system can reduce LOF;
- local imitation does not consistently improve performance;
- in some cases, greater distance from local practice is associated with better performance;
- effects vary by practice.

This undermines a simple "copy the successful locals" prescription.

# 21. Why local imitation is difficult

Organizational practices are not isolated modules.

They are embedded in:
- routines;
- incentive systems;
- skills;
- history;
- organizational culture;
- complementary practices.

A foreign subsidiary may observe a local competitor's successful practice without possessing the broader organizational system that makes the practice work.

This connects the paper to resource-based/evolutionary arguments about **imperfect imitability**.

# 22. Administrative heritage

A central implication is the importance of **administrative heritage**.

Organizations accumulate historically developed:
- routines;
- management practices;
- control systems;
- knowledge;
- skills;
- ways of coordinating people.

When the firm's main FSA resides in those organizational capabilities, foreign subsidiaries may be better off preserving/transferring them than replacing them with host-country practices the parent does not know how to implement effectively.

# 23. But the paper is NOT a "never localize" argument

Zaheer explicitly cautions against overgeneralization.

If the firm's main FSA is embodied in:
- technology;
- brand;
- scale;
- a differentiated product;
- another non-organizational resource;

then localization of organizational practices may not damage—and may even improve—subsidiary performance.

Some practices may need local adaptation because of:
- regulation;
- professional norms;
- physical constraints;
- local cost conditions;
- legitimacy requirements.

# 24. Multiple sources of isomorphism

A subsidiary may resemble others for different reasons.

## Local isomorphism
Host-country pressure or local efficiency.

## Home-company isomorphism
Replication of parent routines / administrative heritage.

## Global-industry isomorphism
Some practices converge worldwide because industry efficiency demands a common model.

Example logic from the paper:
- trading-desk structures can be globally standardized because customers and counterparties expect familiar functional roles.

## Economic constraint rather than imitation

Some practices look locally similar simply because all firms face the same local economics.

Example:
- expensive Tokyo real estate encourages tightly packed office layouts regardless of a firm's home-country style.

# 25. Integration versus responsiveness

The study offers a nuanced way to approach the classic MNE design problem.

Instead of asking only:

**Should the subsidiary be globally integrated or locally responsive?**

Ask for each practice/activity:

1. Is this activity a source of firm-specific advantage?
2. How strongly is it embedded in the firm's administrative heritage?
3. Does the host environment force or strongly reward adaptation?
4. Is there a global industry norm?
5. Can the local practice actually be imitated effectively?

Different activities within the same subsidiary can therefore require different levels of integration and local responsiveness.

# 26. Caveats / limitations

Zaheer stresses several limitations:

- small number of organizational units;
- one industry;
- only the U.S. and Japan;
- performance measured at one point in time;
- specific organizational practices may have different effects in other industries;
- LOF sources vary by firm/country/industry;
- findings based on matched cross-national subunits should be viewed as suggestive rather than universal.

At the same time, the measures aggregate many individual trader responses, and the matched-pair design offers unusually strong control over parent-company differences.

# 27. Main theoretical contributions

The paper:

1. provides empirical evidence that **liability of foreignness exists**;
2. shows LOF even in a highly global and apparently friction-light industry;
3. connects MNE theory to **firm-specific advantage** and the resource-based view;
4. tests institutional-theory predictions about local isomorphism;
5. shows that copying successful local practices can be difficult or counterproductive;
6. demonstrates the role of **administrative heritage**;
7. adds nuance to global integration versus local responsiveness;
8. helps connect theories of why MNEs exist with theories of how MNEs should be organized.

**Ghemawat:** borders and home bias remain significant at the macro level.

# 30. Connection to Bean of Africa

The coffee case demonstrates LOF-like frictions before a formal MNE even exists:
- unfamiliar institutions;
- weak information;
- distance;
- logistics;
- payment-system mismatch;
- documentation;
- customs uncertainty;
- dependence on local relationships/intermediaries.

A professional importer would need capabilities that systematically reduce those disadvantages.

# 31. Exam retrieval cue

**Use Zaheer when asked why a foreign company might underperform local competitors or how an MNE should respond to host-country differences.**

Answer structure:

1. Identify the relevant **LOF sources**: spatial, unfamiliarity, host-country legitimacy/institutional, home-country constraints.
2. Identify the firm's **FSA**.
3. Ask whether the FSA depends on preserving home-country routines/administrative heritage.
4. Separate activities that should remain integrated from activities that should be localized.
5. Do not assume copying local firms automatically improves performance.
