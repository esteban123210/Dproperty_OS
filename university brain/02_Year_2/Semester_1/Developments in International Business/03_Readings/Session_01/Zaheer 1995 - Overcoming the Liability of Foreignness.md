# Zaheer 1995 - Overcoming the Liability of Foreignness

**Author:** Srilata Zaheer  
**Journal:** Academy of Management Journal, 38(2), 341-363  
**Status:** Supplementary Session 1 reading / foundational framework

## Core question
Do foreign firms actually suffer a **liability of foreignness**, and if so, should foreign subsidiaries overcome it by copying local firms or by importing organizational capabilities from the parent MNE?

## Liability of Foreignness (LOF)
Zaheer defines LOF as the additional costs of doing business abroad that a local firm does not incur.

Four broad sources are identified:
1. **Spatial-distance costs** - travel, transportation, coordination over distance and time zones.
2. **Firm-specific unfamiliarity costs** - lack of roots, knowledge, experience, and relationships in the local environment.
3. **Host-country costs** - lack of legitimacy, discrimination, economic nationalism, institutional barriers.
4. **Home-country costs** - restrictions or constraints originating in the firm's home country that affect foreign operations.

These costs vary by firm, industry, host country, and home country.

## Why LOF matters
All else equal, a foreign firm should be less profitable—and may have a lower probability of survival—than a comparable local firm unless it possesses advantages that compensate for the extra costs.

This is a foundational explanation for why **firm-specific advantages (FSAs)** matter in International Business.

## Competing responses to LOF
Zaheer compares two broad logics:

### 1. Local isomorphism
The foreign subsidiary becomes more like successful local firms.

Logic:
- local practices are adapted to the local institutional environment;
- copying them may increase legitimacy and fit;
- this aligns with institutional theory.

### 2. Imported firm-specific advantage
The foreign subsidiary relies on distinctive routines and capabilities developed by the parent organization.

Logic:
- the reason the MNE can compete abroad may be precisely that it possesses capabilities locals do not;
- copying local practices too closely can erase that advantage;
- organizational routines are also difficult to imitate effectively.

## Empirical setting
The study examines foreign-exchange trading rooms of Western and Japanese banks in New York and Tokyo.

This is a demanding test because foreign exchange is a highly global, competitive, relatively standardized activity where one might expect cross-border disadvantages to be small.

The study nevertheless finds evidence that foreign trading rooms were less profitable than local rooms, supporting the existence of LOF.

## Main finding
When the firm's key advantage is embedded in **organizational capabilities**, foreign subsidiaries may perform better by retaining/importing effective parent-company routines rather than fully imitating local organizational practices.

However, the result is not a simple 'never localize' rule.

Some practices may need to adapt because of:
- local economic conditions;
- regulation;
- physical constraints;
- institutional legitimacy;
- globally accepted industry standards.

The right unit of analysis may therefore be the **specific activity or practice**, not the entire subsidiary.

## Integration-responsiveness implication
Zaheer anticipates a central MNE design problem:

- Activities that embody the firm's distinctive advantage may benefit from global integration / replication.
- Activities where the MNE has no distinctive advantage—or where local fit is critical—may be better decentralized and locally responsive.

This creates a more nuanced model than choosing between total standardization and total localization.

## Connection to Session 1 slides
The slides simplify the logic into:

**Foreignness creates extra costs**  
→ MNE needs **FSAs**  
→ examples: brand, technology, marketing, capital access, know-how, management capability  
→ if FSAs exceed the liability of foreignness, foreign expansion can be competitive.

## Connection to Ghemawat
Ghemawat shows at the macro level that borders and distance remain consequential.

Zaheer explains one firm-level consequence:

> Borders translate into additional costs and disadvantages for foreign firms.

## Connection to Bean of Africa
The case illustrates several LOF-like frictions even before a formal MNE exists:
- unfamiliar procedures;
- customs uncertainty;
- weak cross-border payment infrastructure;
- communication failures;
- address and logistics problems;
- dependence on local knowledge and relationships.

A future importer would need capabilities or partners that systematically reduce these disadvantages.

## Exam-ready version
**Definition:** LOF is the additional cost/disadvantage a foreign firm faces relative to a local firm.  
**Mechanism:** distance, unfamiliarity, legitimacy/institutional pressures, and home/host-country constraints.  
**Strategic response:** build or transfer FSAs strong enough to offset LOF while selectively adapting activities where local fit matters.  
**Use when:** explaining foreign subsidiary underperformance, localization choices, or why an MNE needs transferable advantages.  
**Best comparison:** local responsiveness / isomorphism vs imported firm-specific capabilities.
