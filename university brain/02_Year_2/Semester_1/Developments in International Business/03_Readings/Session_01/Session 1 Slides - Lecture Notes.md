# 3. Bean of Africa - opening case exercise

The slide deck explains that the case is based on personal experience importing products from outside the EU/EEA and is intended to conceptualize those experiences through International Business frameworks.

Students are first asked to recall a time they bought something from abroad, especially outside the EU/EEA, and identify what was harder than expected compared with buying the same thing domestically.

The case is then revisited through several frameworks.

## Institutional Theory and Liability of Foreignness

Prompt:
- In your own cross-border buying or selling experiences, which external factors influenced the transaction?
- How did those factors facilitate or complicate the transaction?

Purpose:
- make formal and informal institutions visible;
- recognize that foreign actors may face disadvantages not faced by locals;
- connect country context to costs of doing business abroad.

## Uppsala / Stages Model

Prompt:
- In cross-border buying/selling experiences, how did things become easier over time?
- What did you learn?
- How did the learning happen?

Purpose:
- introduce experiential learning;
- show how uncertainty can fall as firms accumulate foreign-market knowledge;
- connect knowledge acquisition to gradual commitment.

## Market Entry Modes

Prompt:
- Map every party involved in Felix's import transaction.
- Who are they?
- What problem does each party solve?

Purpose:
- show that foreign-market transactions depend on governance architecture and intermediaries;
- expose hidden actors between producer and customer;
- make clear that each intermediary can solve one friction while creating costs, dependencies, or information asymmetries.

## Transaction Cost Theory and Internalization

Prompt:
- What are the main stages of Felix's transaction?
- For each stage—search, negotiation, contract, monitoring, enforcement—what costs arose?

Purpose:
- distinguish purchase price from the total cost of exchange;
- introduce transaction-cost economics;
- prepare the market-versus-hierarchy decision.

# 10. What distinguishes International Business?

International Business concerns economic activity across national borders. The key managerial distinction is that crossing borders introduces contextual differences and additional uncertainty beyond ordinary domestic business.

Examples emphasized in the lecture include:
- cultural differences;
- political risk and host-government policies;
- foreign-exchange risk;
- distance and transportation costs;
- additional monitoring requirements;
- unfamiliar institutions;
- legitimacy problems;
- cross-border coordination.

These are introduced as **costs of doing business abroad**.

# 11. Liability of Foreignness (LOF)

Source in slides: **Zaheer (1995)**.

The lecture frames LOF as a disadvantage foreign firms face relative to local competitors.

Highlighted mechanisms:
- unfamiliarity with the host country -> learning costs;
- lack of legitimacy;
- economic nationalism or skepticism from local stakeholders;
- broader distance/coordination problems.

LOF varies by **industry and country**. It is not a universal fixed penalty.

# 12. Firm-Specific Advantages (FSAs)

The lecture then asks why an MNE can compete successfully at all if the foreign firm faces LOF and the local competitor does not.

Answer: the MNE needs **firm-specific advantages** capable of offsetting the foreignness disadvantage.

Examples listed in the slides:
- product-differentiation ability;
- superior marketing and/or distribution skills;
- brand names;
- access to capital;
- access to raw materials;
- know-how;
- management skills;
- technology;
- other intangible assets/capabilities.

A useful simplified condition is:

**Value of transferable FSA > additional costs created by foreignness.**

This is not a literal accounting equation but a strategic logic: the foreign firm needs some reason it can outperform local firms despite home-field disadvantages.

# 13. Uppsala / stages logic

The Bean of Africa exercise connects the session to the Uppsala idea of internationalization as a learning process.

Core logic:
- firms begin with limited foreign-market knowledge;
- uncertainty and unfamiliarity constrain commitment;
- actual experience generates market knowledge;
- market knowledge can reduce uncertainty;
- lower uncertainty can support greater commitment;
- international expansion can therefore proceed incrementally.

In the coffee case, one transaction produces knowledge about:
- supplier reliability;
- local relationships;
- international payments;
- logistics providers;
- shipping economics;
- customs procedures;
- documentation;
- address conventions;
- monitoring and tracking;
- hidden costs.

That learning changes the economics and feasibility of future transactions.

# 14. Institutions

The opening case makes the role of institutions visible.

### Formal institutions
Examples include:
- customs rules;
- tariff classification;
- VAT/tax rules;
- payment regulation;
- postal/logistics systems;
- trade preferences;
- documentation requirements.

### Informal institutions
Examples include:
- trust;
- personal relationships;
- local business norms;
- informal problem-solving;
- reliance on networks and intermediaries.

International firms frequently have to operate across multiple formal and informal institutional systems simultaneously.

# 15. Market entry modes and intermediary architecture

The slides ask students to map all parties in the Bean of Africa transaction because international exchange is rarely just "seller -> foreign buyer."

Foreign-market access can involve a continuum of arrangements with different levels of:
- ownership;
- control;
- resource commitment;
- risk;
- local knowledge;
- partner dependence;
- transaction cost.

Even a small import can involve producer, informal intermediary, payment provider, mobile-money infrastructure, logistics carrier, postal operator, customs authority, importer, and other parties.

# 16. Transaction Cost Theory (TCT)

The slides connect the session to **Coase (1937)** and **Williamson (1975)** and later internalization applications.

## Core idea

Every economic transaction has costs **beyond the nominal price** of the good or service.

The Bean exercise explicitly asks students to identify costs across:
- search;
- negotiation;
- contracting;
- monitoring;
- enforcement.

Other relevant cross-border transaction costs include:
- verifying counterparties;
- information gathering;
- coordination;
- payment and FX frictions;
- logistics;
- compliance;
- customs;
- documentation;
- delays;
- opportunism;
- dispute resolution.

The managerial goal is to choose a governance structure that minimizes the relevant total costs and risks—not simply the supplier's quoted price.

# 17. Markets versus hierarchies

The slide deck introduces the classic **make-versus-buy** distinction.

### Market / buy
Coordinate activity through arm's-length exchange with external parties.

### Hierarchy / make
Coordinate activity internally within the firm's organizational boundaries.

The lecture's key point:

> It can be more efficient to organize some economic activities inside firms than through markets.

International example in the slides:
- a foreign subsidiary may sometimes be more efficient than pure exporting through external market relationships.

# 18. Vertical integration

The slides illustrate the value chain as:

**Raw materials -> components -> manufacturing -> distribution -> sales -> service**

A firm may integrate in two directions.

### Backward / upstream integration
Move into activities closer to:
- raw materials;
- components;
- suppliers/inputs.

### Forward / downstream integration
Move into activities closer to:
- distribution;
- sales;
- service;
- end customer.

Vertical integration is one method of replacing external market exchange with internal coordination.

# 19. Internalization and why MNEs exist

Transaction-cost logic is then applied internationally.

If cross-border markets function poorly because of:
- high transaction costs;
- information asymmetry;
- weak contracting;
- difficult enforcement;
- opportunism;
- imperfect knowledge markets;
- unreliable intermediaries;

then it may be more efficient for a firm to own/control the activity rather than repeatedly transact through external parties.

This helps answer a foundational IB question:

**Why does a multinational enterprise exist instead of a set of independent firms simply trading with each other?**

One answer is **internalization**: certain cross-border transactions are more efficiently coordinated inside one multinational organization than across arm's-length markets.

# 20. Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)

FDI is presented as a high-commitment internationalization form in which the firm takes a lasting ownership interest and managerial influence/control in foreign activity.

The strategic question becomes:

**When is foreign ownership/control preferable to exporting, licensing, contracting, or other external modes?**

OLI theory is introduced to organize this answer.

# 21. Eclectic Paradigm / OLI Theory

Sources in slides: **Dunning (1977, 1988, 1998)**.

The deck describes OLI as integrating firm-level and country-level explanations of foreign direct investment.

## O - Ownership advantages

The firm possesses specific capabilities/resources that help it overcome the liability of foreignness.

Examples:
- brand;
- technology;
- know-how;
- managerial capability;
- marketing/distribution capability;
- proprietary assets;
- scale or other firm-specific strengths.

**Question answered:** Why this firm?

Without a sufficient ownership/FSA advantage, a foreign firm may be unable to compensate for LOF.

## L - Location advantages

The host country possesses location-specific assets the firm wants to access.

Examples shown:
- markets;
- natural resources;
- labor;
- other country-specific assets/conditions.

**Question answered:** Why this country/location rather than serving the opportunity entirely from home?

## I - Internalization advantages

There are advantages to replacing arm's-length trade with intra-firm exchange/control.

**Question answered:** Why own/control the activity instead of licensing, contracting, exporting, or relying on an independent partner?

## OLI and FDI

The slides state that we expect FDI particularly when all three conditions coexist:

1. **Ownership advantage** - the firm has a transferable FSA;
2. **Location advantage** - the foreign country offers something worth locating there for;
3. **Internalization advantage** - internal control is superior to arm's-length market exchange.

Compact exam mnemonic:

**O = Why this firm?  
L = Why this country?  
I = Why ownership/internal control?**

# 22. How the Session 1 frameworks fit together

The lecture is best understood as one integrated argument rather than separate definitions.

### Step 1 - Crossing borders creates frictions
Country borders introduce cultural, institutional, political, geographic, informational, and coordination differences.

### Step 2 - Foreign firms suffer LOF
Local firms generally possess familiarity, legitimacy, relationships, and embedded knowledge that a foreign entrant initially lacks.

### Step 3 - The foreign firm needs an FSA
It requires some transferable advantage worth bringing abroad.

### Step 4 - The location must provide a reason to be there
That is the **L** in OLI.

### Step 5 - Governance matters
The firm compares markets with internal organization.

### Step 6 - High market transaction costs can create an internalization advantage
This is the **I** in OLI.

### Step 7 - O + L + I can make FDI/MNE organization rational
This gives a coherent explanation for when direct foreign ownership can make strategic sense.

# 23. Bean of Africa mapped onto Session 1 theory

## Institutions / LOF
The Ugandan-Austrian transaction spans different payment, logistics, customs, documentation, and business systems. The buyer lacks local knowledge and relies on people embedded in Uganda.

## Uppsala
The first transaction functions as an experiential-learning episode. Future transactions begin with more knowledge and lower uncertainty.

## Market-entry / intermediaries
The purchase depends on several parties rather than a robust direct producer-to-consumer process.

## Transaction costs
The economic cost includes much more than coffee price:
- search;
- communication;
- payment failure/fees;
- shipping choices;
- documentation;
- customs;
- monitoring;
- uncertainty.

## Internalization
If repeated commercial transactions remain unreliable or expensive, a future business would need to decide which sourcing/import/distribution activities to control more directly.

## OLI
A scalable business would require:
- **O:** a firm-specific advantage such as European market access, brand, logistics/compliance know-how, customer relationships, or a repeatable sourcing system;
- **L:** attractive Ugandan coffee resources and/or European market demand;
- **I:** a reason to internalize functions instead of relying entirely on arm's-length intermediaries.

# 24. Session 1 exam diagnostic

For an unfamiliar exam case, ask in this order:

1. What specifically becomes harder because the firm crosses a border?
2. What are the sources of LOF?
3. What FSA could compensate for them?
4. What can be learned through experience and gradual commitment?
5. Which institutions shape the activity?
6. What transaction costs arise beyond the quoted price?
7. Which activities should remain market-based and which could be internalized?
8. Does OLI support FDI: why this firm, why this location, and why internal ownership/control?
