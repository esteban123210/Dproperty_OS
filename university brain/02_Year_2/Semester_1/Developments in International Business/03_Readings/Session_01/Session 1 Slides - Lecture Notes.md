# Session 1 Slides - Lecture Notes

**Source:** `Session_1.pdf`  
**Course:** Developments in International Business  
**Session:** 1 - What is International Business? Why do MNEs exist?

## Core framing
International Business concerns firms engaging in **cross-border economic activities**. The defining managerial challenge is that crossing borders adds complexity and uncertainty. International managers therefore need awareness of differences across environments, strategic judgment, and an interdisciplinary perspective.

## Essential concepts from the slides

### 1. International Business
A business becomes an international-business actor when it engages in economic activity across national borders.

The managerial problem is not merely 'selling abroad.' Cross-border activity exposes firms to additional:
- cultural differences;
- political and regulatory differences;
- institutional differences;
- geographic/logistical frictions;
- coordination challenges;
- uncertainty.

### 2. Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)
FDI is an investment made to acquire a lasting interest in an enterprise outside the investor's home economy.

The important distinguishing feature is **control**: the investment gives the investor an effective voice in the management of the foreign enterprise through equity ownership.

**Exam cue:** distinguish FDI from a simple portfolio investment or export transaction by asking whether the investor is acquiring a lasting managerial interest / degree of control.

### 3. Liability of Foreignness (LOF)
Foreign firms can be disadvantaged relative to local competitors because they incur costs local firms do not face.

Important sources highlighted in the slides:
- unfamiliarity with the host country → learning costs;
- lack of legitimacy;
- economic nationalism or skepticism from local stakeholders;
- broader distance and coordination costs.

LOF varies by **industry and country**; it is not a fixed universal penalty.

### 4. Firm-Specific Advantages (FSAs)
MNEs need advantages that compensate for the costs of being foreign.

Examples in the slides include:
- differentiated products;
- superior marketing / distribution capability;
- brands;
- access to capital or raw materials;
- know-how;
- management capabilities;
- technology and other intangible assets.

**Core logic:** if a foreign firm bears extra costs, it needs an offsetting advantage to compete successfully with firms that already understand and belong to the local market.

### 5. Markets versus hierarchies
The slides introduce the classic **make-versus-buy** logic associated with Coase and Williamson.

A firm can coordinate an economic activity through:
- the **market** ('buy' / transact externally); or
- the **hierarchy** ('make' / organize internally).

The central question is which governance form minimizes the full cost of the transaction.

### 6. Transaction costs
A business transaction creates costs beyond the listed price of a good or service.

These can include:
- searching for a counterparty;
- verifying information;
- negotiating;
- contracting;
- monitoring;
- enforcing agreements;
- handling uncertainty;
- coordination;
- payment and currency frictions;
- logistics;
- customs / compliance;
- opportunism.

If market transaction costs become sufficiently high, performing the activity inside the firm may be more efficient.

### 7. Vertical integration / internalization
Vertical integration brings activities in the value chain under common ownership and coordination.

- **Backward / upstream integration:** raw materials, components, production inputs.
- **Forward / downstream integration:** distribution, sales, service.

In an international context, this can help explain why a firm may establish a foreign subsidiary instead of relying only on exporting or intermediaries.

## Bean of Africa connection
A key exercise in the slides asks students to map every party involved in Felix's coffee import and identify what problem each intermediary solves.

This is a practical transaction-cost exercise. The point is to see that every intermediary exists because some market friction has to be solved—but each intermediary can also add cost, delay, coordination needs, information asymmetry, and dependency.

## The Session 1 logic in one chain

**Cross-border activity creates additional frictions**  
→ these generate a **liability of foreignness**  
→ MNEs need **firm-specific advantages** to overcome that liability  
→ cross-border market transactions themselves may be costly  
→ firms compare **markets versus hierarchy**  
→ when internal coordination is more efficient than external contracting, firms may **internalize** activities  
→ FDI and multinational organization can therefore be understood partly as responses to market imperfections and transaction costs.

## Likely exam moves
Be prepared to use these ideas in a new case to explain:
- why a foreign firm is underperforming local competitors;
- whether a competitive advantage is strong enough to justify entering a foreign market;
- why a firm might replace distributors/intermediaries with a subsidiary;
- why exporting may be cheaper in one situation but FDI more efficient in another;
- which cross-border problems can be solved through learning/local adaptation and which may require structural changes to governance.
