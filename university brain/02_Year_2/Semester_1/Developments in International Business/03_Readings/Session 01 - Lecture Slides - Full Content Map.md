# 3. Bean of Africa as the opening case

The case is based on personal experience importing products from outside the EU/EEA and is used to connect ordinary cross-border transactions to IB theory.

The slides ask students to use their own experiences of buying or selling across borders to identify what becomes more difficult internationally than domestically.

## Framework prompt 1 - Institutional theory and LOF

Question: which external factors affected a cross-border transaction, and how did they facilitate or complicate it?

Purpose: recognize that firms operate inside different formal and informal institutional environments and that foreign actors can be disadvantaged relative to locals.

## Framework prompt 2 - Uppsala / stages model

Question: how did cross-border activity become easier over time? What was learned, and through what experience?

Purpose: connect internationalization to experiential learning, uncertainty reduction, and gradual increases in commitment.

## Framework prompt 3 - Market entry modes

Question: map every actor involved in Felix's import transaction and identify which problem each actor solves.

Purpose: show that market access can be structured through multiple intermediaries and governance arrangements rather than simply "firm sells to foreign customer."

## Framework prompt 4 - Transaction Cost Theory / internalization

Question: identify transaction stages—search, negotiation, contracting, monitoring, enforcement—and the costs arising at each stage.

Purpose: distinguish the purchase price from the **total cost of exchange** and introduce why activities may be moved inside firm boundaries.

# 6. Debate format

From Week 2 onward, teams represent opposing strategic perspectives on cases.

The slide-deck debate structure specifies:

- Team A opening statement: 7 minutes;
- Team B opening statement: 7 minutes;
- 10-minute recess / rebuttal preparation;
- Rebuttal 1: 4 minutes per team plus class questions;
- Rebuttal 2: 4 minutes per team plus class questions;
- Closing statement: 2 minutes per team.

Non-presenting students act as critical stakeholders such as a Board of Directors.

Non-presenting teams submit:
- one page excluding references;
- response to the week's prompt;
- focus on general concerns around the business decision and context;
- one group submission.

The Week 2 example is IKEA India. The proposition asks whether IKEA should accelerate expansion and treat India as a priority growth market. One side argues that location advantages outweigh market-specific challenges; the opposing side argues the challenges outweigh the location advantages. Non-debating teams assess whether IKEA should accelerate, maintain, or slow expansion over 3-5 years.

# 10. What makes International Business different?

The lecture frames IB as business activity crossing national boundaries where firms face contextual differences not present—or not present to the same extent—in domestic activity.

The deck emphasizes that international activity exposes firms to additional complexity such as:

- cultural differences;
- political risk and host-government policy;
- foreign-exchange risk;
- physical distance and transport costs;
- additional information/monitoring requirements;
- institutional differences;
- legitimacy problems;
- coordination across borders.

These are collectively introduced as **costs of doing business abroad**.

# 11. Liability of Foreignness (LOF)

Source in slides: Zaheer (1995).

## Core idea

MNEs can be disadvantaged relative to local competitors because foreignness creates extra costs.

The lecture emphasizes particularly:

- unfamiliarity with the host country -> learning costs;
- lack of legitimacy;
- economic nationalism or preference for local actors;
- effects vary by industry and country.

LOF is not one fixed number; it depends on context.

# 12. Firm-Specific Advantages (FSAs)

The slides connect LOF to the question: **why can a foreign firm compete at all if locals start with home-field advantages?**

Answer: the foreign firm must possess **firm-specific advantages** strong enough to offset LOF.

Examples listed:

- product-differentiation ability;
- superior marketing and/or distribution skills;
- brand names;
- access to capital;
- access to raw materials;
- know-how;
- management skills;
- technology;
- other intangible assets/capabilities.

Conceptual relation:

**FSA value > costs of foreignness -> foreign expansion can be competitively viable.**

# 13. Uppsala / stages logic

The Bean of Africa discussion prompt uses Uppsala to highlight the role of **experience and learning** in internationalization.

Core logic captured in the class framing:

- firms begin with limited foreign-market knowledge;
- uncertainty and psychic/institutional distance raise perceived risk;
- experience generates market knowledge;
- knowledge can reduce uncertainty;
- reduced uncertainty can justify increased commitment;
- internationalization can therefore unfold incrementally rather than as one large decision.

In the Felix example, the first coffee transaction produces knowledge about:
- suppliers;
- payment rails;
- logistics;
- customs;
- documentation;
- intermediaries;
- costs;
- risks.

That experiential knowledge could change the attractiveness and feasible structure of a future import business.

# 14. Institutions

The Bean of Africa case is used to make institutions visible.

Formal institutions include:
- customs rules;
- tariff classification;
- tax/VAT rules;
- payment regulation;
- postal/logistics systems;
- trade preferences;
- documentation requirements.

Informal institutions include:
- trust;
- personal relationships;
- norms around doing business;
- informal problem solving;
- reliance on local networks/intermediaries.

International firms must operate across multiple institutional systems simultaneously.

# 15. Market-entry modes and intermediaries

The slides ask students to identify all actors in the coffee transaction and the function of each.

This establishes a general market-entry principle:

Foreign-market access can be organized through a continuum of external and internal arrangements, involving different degrees of:

- ownership;
- control;
- resource commitment;
- risk;
- local knowledge;
- dependence on partners;
- transaction cost.

Even a seemingly simple cross-border purchase may rely on producer, informal intermediary, payment provider, logistics carrier, postal operator, customs authority, importer, and other actors.

# 16. Transaction Cost Theory (TCT)

Sources highlighted in the lecture include Coase (1937), Williamson (1975), and later IB/internalization applications.

## Core premise

Every business transaction has costs beyond the nominal price of the good/service.

The slides explicitly direct students to identify costs in:

- search;
- negotiation;
- contracting;
- monitoring;
- enforcement.

Additional examples from the Bean of Africa context include:
- finding a working supplier channel;
- identifying a trustworthy intermediary;
- payment fees/failures;
- comparing carriers;
- documentation uncertainty;
- customs classification;
- monitoring shipment status;
- dealing with address standards;
- potential dispute resolution.

## Markets versus hierarchies

The firm's governance question is whether to:

- **buy/use markets** through arm's-length transactions; or
- **make/internalize/use hierarchy** through activities coordinated inside the firm.

The lecture describes internal exchange as replacing market transactions when internal organization is more efficient.

# 17. Vertical integration

The slides illustrate the value chain:

**Raw materials -> components -> manufacturing -> distribution -> sales -> service**

A firm can integrate:

- **backward/upstream** toward raw materials, components, suppliers;
- **forward/downstream** toward distribution, sales, or service.

Vertical integration is presented as one way to replace costly or unreliable external market exchange with internal coordination.

# 18. Internalization and the MNE

The same transaction-cost logic is extended internationally.

If arm's-length cross-border markets work poorly because of transaction costs, information problems, contracting problems, weak enforcement, opportunism, or imperfect markets, a firm may prefer direct control through a foreign subsidiary rather than relying purely on exports or external partners.

Thus internalization theory helps answer:

**Why does an MNE exist rather than a set of independent firms trading with each other?**

Answer: in some circumstances, coordinating transactions inside one multinational organization is more efficient than using external markets.

# 19. Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)

FDI is treated as a high-commitment form of internationalization involving ownership/control of business activity abroad rather than simply selling across borders through arm's-length exports.

The lecture uses FDI as the phenomenon that OLI theory is designed to explain.

The key question becomes:

**When is foreign ownership and internal control more attractive than exporting, licensing, contracting, or other market-based modes?**

# 20. Eclectic Paradigm / OLI theory

Sources: Dunning (1977, 1988, 1998).

The deck presents OLI as an integration of firm-level and country-level explanations of FDI.

## O - Ownership advantages

The firm possesses capabilities/resources that allow it to overcome the liability of foreignness.

Examples connect directly to FSAs:
- brand;
- technology;
- managerial capability;
- know-how;
- distribution/marketing capability;
- scale;
- proprietary assets.

Without a sufficient ownership advantage, a foreign firm may be unable to outperform local competitors that do not bear LOF.

## L - Location advantages

The foreign country possesses assets or conditions that make locating activity there attractive.

Examples in the slides include:
- market opportunities;
- natural resources;
- labor;
- other location-specific assets.

Location advantage answers:

**Why this country rather than serving the opportunity entirely from home?**

## I - Internalization advantages

There are benefits to replacing arm's-length exchange with intra-firm coordination.

Internalization advantage answers:

**Why own/control the foreign activity rather than license, contract, export, or rely on an independent partner?**

## OLI decision logic

FDI becomes particularly likely when all three are present:

1. **O:** the firm has an ownership/FSA advantage;
2. **L:** the foreign location offers valuable location advantages;
3. **I:** internal organization is more efficient/valuable than external market exchange.

Compact exam logic:

**O asks why this firm.  
L asks why this country.  
I asks why ownership/internal control.**

# 21. How the theories fit together

The deck is not a set of disconnected concepts. The theories form a chain:

### Step 1 - Borders create friction
Cross-border activity is different because country boundaries create extra complexity and costs.

### Step 2 - Foreign firms suffer LOF
Local competitors possess familiarity, legitimacy, and embedded networks.

### Step 3 - MNE needs an FSA
A firm must have some transferable advantage to justify competing abroad.

### Step 4 - Country must offer a reason to locate there
That is the **L** in OLI.

### Step 5 - Governance choice matters
If market exchange is costly or unreliable, internalization may create value.

### Step 6 - O + L + I can make FDI rational
This provides one explanation for the existence of MNEs.

# 22. Bean of Africa mapped onto the theory

## Institutions / LOF
- Uganda and Austria operate through different payment, logistics, customs, and documentation systems.
- Felix lacks local knowledge and depends on people embedded in Uganda.

## Uppsala
- the first transaction is a learning experiment;
- knowledge accumulated can reduce uncertainty in future transactions.

## Entry modes
- the transaction depends on multiple intermediaries rather than a clean direct producer-to-customer channel.

## Transaction costs
- search, communication, payment, logistics, documentation, customs, and monitoring costs are large relative to the coffee's nominal purchase price.

## Internalization
- if Felix commercialized the opportunity, he would need to ask whether some functions should be controlled more directly rather than repeatedly improvised through ad hoc external transactions.

## OLI
Potential commercial import activity would require:
- **O:** some FSA such as market knowledge, distribution, branding, customer access, process capability;
- **L:** attractive Ugandan coffee resources and European premium demand;
- **I:** a reason to internalize parts of sourcing/import/distribution rather than relying entirely on arm's-length intermediaries.

# 23. Session 1 exam framework

For an unfamiliar case, use this diagnostic order:

1. What specifically changes because the firm crosses a border?
2. What are the firm's LOF sources?
3. What FSA can compensate for those disadvantages?
4. What does the firm learn through experience?
5. What external institutions/intermediaries shape the transaction?
6. What transaction costs arise?
7. Which activities should remain market-based and which should be internalized?
8. Does OLI support FDI? Why this firm, this location, and this governance mode?
