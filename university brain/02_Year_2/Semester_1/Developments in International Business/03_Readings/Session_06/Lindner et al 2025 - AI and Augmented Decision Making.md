## Core thesis

AI changes the behavioral assumptions of IB theory by easing some human rationality constraints while introducing machine and human–AI interaction biases.

## Mechanism and evidence

Humans face incomplete information and cognitive limitations, use heuristics and display judgment and present-state biases. AI adds information and processing capability, but biased training data and objectives can distort outputs; benefits and trust in human–AI interaction can also be uneven. Reassess the boundary conditions of theories such as Uppsala and internalization when decision processes are augmented.

## Application and limits

Augmentation is not perfect information or replacement of experiential learning. Local definitions, objectives, incentives and oversight still matter. Compare Anwar implementation governance and Moses data accuracy; directly useful for mock-exam Question 2.

ABSTRACT
This paper discusses the impact of artificial intelligence (AI) on decision-making in multinational enterprises 
(MNEs) and its theoretical implications for international business (IB) research. Building on the concept of 
bounded rationality, we analyze how AI affects biases and heuristics that drive human decision-making and 
identify new, AI-specific biases that could distort decision-making outcomes in MNEs. We propose that AI- 
augmented decision-making will alter the behavioral assumptions underlying established IB theories, 
requiring a reevaluation of these theories ’ boundary conditions. By addressing these shifts, the paper contributes 
to updating IB research to the realities of AI-augmented decision landscapes.
1. Introduction
New technologies have always been central to international business 
(IB). Global interconnectedness through the internet (e.g., Kotha, Rin -
dova, & Rothaermel, 2001 ) has boosted internationalization through 
seamless communication and e-commerce activities (e.g., Globerman, 
Roehl & Standifird, 2001 ), and advanced analytics have shaped the 
decision-making of international managers (e.g., Duan et al., 2018 ; 
Strange & Zucchella, 2017 ). In this vein, the current revolution in arti -
ficial intelligence (AI) and machine learning ( Brynjolfsson & Mcafee, 
2017 ) will leave its imprint on IB. Especially the augmentation of human 
decision-making, where decision-makers are supported rather than 
replaced by algorithm-based technologies, creates new competitive ad -
vantages and leads to superior performance ( Krakowski, Luger & Raisch, 
2022 ; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021 ). First applications in industries such as 
pharmaceuticals ( Fleming, 2018 ) or talent acquisition ( Wilson, Daugh -
erty & Bianzino, 2017 ) provide preliminary evidence for such claims and 
provide a glimpse into how augmented human decision-making will 
affect multinational companies (MNEs) and internationalization 
processes.
However, despite these new opportunities for decision-making and 
the expected substantial implications of this technological revolution for 
MNEs, AI applications receive little attention from theoretical IB 
scholarship. This is a surprising omission because AI can fundamentally 
shift decision-making in MNEs. If AI support changes the nature and 
outcomes of decision-making processes in MNEs, MNEs will change their 
behaviors. We, consequently, think that IB scholarship needs to incor -
porate AI into its theorizing. Otherwise, the theoretical perspectives on 
decision-making in internationalization processes will fall short of the 
“ new ” reality that MNEs face.
We argue that the implications of AI applications in MNEs will be 
strongest for those aspects of theories that relate to behavioral as -
sumptions, specifically those regarding human decision-making under 
incomplete information. In several prominent theories in IB (and 
beyond), heuristics and biases stemming from bounded rationality 
( Simon, 1955 ) help explain decision-making. Theories like Interna -
tionalization Theory, Internalization Theory, Institutional Theory, or the 
Knowledge-Based View that, at least implicitly, build on the Behavioral 
Theory of the Firm ( Cyert & March, 1963 ) do not assume that 
decision-makers operate in a strictly utility-maximizing way. Instead, 
they suffer from biases that influence their cognitive abilities and rely on 
heuristics, leading to decisions based on bounded rationality.
We propose that AI applications in MNEs will influence these biases 
and heuristics in the strategic decision-making processes that are 
fundamental to IB theories. AI can contribute to lifting the boundaries of 
rationality that limit MNE managers ’ decision-making. Consequently, AI 
helps to reduce how MNE managers suffer from behavioral biases in 
their decision-making and may challenge assumptions about rationality

We see this as an impetus for IB research to reevaluate the scope and 
validity of our central theories in a world of human-AI decision-making. 
Fourth, we outline avenues for future research that will assist IB theory 
in retaining predictive power over MNE actions in an age of human-AI 
complementation.
2. Bounded rationality in IB
Neoclassical economic theories assume that individuals make de -
cisions according to rational choices ( Nash, 1950 ; Savage, 1954 ; von 
Neumann & Morgenstern, 1944 ). These theories suggest that individuals 
will choose among a given, fixed set of alternatives with known prob -
ability distributions of outcomes for each alternative to maximize their 
expected value of a given utility function. For example, when deciding 
whether to enter a foreign market, an MNE might choose the market that 
offers the highest expected return based on perfectly rational and 
complete information about all potential markets. Thus, 
decision-makers have complete information and use it to behave ratio -
nally. However, in practice, decision-makers cannot know about the full 
set of available alternatives, the probability distributions of these al -
ternatives, or the exact function underlying their utility generation 
( Simon, 1990 ). In addition, humans make their decisions based on their 
perceptions about their environment ( Simon, 2000 ). For instance, an 
MNE might perceive a foreign market to be highly risky based on pre -
vious experiences in other countries, even though objective data sug -
gests that this new market is stable and offers high potential returns. The 
bounded rationality framework ( Simon, 1956 ) provides a theoretical 
representation of how the divergence between the perception of the 
environment and the actual environment shapes decision-making.
The starting point of the framework is that decision-makers fail to 
optimize their utility function rationally as they suffer from two 
boundaries of rationality ( Simon, 1955 ): incomplete information and 
cognitive limitations. Incomplete information relates to the decision- -
makers’ inability to gather all necessary information for utility optimi -
zation. Cognitive limitations relate to decision-makers’ inability to 
process all information for utility optimization. These boundaries of 
rationality are constraints that increase the differences between the 
perception of the environment and its objective reality, leading to 
greater deviation from rational decision-making—i.e., bounded ratio -
nality. In the context of FDI, bounded rationality may lead an MNE to 
misunderstand the difficulties of operating in a new country due to 
cognitive limitations, preventing it from making optimal investment 
decisions.
To navigate bounded rationality, decision-makers resort to heuri -
stics—cognitive shortcuts that enable them to make decisions despite 
incomplete information and cognitive limitations ( Gigerenzer, 2004 ; 
Simon, 1990 ). In IB, MNE managers may rely on heuristics when 
selecting foreign markets for expansion. For example, they favor coun -
tries they are more familiar with or have had previous success in, rather 
than objectively evaluating all potential markets. In this context, heu -
ristics are not mere cognitive quirks but powerful tools that allow 
decision-makers to operate within bounded rationality.
While heuristics can outperform complex decision-making strategies 
under conditions of high uncertainty ( Artinger, Gigerenzer, & Jacobs, 
2022 ), they also introduce biases, which we understand as systematic 
errors that subtly influence how information is processed and decisions 
are made ( Dutton & Jackson, 1987 ; Tversky & Kahneman, 1974 ). For 
example, a decision-maker might assume that a market similar to 
another successful market will perform just as well, leading to over -
confidence in market entry decisions. Or, decision-makers might prefer 
to stick to established markets instead of exploring new opportunities 
that seem less familiar or riskier.
2.1. Decision-making in IB
A core aspect of IB is that information about foreign markets is 
generally incomplete. Various IB theories use this lack of information to 
explain MNE behavior and hypothesize how they may overcome the 
challenges they face in foreign markets. Due to the prevalence of 
boundaries of rationality in IB, decision-making under bounded ratio -
nality, either explicitly or implicitly, has become a central building block 
of IB theories ( Aharoni, Tihanyi, & Connelly, 2011 ; Elia, Larsen, Pisci -
tello, 2019 ; Foss & Weber, 2016 ; Narula, Asmussen, Chi, & Kundu, 2019 ; 
Schubert, Baier, & Rammer, 2018 ). We focus our discussion of bounded 
rationality in IB on four theories that are central to IB (while also having 
applications outside of IB): Internationalization Theory, Internalization 
Theory, Institutional Theory, and the Knowledge-Based View.
In the context of Internationalization Theory ( Johansen & Vahlne, 
1977 , Vahlne & Johanson, 2017 , 2020 ), bounded rationality affects how 
MNEs make internationalization decisions ( Dow, Liesch, & Welch, 2018 ; 
Niittymies, 2020 ; Vahlne & Schweizer, 2022 ), select foreign markets 
( Ambos, Cesinger, Eggers, & Kraus, 2020 ; Clark, Li, & Shepherd, 2018 ),

The concept of liability of foreignness and the perspectives on 
Institutional Theory (which liabilities of foreignness builds on) relate to

familiar with a foreign context plays a crucial role in international in -

foreignness perspective. Research suggests that, due to bounded ratio -
nality, MNE managers are more likely to invest in domestic markets or 
“ familiar ” foreign countries ( Bell, Filatotchev, & Rasheed, 2012 ; De 
Maeseneire & Claeys, 2012 ; Filatotchev, Bell, & Rasheed, 2016 ; Ke, Ng, 
& Wang, 2010 ).
Along the lines of the Knowledge-Based View ( Kogut & Zander, 
1993 ), bounded rationality can curb the efficient flow ( Makela, Ander -
sson, & Seppala, 2012 ), absorption ( Cuervo-Cazurra & Rui, 2017 ), and 
sourcing ( Monteiro, 2015 ) of knowledge in organizations. Due to 
bounded rationality, decision-makers frequently favor existing and 
proven knowledge within the MNE ( Monteiro, 2015 ).
Our reading of the literature highlights that the central IB theories 
discuss bounded rationality in a wide array of phenomena across many 
subdomains. As a result, AI-driven changes in the boundaries of ratio -
nality will have a very broad impact on decision-making in IB. These 
boundaries, in turn, lead to two categories of biases that are particularly 
prominent in IB research: judgment biases and present state biases. We 
briefly outline these in the next section before outlining how the use of 
AI for decision-making will alter the boundaries of rationality and, 
eventually, change these biases. Afterward, we explain how such 
changes will affect IB theory. We will also highlight machine biases that 
AI can introduce into IB theory.
2.2. Heuristics and biases in IB
2.2.1. Judgment biases in IB
Judgment biases
1
arise when individuals must make decisions under 
conditions of uncertainty — that is, when they lack sufficient information 
about the objectively correct answer and are thus compelled to rely on 
subjective impressions ( Pohl, 2004 ). Judgments refer to beliefs about 
the likelihood of uncertain events, such as predicting the outcome of an 
election. These beliefs are typically formed using data of limited validity 
and are processed through heuristic strategies ( Tversky & Kahneman, 
1974 ). However, these heuristics or certain characteristics of the event 
may distort judgment. For instance, individuals may assess probabilities 
based on how much one event resembles another (representativeness 
heuristic), judge likelihood based on how easily examples come to mind 
(availability heuristic), or anchor their estimates on an initial reference 
point (anchoring bias). Additionally, people often consider themselves 
better than average in terms of ability and judgment, reflecting an 
overconfidence bias (e.g., Alicke & Govorun, 2005 ; Kruger, 1999 ).
Judgment biases are critical in shaping key theoretical frameworks 
such as Internalization Theory and the Knowledge-Based View. These 
biases become particularly relevant in IB, where managers often face 
heightened uncertainty due to unfamiliar foreign markets.
2.2.2. Present state biases in IB
Several types of present state biases illustrate how current conditions 
influence decisions involving information, knowledge, or other param -
eters. One such bias is the status quo bias, which refers to the tendency to 
disproportionately favor the existing state of affairs when evaluating 
alternatives ( Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988 ). Another example is the 
confirmation bias, which involves individuals showing strong confi -
dence in their preferred hypothesis while discounting opposing infor -
mation ( Klayman, 1995 ; Popper, 1959 ; Wason, 1960 ). A further 
manifestation of present state bias is the familiarity bias, where in -
dividuals prefer to make decisions in contexts where they feel more 
knowledgeable. For instance, decision-makers may favor and allocate 
resources to more familiar options, even when unfamiliar alternatives 
might offer greater benefits ( Garbuio, King, & Lovallo, 2011 ; Heath & 
Tversky, 1991 ).
Present state biases are highly relevant to understanding interna -
tionalization decisions and the behavior of MNEs. For instance, confir -
mation bias might lead managers to continue investing in markets where 
they have previously experienced success, even when emerging markets 
present more profitable opportunities. Similarly, the familiarity effect 
could cause MNEs to favor countries with institutional frameworks 
similar to their home country ( Bell et al., 2012 ).
3. AI and bounded rationality in IB
In this section, we outline how the use of AI in decision-making af -
fects the bounded rationality of humans who rely on AI. First, we discuss 
how AI reduces the boundaries of rationality that humans experience. 
This is to say, how AI reduces incomplete information and cognitive 
limitations. Second, we outline how this change to the boundaries of 
rationality translates into a reduction in the biases that human decision- 
makers experience when using AI. Third, we propose that AI will not 
only reduce the boundaries of rationality. AI suffers from machine biases 
that determine whether and to what degree human-AI decision-making 
becomes more rational. In this discussion, we use the introduction of AI- 
driven “ control towers ” and “ digital twins ” in Renault ’ s supply chain as 
an example to illustrate how AI changes decision-making in IB. Fig. 1
summarizes the proposed effects of AI on bounded rationality.

AI can significantly reduce information gaps in IB by automating the

drawing on pre-trained models and large-scale datasets, AI tools also

customer demographics, and competitive intensity ( Amore et al., 2023 ). 
In doing so, AI helps decision-makers build a more complete and 
nuanced understanding of foreign market environments, thereby 
addressing one of the key informational constraints underpinning 
bounded rationality.
Beyond expanding access to information, AI also addresses the 
cognitive limitations that constrain human decision-making in interna -
tional business. Managers often struggle to make sense of the vast and 
complex data required to evaluate foreign markets, especially when that 
data is unstructured, multilingual, or highly contextual. AI systems help 
overcome these limitations by processing and structuring such data, for 
example, extracting insights from local news articles, legal documents, 
and customer feedback in different languages ( Kim et al., 2024 ). This 
capability allows MNEs to interpret cultural nuances and institutional 
conditions that would otherwise be difficult to grasp through manual 
analysis. In addition, AI tools can identify non-obvious patterns and 
correlations within large datasets, supporting a deeper understanding of 
local market dynamics and facilitating the detection of weak signals 
across global contexts ( Grimes, von Krogh, Feuerriegel, Rink, & Gruber, 
2023 ). Explainable AI models further enhance cognitive support by 
making complex algorithmic outputs more interpretable to human 
decision-makers. These models help clarify the causal relationships be -
tween local developments and global performance, enabling MNE 
managers to move beyond intuition and engage in more structured, 
evidence-based reasoning ( Senoner, Netland, & Feuerriegel, 2022 ; 
Weiser & von Kroh, 2023 ). In this way, AI not only expands what 
decision-makers can know but also what they can cognitively process 
and act upon.
AI can further extend the boundaries of rationality by enabling sce -
nario modeling and real-time feedback, which allow decision-makers to 
anticipate, test, and refine strategic options with greater precision. 
Through advanced simulation capabilities, AI can model a wide range of 
market entry strategies or operational decisions, accounting for vari -
ables such as regulatory shifts, supply chain disruptions, and consumer 
behavior changes across multiple countries ( Weiser & von Kroh, 2023 ). 
These simulations help MNEs forecast potential outcomes, such as rev -
enue growth, market positioning, or risk exposure, before committing to 
action. Just as importantly, AI systems provide immediate feedback by 
analyzing real-time data on how chosen strategies are performing, 
enabling rapid course corrections. For example, changes in customer 
sentiment or competitive responses can be quickly detected and incor -
porated into revised decision models ( Lee & Puranam, 2016 ; Weiser, 
Jarzabkowski, & Laamanen, 2020 ). This continuous feedback loop not 
only supports more agile and responsive decision-making but also en -
hances organizational learning by replacing trial-and-error approaches 
with data-driven experimentation. In complex and dynamic interna -
tional environments, these capabilities allow firms to adapt more 
quickly and effectively, thereby reducing the risks associated with un -
certainty and misjudgment.
Renault’s deployment of AI technologies in its global supply chain 
illustrates how firms can reduce both informational and cognitive lim -
itations through data-driven decision-making. The company has 
implemented AI-powered control towers and digital twin systems that 
monitor real-time data from over 6,000 suppliers, enabling continuous 
analysis of performance, risk, cost, and sustainability metrics 
( SupplyChainBrain, 2024 ; Trecan, 2024 ). These systems reduce infor -
mation gaps by scanning vast data sources in real time and highlighting 
emerging disruptions before they escalate. At the same time, they 
address cognitive constraints by structuring and synthesizing large 
volumes of complex supplier data, far beyond what human managers 
could process alone. The use of digital twins further enhances these 
capabilities by enabling predictive modeling and scenario testing, 
helping Renault to visualize future outcomes and make forward-looking 
decisions ( Flaherty, 2022 ; Renault Group, 2025 ). As a result, 
decision-makers in the firm suffer less from incomplete information and 
cognitive limitations.
3.2. The effect of AI on biases in IB decision-making
Building on our analysis of biases and heuristics in IB research and 
our understanding of how AI can push the boundaries of rationality, we 
conceptualize how AI may shift biases that serve as explanatory mech -
anisms in IB theories. Our central argument is that since AI raises the 
boundaries humans experience in their rationality, decision-makers will 
suffer less from bounded rationality and be better positioned to make 
optimal decisions ( Simon, 1955 , 1957 , 1990 , 1997 ). Consequently, AI 
will reduce the decision-making biases that result from these biases. 
Below, we highlight how AI affects the biases that build the basis for 
many IB theories. Fig. 1 illustrates this indirect effect of AI by the dashed 
arrows running from boundaries of rationality to bounded rationality 
and the resulting biases.
AI can play a central role in reducing judgment biases by expanding 
the range and depth of information available to decision-makers ( von 
Kroh, Roberson, & Gruber, 2023 ; Weiser & von Kroh, 2023 ). One key 
mechanism is AI’s ability to generate and evaluate a wide set of alter -
native scenarios. By simulating multiple outcomes based on diverse in -
puts, such as regulatory changes, consumer preferences, or competitor 
strategies, AI enables MNEs to make more informed comparisons be -
tween strategic options ( Shrestha, Krishna, & von Kroh, 2021 ). This 
reduces reliance on the most readily recalled examples or familiar paths, 
thereby mitigating availability bias. It also weakens the influence of 
anchoring and overconfidence by challenging initial assumptions and 
past successes with data-driven projections of unfamiliar contexts 
( Mussweiler, Strack, & Pfeiffer, 2000 ; Wang, Yang, Abdul, & Lim, 2019 ). 
In addition, AI systems can support more objective reasoning through 
probabilistic tools. By incorporating base rates (e.g., average success 
rates of market entrants) and presenting natural frequencies (e.g., like -
lihood of political instability or currency fluctuations), AI helps 
decision-makers avoid base rate neglect and reduces representativeness 
bias, where overly simplistic comparisons between markets may distort 
judgment ( Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, 1995 ; Kliegr, Bahník, & Fürnkranz, 
2020 ). Real-time simulations and feedback mechanisms further enhance 
this process by allowing decision-makers to experiment with hypothet -
ical strategies and immediately assess their projected outcomes ( Lee & 
Puranam, 2016 ; Vert, 2023 ; Weiser, Jarzabkowski, & Laamanen, 2020 ). 
These feedback loops improve experiential learning and reduce over -
reliance on intuition or heuristics developed in prior markets. In doing 
so, AI helps create a more balanced and evidence-based foundation for

AI also helps reduce status quo and present-state biases by encour -
aging decision-makers to move beyond familiar routines and consider 
less obvious but potentially more advantageous alternatives ( Wang 
et al., 2019 ). In IB, managers often favor known strategies or markets 
due to uncertainty or lack of information about unfamiliar environ -
ments. AI challenges this tendency by surfacing data-driven insights that 
highlight underexplored opportunities, such as emerging markets or 
unconventional entry strategies, that may not align with past practices 
but offer strong potential. In doing so, AI reduces the tendency to default 
to existing preferences simply because they are familiar. Moreover, AI 
actively counters confirmation bias by presenting disconfirming evi -
dence and incorporating diverse data sources. Instead of reinforcing 
prior beliefs, AI systems can spotlight contradictory trends, competitor 
actions, or local dynamics that may challenge an established mental 
model. This helps decision-makers reevaluate their assumptions and 
adapt their strategies accordingly. Finally, AI makes unfamiliar or 
ambiguous environments more navigable by processing and structuring 
large volumes of unstructured data, such as local news, regulatory texts, 
or customer sentiment in foreign languages ( De Maeseneire & Claeys, 
2012 ). By turning fragmented and context-specific information into 
coherent insights, AI reduces the cognitive burden associated with 
interpreting new markets. As a result, decision-makers are less likely to 
avoid the unknown and more willing to engage with novel, 
data-informed options.
Taken together, AI ’ s capacity to reduce judgment and present-state 
biases supports more flexible and adaptive decision-making strategies 
in international business. By expanding the range of alternatives 
considered, integrating diverse and disconfirming data sources, and 
structuring complex or ambiguous information, AI enables decision- 
makers to move beyond cognitive shortcuts and habitual responses. 
Importantly, the reduction of bias is not merely cognitive — it is also 
structural. AI systems embed new decision routines, such as continuous 
environmental scanning, real-time feedback, and scenario simulation, 
that institutionalize broader and more balanced information use. These 
mechanisms help firms move from reactive, experience-based decision- 
making to proactive, evidence-driven strategy development ( Senoner 
et al., 2022 ; Wang et al., 2019 ). As such, AI does not simply enhance 
decision quality at the individual level but transforms how organizations 
process information, learn from uncertainty, and engage with global 
complexity. In this way, AI emerges as a boundary-expanding and 
bias-countering force that redefines the limits of rationality in IB 
decision-making.
Renault ’ s use of AI in its global operations offers a practical illus -
tration of how these bias-reducing mechanisms work in tandem. 
Through AI-powered control towers and digital twin technologies, 
Renault continuously monitors real-time data from over 6000 suppliers, 
enabling the simulation of alternative sourcing scenarios and risk fore -
casts ( Flaherty, 2022 ; Renault Group, 2025 ; SupplyChainBrain, 2024 ; 
Trecan, 2024 ). These tools reduce judgment biases by allowing 
decision-makers to test hypothetical outcomes and evaluate supplier 
performance based on objective, multi-criteria assessments rather than 
relying on intuitive judgments or past experiences. At the same time, 
Renault ’ s algorithmic partner selection process helps mitigate 
present-state and status quo biases by encouraging diversification 
beyond familiar suppliers. Instead of defaulting to established routines 
or long-standing relationships, the company uses AI-generated insights 
to identify strategic opportunities in less conventional geographies and 
underexplored networks. By embedding these technologies into its 
decision-making processes, Renault has shifted from reactive, 
routine-driven practices to a more adaptive and data-informed 
approach. The company ’ s improved responsiveness to global supply 
chain disruptions highlights the tangible benefits of using AI to over -
come the limitations of bounded rationality in international business 
contexts.
3.3. Machine biases created by AI in IB decision-making
Above, we outlined how AI helps decision-makers reduce some of 
their boundaries of rationality and reduce the biases they experience in 
decision-making. However, AI systems also suffer from their own biases. 
Like bounded rationality that affects human decision-making, the “ ra -
tionality ” of AI systems can be bounded. This results in machine biases 
that affect the outcomes of AI-based decision-making by humans. There 
is a wide-ranging field of research on machine biases that either result 
from biased training sets or biases in the algorithm itself (e.g., Crawford, 
2013 ; Cowgill & Tucker, 2020 ; Obermeyer, Powers, & Mullainathan, 
2019 ; Osoba & Welser, 2017 ). For example, AI has been criticized for 
reinforcing implicit biases in the training data it obtains, particularly if 
potentially biased humans classified the data. Crawford and Calo (2016)
show that a bias against African-American defendants informed an al -
gorithm designed to predict the likelihood that defendants will re-offend 
after being released from custody. In another example, Lambrecht and 
Tucker (2016) show that algorithm-driven targeting of advertisements 
has an inherent gender bias. By integrating AI support into 
decision-making, companies import these biases into their 
decision-making. Table 1 provides a summary of the generic machine 
biases introduced by applying AI in decision-making. While not inten -
ded as a complete list of potential limitations to AI, this section outlines 
how AI can suffer from some forms of bounded rationality or create new 
boundaries of human rationality that, in turn, result in new 
decision-making biases. We group these new AI-driven biases as “ biased 
data, ” “ biased objectives, ” “ biased benefits, ” and “ biased interaction. ” 
Biased data and objectives refer to distortions in the functioning of the 
AI. They are similar to the limits of rationality encountered by human 
decision-makers and can thus be considered “ human-like ” machine 
biases. On the other hand, biased benefits and interaction refer to dis -
tortions in the collaboration between humans and AI. These “ generic ” 
biases are unique to AI and differ from extant conceptualizations of 
human bounded rationality. Fig. 1 illustrates these machine biases as a 
separate box labeled “ Machine Biases. ” Table 1 summarizes the effects of 
these machine biases.
3.3.1. Biased data of AI in IB
Biased training data in AI may be the category of biases that human 
decision-makers can relate to most easily. At the heart of the AI algo -
rithm lies a model that often uses enormous amounts of training data. 
However, limitations in this training data can negatively affect AI sys -
tems, which, like human decision-makers, suffer from incomplete in -
formation. These limitations may stem from biased human data coding, 
low data availability, or institutionalized inequalities in the data used 
( Chandler & Munday, 2011 ; Karimi, G ´enois, Wagner, & Singer, 2018 ; 
Ntoutsi et al., 2020 ; Tufekci, 2014 ). The incomplete or skewed infor -
mation in the training data leads to data bias in the AI model ( Leavy, 
Meaney, Wade, & Greene, 2020 ). For instance, an AI used to assess 
foreign market attractiveness might disproportionately favor countries 
with extensive historical data on economic performance (e.g., the United 
States and Germany) while underestimating the potential of markets 
with less comprehensive data, such as certain African or Southeast Asian 
countries. This underrepresentation could discourage MNEs from 
considering these emerging markets for expansion.
AI systems may also become biased against groups underrepresented 
in the data or represented only with specific attributes. In the context of 
international hiring, AI tools trained on hiring data from one region (e. 
g., North America) might inadvertently introduce biases when used in 
different cultural or regulatory environments, such as Asia or Europe. 
Feedback and reinforcement loops in AI training could further solidify 
this bias ( Gebru, 2020 ). This bias becomes visible in cases such as tar -
geted advertising, where AI systems might skew their output based on 
race, gender, or socio-economic status ( Lambrecht & Tucker, 2019 ; 
Sweeney, 2013 ). This could cause MNEs to miss significant growth op -
portunities in markets where the AI ’ s training data does not adequately

3.3.2. Biased objectives of AI in IB
AI, like humans, can suffer from cognitive limitations. While humans 
are limited by the senses through which they capture their environment, 
AI is limited by the complexity of its objective function ( Amodei et al., 
2016 ). For instance, an AI system to optimize global supply chains might 
be given a simple objective function to minimize costs. While this may 
result in cost reductions, the AI could overlook critical factors such as 
local labor conditions or regulatory requirements, leading to compliance 
issues or reputational damage for the MNE. Amodei et al. (2016) pro -
pose that these distortions can result from the misspecification of the 
machine ’ s objective function, negative side effects due to an incomplete 
objective, or because the AI identifies “ shortcuts ” to achieve its goals in 
unintended ways (i.e., reward hacking). In other words, the AI suffers 
from “ incomplete information ” about the consequences of its actions or 
“ cognitive limitations ” regarding the full complexity of the case to which 
it is applied.
The resulting biased objectives of the AI can substantially distort AI- 
based decision-making ( Lambrecht & Tucker, 2019 ). For example, an 
online algorithm managing career advertisements showed a bias against 
female website users, targeting them predominantly with product ad -
vertisements instead of career-related content. This bias did not arise 
from data bias (as female users were not underrepresented) but from the 
AI ’ s objective function, which prioritized short-term economic value.
3.3.3. Biased benefits from AI in IB
While humans and AI can suffer from bounded rationality that results 
in biases, these limitations can also extend to the interaction between 
humans and AI. As a generic AI bias, that differs from human-like biases, 
biased benefits of using AI manifest in how MNE managers derive value 
from AI systems in different functional areas. For example, AI might 
significantly enhance decision-making in supply chain optimization by 
identifying bottlenecks and reducing costs. However, it may fail to 
improve decisions in areas where human intuition and experiential 
knowledge still play a significant role. This uneven benefit of AI usage 
reflects what Dell ’ Acqua et al. (2023) describe as a “ jagged ” techno -
logical frontier for AI, where AI improves decision-making for some 
tasks but not for others. This presents challenges for MNEs in predicting 
when and how AI will be most useful for specific IB decisions.
Moreover, the growing complexity of AI systems makes it difficult for 
humans to understand why certain applications work well and others do 
not. For instance, when MNEs use LLMs for global marketing commu -
nications or customer service in different countries, these models eval -
uate plain-language prompts probabilistically. This makes it challenging 
for managers to control how the AI responds across different cultural 
contexts. LLMs might produce unexpected results if a human manager 
adjusts a prompt by adding emotional or cultural references, which 
could distort the intended message in a different market ( Li, Zhao, 
Wang, & Liu, 2023 ). In IB, this unpredictability could lead to inconsis -
tent marketing messages or customer interactions across regions, 
potentially damaging brand consistency in global markets. This high -
lights the emerging need for “ prompt engineering ” ( Meincke, Mollick, & 
Terwiesch, 2023 ; White et al., 2023 ) to fine-tune AI interactions, 
particularly in culturally diverse international settings.
Dell ’ Acqua et al. (2023) also show that not all human 
decision-makers benefit equally from AI. While, on average, AI improves 
decision-making, lower-skilled employees or managers with less inter -
national experience may benefit more from AI tools than highly expe -
rienced executives who rely on intuition and deep industry knowledge. 
This unequal distribution of AI benefits can result in biased 
decision-making outcomes, as those with less experience may become 
overly dependent on AI, while highly skilled professionals may

3.3.4. Biased interaction with AI in IB
Research has long discussed the prevalence and implications of 
technology aversion ( Davis, 1989 )—a boundary of human rationality in 
the use of technology. Technology aversion can manifest as resistance to 
adopting AI tools in global operations. For example, MNE managers may 
exhibit a status quo bias by resisting AI-driven decision-making tools 
when expanding into foreign markets, preferring traditional methods 
based on personal experience and intuition. As another generic machine 
bias, this biased human-AI interaction can lead to suboptimal outcomes, 
as managers may undervalue the AI’s capabilities and rely instead on 
less accurate human judgment. Studies have shown that human 
decision-makers tend to mistrust AI, even if they experience positive 
performance effects from AI-based decision-making ( Tong, Jia, Luo, & 
Fang, 2021 ). Along these lines, incidental observation of AI failure 
( Dietvorst, Simmons, & Massey, 2015 ) can undermine confidence in AI 
systems, leading to biased decision-making.
Trust in AI can differ based on the type of AI, the degree of human 
involvement in decision-making, or the possibility of adjusting the AI’s 
outputs. For example, MNEs may trust robotic AI systems to automate 
repetitive tasks in manufacturing but show hesitation when using AI for 
higher-level decision-making, such as recommending market entry 
strategies or pricing models in foreign markets ( Glikson & Woolley, 
2020 ). Furthermore, MNE managers might be more willing to trust AI 
when they have some control or can adjust the system’s parameters, 
such as fine-tuning AI recommendations based on their knowledge 
( Dietvorst, Simmons, & Massey, 2018 ).
However, while distrust in AI can lead to missed opportunities, 
overconfidence in AI can result in unrealistic expectations. For instance, 
an MNE might expect the AI to accurately predict consumer behavior 
across culturally diverse regions, even though the AI may not be fully 
attuned to local nuances, resulting in lower performance ( Bogert, Lau -
haratanahirun, & Schecter, 2022 ). In such cases, overreliance on AI may 
prevent managers from making critical adjustments based on their own 
expertise, leading to flawed decision-making in complex global markets.
Human-AI interaction may also suffer from human biases about 
other humans who use AI. If an MNE uses algorithmic management to 
evaluate employee performance in its global operations, employees who 
are managed by AI may be perceived as less creative by their peers or 
managers, leading to lower collaboration or team cohesion ( Schweitzer 
& De Cremer, 2024 ). This bias can affect the overall perception of 
AI-managed employees, potentially limiting their career progression or 
involvement in important decision-making processes within the orga -
nization. Consequently, humans’ bounded rationality not only affects 
their interaction with AI but also indirectly influences how others 
interact with AI.
3.4. Implications resulting from institutional variation
In addition to the technological constraints that AI-augmented de -
cision-making faces, MNEs will also have to navigate a complex insti -
tutional environment when it comes to how AI is used, trained, and 
governed. We see variation in both formal and informal institutional 
environments as potential contingencies on how AI shifts the boundaries 
of rationality.
Regarding formal institutions, variation in the regulatory 
frameworks governing AI are at the forefront of potential sources for 
biases. For example, the European Union’s General Data Protection 
Regulation (GDPR) imposes rigorous data protection standards, 
affecting how MNEs collect, process, and analyze data ( Brouthers, 
Geisser, & Rothlauf, 2016 ; Coche, Kolk, & Ocelík, 2024 ; Cui, Meyer, & 
Hu, 2014 ). When entering markets with robust data regulations, MNEs 
might experience constraints in using AI for analytics, leading to 
reduced market responsiveness and competitive disadvantage compared 
to markets with less stringent data privacy regulations. Conversely, 
operating in environments with lax regulatory frameworks allows MNEs 
to leverage more extensive consumer datasets, potentially enhancing 
predictive capabilities and strategic agility, albeit raising ethical con -
cerns ( Gaur et al., 2014 ). As a result, AI-informed decision-making may 
favor some markets over others, not because these markets are inher -
ently more attractive, but because AI has an easier time analyzing their 
data.
Additionally, government investment in AI infrastructure creates 
disparities in MNEs’ strategic choices across markets. Countries such as 
China and the United States, which heavily invest in AI research and 
development, foster environments conducive to rapid adoption and 
innovation in AI-driven strategic decisions ( Buckley, Doh, & Benischke, 
2017 ; Luo & Tung, 2018 ). MNEs operating in such supportive countries 
can utilize advanced AI tools for decisions related to global market 
expansion, risk assessment, and resource allocation. In contrast, MNEs in 
countries with minimal AI infrastructure support might rely more 
heavily on traditional decision-making methods, resulting in slower 
responsiveness to international market dynamics and potential missed 
opportunities in competitive global markets. As a result, uneven access 
to AI support may create an uneven playing field between MNEs, or even 
within MNEs, regardless of product or service quality.
Regarding informal institutions, cultural differences profoundly 
affect the adoption and integration of AI in international business op -
erations. Societies exhibiting high technology acceptance, such as South 
Korea and Japan, are more likely to embrace AI-driven decision-making 
tools across various business functions, including market analysis, 
product development, and customer relationship management 
( Magnusson, Westjohn, Semenov, Randrianasolo, & Zdravkovic, 2013 ; 
Reiche, Lee, & Quintanilla, 2012 ). This facilitates smoother imple -
mentation and acceptance of AI-enhanced strategic decision-making in 
MNEs. Conversely, countries characterized by high uncertainty avoid -
ance, prevalent in parts of Europe and Latin America, may show resis -
tance towards AI-based decisions, particularly in sensitive areas like 
hiring, performance evaluation, or strategic market entry, leading to 
delayed or suboptimal AI integration. While it is unclear whether 
AI-supported decision-making is superior to human-only decision- -
making, uneven use of AI will lead to differences in how decisions are 
made, and hence investment strategies, for example, might look 
different for reasons unrelated to economic optima.
Finally, language barriers might further compound cultural varia -
tion, as most AI tools are optimized for widely spoken languages such as 
English, Spanish, or Chinese. AI-driven market analyses and communi -
cation strategies can thus perform sub-optimally in linguistically diverse 
markets, like Eastern Europe, smaller Asian economies, or multi- 
language countries such as India or Nigeria, where local language nu -
ances significantly impact consumer behavior and market interactions 
( Brannen, Piekkari, & Tietze, 2014 ; Harzing & Feely, 2008 ). For 
example, AI-driven customer service or marketing tools might inade -
quately interpret culturally specific idioms, resulting in ineffective 
marketing campaigns or customer interactions, and consequently 
weakening an MNE’s competitive positioning and brand perception in 
these markets. As a result, AI-augmented decision-making may favor 
more homogeneous markets over more heterogeneous ones.

The central argument of this study is that AI changes how humans 
make decisions. Augmentation, i.e., the combination of human and 
algorithmic abilities, allows human decision-makers to lift and shift 
their boundaries of rationality. By providing humans with access to new, 
unstructured, or unknown datasets and massive computing power, AI 
limits the degree to which humans suffer from incomplete information 
and cognitive limitations. Consequently, augmented decision-makers 
suffer less from bounded rationality in their decisions, rely less on 
heuristics, and their decisions become less distorted by biases. At the 
same time, AI creates human-like and generic machine biases that relate 
to biased data, objectives, benefits, and interactions. In this section, we 
ask how these AI-induced changes to bounded rationality affect IB the -
ories that describe mechanisms that build on learning and decision- 
making under uncertainty.

exhaustive, and there might be additional ways by which AI affects the

ticipants are replaced by “virtual” ones. These new knowledge- 
generation processes will change how MNEs create and sustain 
knowledge-based competitive advantages. (P.11) By making knowledge 
less context-specific, AI expands the applicability of knowledge and 
extends learning to new areas. Yet, this also allows firms to generate a 
knowledge-based advantage by combining context-unspecific AI-based 
knowledge with context-specific “on-the-ground” learning. In sum, AI- 
based augmentation will facilitate learning processes and make them 
more efficient and less biased. However, these changes will also influ -
ence how MNEs create competitive advantages based on sourcing, 
transforming, and transferring knowledge. In conclusion, based on the 
profound effects of AI on learning, we assume that AI changes the re -
sources and capabilities necessary to create and sustain a knowledge- 
based competitive advantage.
Machine biases. The emergence of machine biases is a central impli -
cation of AI-based decision-making ( Table 2 , Panel B). We argue that the 
implications of these biases are not limited to single theories but are 
relevant to IB in general. The implications of “human-like” machine 
biases follow the established lines of human bounded rationality. (P.12) 
Biased training data can lead to algorithms trained on observations that 
are not representative of their field of application. This will result in 
perceptions of foreign countries and decision-making that do not cap -
ture the full complexity of IB. (P.13) Biased objectives can lead to 
simplified objective functions that ignore important boundary condi -
tions and exhibit unintended optimization behavior ( Amodei et al., 
2016 ). Hence, MNEs will use AI to optimize decisions in a way that ig -
nores the complexity of IB. However, AI can also create new “generic” 
biases that are distinct from human boundaries of rationality. Since 
these generic machine biases deviate from human bounded rationality, 
their implications for IB theory are less clear. (P.14) Moreover, the 
benefits of augmented decision-making may differ by application area, 
application form, and the human decision-maker using AI ( Dell’Acqua 
et al., 2023 ; Meincke et al., 2023 ; White et al., 2023 ). This makes it hard 
to understand when AI offers the greatest benefit. Therefore, the impact 
of AI on IB theories may not be fully generalizable to all MNEs and 
situations. (P.15) Biased human-AI interaction, where humans under- or 
overreact to reactions from AI, can be a consequence of this ( Dietvorst 
et al., 2015 ; Tong et al., 2021 ). In extreme cases, human decision-makers 
may even become biased against the recommendations of other humans 
who use AI ( Schweitzer & De Cremer, 2024 ). Thus, the impact of AI on 
decision-making in MNEs depends on how they implement processes 
where AI and humans interact.
Formal and informal institutions. It is essential to keep in mind that the 
implications of AI for decision-making in IB do not unfold in a vacuum. 
Instead, how AI will shape human biases and whether it will generate 
machine biases that nullify any positive effects depends on the institu -
tional envelope in which firms operate. Formal institutions like data- 
governance rules could prevent MNEs from employing AI in some of 
their host countries ( Brouthers et al., 2016 ; Coche et al., 2024 ; Cui et al., 
2014 ). Along the same lines, informal institutions like culture or lan -
guage can also influence the usage of AI for decision-making in IB 
( Brannen et al., 2014 ; Harzing & Feely, 2008 ; Magnusson et al., 2013 ; 
Reiche et al., 2012 ). They might explain why MNEs can successfully 
leverage AI for decision-making in some countries and will fail in others. 
(P.16) Consequently, these formal and informal institutions condition 
the implications of AI-supported decision-making in IB.
In summary, we propose that AI will have fundamental implications 
for theorizing decision-making in MNEs. However, how AI changes this 
theorizing also depends on assumptions about MNEs’ abilities to handle 
machine biases. One way of thinking about the tradeoff between human 
and machine biases in MNEs is to distinguish between automation and 
augmentation of decision-making ( Krakowski et al., 2022 ). Where AI 
suffers little from machine bias and can function close to rationality (e. 
g., in situations with clearly defined rules and unbiased data), managers 
can use AI to, at least partially, automate decisions to limit the negative 
impact of human bias. However, AI will augment human 
decision-making rather than replace it in the face of machine biases. AI 
helps managers shift their boundaries of rationality, and managers 
balance machine biases. Consequently, balancing human and machine 
biases will require hybrid human-AI problem-solving ( Raisch & Fomina, 
2024 ). This tradeoff, or rather, firms’ resources to balance human and 
machine biases in decision-making, will become an important boundary 
condition for IB theories that will require further consideration and 
investigation.

5. Conclusion and avenues for future research
Revolutionary progress in AI and advances in machine learning have 
the potential to fundamentally change how managers in MNEs make 
decisions. Still, despite its efficiency, the integration of AI in decision- 
making presents several firm-level and societal challenges. At the firm 
level, AI ’ s inherent biases, originating from training sets and algorithms 
(e.g., Cowgill & Tucker, 2020 ; Crawford, 2013 ), necessitate a mutual 
de-biasing process with humans to enhance machine learning applica -
tions. Intermediary outcomes that aid human understanding of AI sug -
gestions are crucial for effective human-machine collaboration, 
especially when decisions require human intuition. Additionally, 
replacing diverse human learning with uniform machine learning might 
limit organizational knowledge and worsen learning myopia. Societally, 
ethical dilemmas in AI deployment, like those about security and safety 
in autonomous vehicles ( Gogoll and Müller, 2016 ), require uniform 
ethical standards, and the shift towards AI could reshape job skills, 
particularly in white-collar roles, pushing for rapid acquisition of 
advanced AI competencies ( Kerr and Moloney, 2019 ). Regulatory 
measures, such as those under GDPR, emphasize maintaining human 
oversight in automated systems to ensure accountability and trans -
parency in AI-driven decisions, highlighting the dynamic evolution of 
legal frameworks in response to AI adoption.
Due to its far-reaching implications and challenges, AI will, like other 
technological disruptions before, change how MNEs engage in cross- 
border transactions and make internationalization decisions. Because 
theories of MNE decision-making are rooted in bounded rationality, AI- 
induced changes in the boundaries of rationality will influence how 
MNEs make decisions and how IB theories explain those decisions. 
Building on the theoretical implications above, we suggest a range of 
avenues for future research on the micro and macro levels. IB research 
needs to investigate research questions in these avenues to extend our 
understanding of MNE decision-making.
First, we need to extend our understanding of how AI changes human 
biases in the context of MNEs. While behavioral economics has explored 
a variety of interactions between AI and human biases, few such biases 
have been investigated in contexts relevant to IB research. Given the 
complexity of MNEs and the uncertainty within which decisions in IB are 
made, it remains an open question how much of the behavioral research 
in clean experimental settings translates into MNEs. For example, 
although AI can help overcome overconfidence ( Kliegr et al., 2020 ; 
Senoner et al., 2022 ), the relationship between overconfidence and 
internationalization is more complicated. Overconfidence seems to have 
a non-linear (inverted-U) effect on investment distance ( Li et al., 2023 ), 
and overconfidence leads to higher stakes in international joint ventures 
( Lai, Lin, & Chen, 2017 ). Will AI support similarly change how biases 
influence investment distance and ownership stakes in joint ventures? 
Or is it plausible that the overconfidence that substitutes for experiential 
learning interacts differently with AI than the overconfidence that 
downplays risk?
Second, we do not know how machine biases will influence MNE 
decision-making in an IB context, particularly in the case of generic 
machine biases that deviate from human-like bounded rationality. The 
complexity inherent in IB contexts may make training powerful AI 
models more difficult in IB than in other business contexts. While lan -
guage models for screening CVs can work well in individual contexts, the 
underlying cultural contexts that play important roles in what is desir -
able in a candidate differ across countries. AI-supported HR decisions 
must be more cognizant of machine biases in an international context 
than in a purely domestic one. Similarly, the institutional context for AI 
applications differs ( Coche et al., 2024 ; Rong, Ling, Yang, & Huang, 
2025 ), with a particularly challenging context with GDPR in the Euro -
pean Union, which has been at the core of much IB research. Making AI 
accountable for its decision support ( Busuioc, 2021 ) without 
over-emphasizing the machine biases will be an important challenge.
Third, major IB theories may make different predictions under 
changed boundaries of rationality when humans make decisions with AI 
support. With more information available via automated data collection 
and analysis, experiential learning could be partially replaced by other 
learning types. Still, it is unclear to what extent AI can replace experi -
ential learning in internationalization because a central tenet of expe -
riential learning in internationalization theory is that it needs to happen 
on the ground and include important contextual information, which AI 
will have difficulty absorbing effectively. Will we see faster and more 
distant internationalization in firms that use AI as decision support 
systems? Similarly, lower uncertainty should lead to cheaper trans -
actions via the market instead of hybrid and hierarchical governance 
modes. When information becomes more complete, and boundaries of 
rationality lift and shift to some extent, will we see MNEs opt for more 
market-based governance modes? Alternatively, it could also be the case 
that the specificity in AI-supported decision-making leads to a higher 
expected cost of opportunistic behavior and a positive relationship be -
tween AI adoption and hierarchical governance modes. When informa -
tion becomes easier to absorb, and the institutional context is easier to 
understand, liabilities of foreignness should recede, and firms should 
become quicker at catching up with competitors with knowledge ad -
vantages. But maybe AI will create a positive feedback cycle, with 
adopters learning ever more quickly than non-adopters, leading to a 
change in oligopolistic structures, potentially fewer competitors, and an 
emergence of duopolies where bandwagon effects or tacit collusion 
might lead to a reduction in distant market entries. Which one is it going 
to be?
While we have some insights into AI ’ s linear effects on some biases, 
the complexities of IB make direct predictions in the light of major IB 
theories difficult. As a result, we call for research investigating how AI 
influences MNEs, using all research paradigms and theoretical schools of 
thought. We have outlined a few particularly salient theories when 
MNEs adopt AI for decision support. Most new avenues for research will 
come from interactions between theoretical mechanisms and novel in -
sights into how AI changes and complements human cognition.
