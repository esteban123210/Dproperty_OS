## Lecture synthesis

AI changes both international decision-making and operations. Human bounded rationality creates judgment and present-state biases; augmentation can ease information and processing constraints but introduces biased data, objectives, benefits and interaction. Governance and local data validation remain essential. Digital MNEs, born globals, direct and indirect network effects and complementors challenge assumptions about scaling while Hennart tests what existing theory already explains. Acorai links global ambitions to local validation, capital, stakeholders and institutions. The deck announces an AI guest talk for the following week; its separate slides are absent.

## Page 25

• Managers are bound in their rationality by incomplete 
information and cognitive limitations
• Despite these boundaries, managers have to make decisions
• They use heuristics: simplified decision rules
• Consequently, managers suffer from biases 
• Judgment biases occur when individuals have no idea 
about the correct solution and is rather bound to rely on 
subjective impressions 
• Present-state biases occur when individuals tend to base 
their decisions on the status quo and avoid change
Biased Decision-Making in IB
Lindner, Puck, & Puhr (2025)

## Page 26

• Example: Choosing a new foreign market
• The CEO of a Dutch mid-sized sensor manufacturer has six weeks to choose one new foreign 
market out of 20 candidates (time constraints). Her team cannot evaluate every market on every 
relevant factor (cognitive limitations), and reliable data on some markets, such as Vietnam, is 
outdated or only available in the local language (information availability).
• She still has to decide, so she uses a simple rule of thumb: “Go where we already know how things 
work” (heuristic). She picks the Czech Republic because it “looks like Germany,” where the firm 
is already successful. She expects similar results and bases her sales forecast on the German 
figures (judgment bias). At the same time, the team prefers a market with familiar institutions and 
discounts a report showing much faster demand growth in Vietnam (present-state bias).
• Result: The decision is fast and defensible, but not necessarily optimal. The market with the 
highest potential was never seriously considered.

## Page 30

• Like humans, AI can suffer from boundaries to its rationality
• AI might have been trained on incomplete or non-representative data
• AI might suffer from cognitive limitations due to simplified objectives
• There is also bounded rationality in how humans use AI
• AI does not offer the same benefits to all users
• Humans may mistrust AI or users of AI (or the opposite)
• AI-related biases in decision-making
• Biased data: AI suffers from bias due to bad training data
• Biased objectives: AI suffers from bias due to bad objective functions
• Bias benefits: Human-AI interaction is biased due to unequal benefits
• Biased interaction: Human-AI interaction is biased due to a lack of trust

## Page 31

AI and Decision-Making
• Example: AI-supported supplier selection
• A European sportswear company uses AI to select suppliers in Asia.
• AI expands rationality: The tool scans local news, customs data, and audit reports in several 
languages and estimates each supplier’s risk of delay. Instead of sticking with its familiar supplier 
in China, the company moves orders to a lower-risk supplier in Vietnam.
• But AI has its own boundaries:
• Biased data: Trained mostly on large Chinese suppliers, the AI underrates small suppliers in 
Bangladesh.
• Biased objectives: Told to minimize cost, it recommends a supplier that violates labor 
standards.
• Biased interaction: Veteran buyers ignore its warnings, while junior buyers follow it blindly.
