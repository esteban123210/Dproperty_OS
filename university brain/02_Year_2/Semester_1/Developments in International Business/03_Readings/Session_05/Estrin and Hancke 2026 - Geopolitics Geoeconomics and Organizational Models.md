## Core thesis

Geopolitical blocs and renewed vertical integration can reshape international production without eliminating global trade or value chains.

## Mechanism and evidence

The commentary constructs scenarios around two inward-looking political-economic groupings, weakly aligned countries and blocs such as the EU, and firms vertically reintegrating under technological or political pressure. Home location changes firms strategic options; policy and security considerations may displace narrow comparative advantage in sourcing and location decisions.

## Application and limits

This is scenario-based conceptual analysis, not a prediction that a single two-bloc future must occur. Distinguish changes in the composition of flows from a collapse in their aggregate volume. Compare Altman et al. resilient flows and the Session 5 shift from global efficiency toward security.

How geopolitics, geoeconomics, and new organizational models are

Abstract
We explore the implications for international business of scenarios in which the gradual dealignment between geopolitics 
and geoeconomics has led to the emergence of two inward-looking political-economic groupings. These developments leave 
some countries and blocs, especially the European Union, only weakly aligned. Firms in some sectors are simultaneously 
vertically reintegrating, abandoning elements of global comparative advantage under technological or political pressure. 
International value chains and trade do not disappear as a result, but their shape and effects will change. How an MNE 
approaches these big shifts in the world economy will depend on its home location with implications for national economic 
policy, MNE internationalization, and corporate strategizing.
Keywords Geopolitics · Geoeconomics · International business · Global value chains · Competing blocs · Non-alignment
Setting the scene
As the international business (IB) literature is well aware, 
the global economy is undergoing major change (see e.g., 
Petricevic & Teece, 2019; Witt, 2019; Beugelsdijk & Luo, 
2024). This has led scholars to consider the impact of the rise 
of populism and nationalism as the basis of increased protec-
tionism and restrictions on inward foreign direct investment 
(FDI) (e.g., Hartwell et al., 2024; Luo, 2021). There has also 
been particular concern about the decoupling of the world’s 
two largest economies, China and the United States (US), 
and the rise of techno-nationalism (e.g., Cui et al., 2023 ; 
Witt et al., 2023), and the increased ‘weaponization’ of ele-
ments of inter-state economic relations to achieve national 
objectives has become increasingly common, for example, 
through politically motivated tariffs, constraints on FDI, and 
sanctions (Li et al., 2024; Meyer et al., 2023; Sethuram & 
Gaur, 2024; Teece, 2025). All these factors are changing the 
political and business environment for multinational enter -
prises (MNEs) to one in which political constraints increas-
ingly dominate economic considerations. As a result, many 
of the processes that sustained and made MNEs successful 
are likely to be questioned in a world of more inward-look-
ing blocs and enhanced vertical integration. In this essay, 
we develop a set of related scenarios that help us to think 
through the changing political and technological arrange-
ments before going on to analyze their implications for MNE 
internationalization and corporate strategies.

Geopolitics, geoeconomics, and new 
organizational models
Our premise is that we seem to be witnessing a retreat from 
globalization, at least as we have known it.1 Two big politi-
cal-economic shifts in particular, with significant repercus-
sions for business, attract our attention. The first is a realign-
ment of geopolitical and geoeconomic alliances in a world 
economy driven more by sweeping political and ideological 
considerations than narrow business motives. The second is 
a slow pendulum shift away from thin, long, and complex 
global value chains towards a world of more integrated eco-
nomic activities – driven by a combination of geopolitical 
tensions and the uncertainties associated with the digital and 
green transitions. Whilst the former has been examined quite 
extensively in the IB literature (e.g., recently, Contractor 
et al., 2026), the latter has received less attention (but see 
Gereffi et al., 2025). We examine the logic of each and their 
key empirical manifestations.
Much of this is ongoing as we write, and this exercise 
therefore requires a combination of expertise and imagina-
tion. It shares methodological similarities with paleontolo-
gists building a skeleton from bone fragments: facts are, at 
best, only emerging, but our broader understanding of IB 
and international political economy (IPE) allows us to draw 
inferences, though with the obvious caveats. We also hold 
everything else constant. Unless we have clear indications 
about their direct effects on the processes we discuss, we 
will ignore large-scale universal developments like artificial 
intelligence (AI) and other aspects of the digital economy 
and the green transition. We think that their effects are still 
too uncertain to be included and would make an already 
complex issue even more intractable.
The upshot of our discussion is simple: if these two trends 
in IB and IPE persist, businesses are likely to face a future 
that is fundamentally different in all aspects, from tech-
nology and innovation through skills and employment to 
product markets and trade patterns. How can business and 
policymakers, particularly in Europe, understand and adjust 
to such big shifts?
The realignment of geopolitics and geoeconomics
The first process goes back some way (see e.g., Witt, 2019; 
Vertinsky et al., 2023). Over at least the last two decades, we 
have witnessed a gradual dealignment between geopolitics, 
i.e., the influence of geographic ties on power and strategy, 
and geoeconomics, the ability to use trade and macro-eco-
nomics to further geopolitical goals (see e.g., Blackwill & 
Harris, 2016; Clayton et al., 2023). The process dates at 
least to the Obama presidency, which shifted US strategic 
focus from Europe to the Pacific Rim in response to Chi-
na’s dramatic economic rise in the 2000s and 2010s. It was 
accelerated by Trump’s populist America First agenda and 
especially Biden’s Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), reintro-
ducing forms of economic nationalism (Luo & Van Assche, 
2023), culminating in Trump’s recent aggressive trade and 
economic policies. These policies have been paralleled by 
China’s Made in China 2025 industrial strategy of 2015 and 
its underlying Dual Circulation model (Lin & Wang, 2022) 
adopted in 2020, which focuses growth on ‘internal circula-
tion’, while ‘external circulation’ (foreign trade and FDI) is 
relegated to a supporting role. The Chinese growth model 
ultimately involves self-reliance in military, technology, and 
energy production, though systemic overproduction also pro-
vides strong incentives to export.2
Political and military alliances, which used to overlap 
significantly with trade and other forms of economic coop-
eration, have also become more calculated and transactional 
as a result, leaving open the possibilities of geopolitical and 
geoeconomic arrangements à la carte rather than as a fixed 
menu. While NATO, OECD-EU, and the North Atlantic 
part of the Bretton Woods agreement were largely the same 
geographic entity during the Cold War, that is no longer self-
evidently the case. The inward turn of the two hegemonic 
powers, and especially the aggressive recent moves by the 
US concerning the European Union (EU) and Canada, have 
unsurprisingly accelerated the process of political-economic 
disintegration. The impact is particularly keenly felt in 
Europe, with the Russian war in Ukraine and the US threat 
to take over Greenland. So far, this has led to some strong 
responses from the EU and increasing calls from European 
leaders such as Macron, Merz, and Draghi for more Euro -
pean economic and military self-determination3. The discus-

As a result of these developments, two relatively inward-
looking blocs around the two hegemons are emerging, with 
reconfigured links between business and politics in each of 
them (Cass, 2025; Posen, 2025). These are the US and its 
current close allies, on one side, versus China and its allies 
on the other (see e.g., Shearing, 2025). While there is no 
consensus on the exact membership of each bloc (see e.g., 
Goes & Bekkers, 2022; Gopinath et al., 2022), and alliances 
are shifting fast, the Chinese bloc probably includes North 
Korea, Russia and perhaps Iran, as well as other countries 
linked through the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) (Flint & 
Zhu, 2019). On the other side, the US bloc contains at a 
minimum, Japan, South Korea, most of Central and South 
America4 (and possibly Canada in a post-Trump world).
Many observers also include the EU 5 in the US bloc 
but recent tensions surrounding trade, AI regulation, US 
national security, the war following the Russian invasion 
of Ukraine and Trump’s attempt to militarize Greenland 
question this assumption. In the short run, the EU’s rela-
tive military weakness, and especially its complex decision-
making processes, will probably forestall a total rupture with 
the US (NATO remains important as long as Putin resides 
in the Kremlin). However, EU member states are currently 
also actively discussing how a smaller group of countries 
could combine forces and set up a military and economic 
alliance under a much looser NATO umbrella with other 
nations searching for political-economic alternatives to the 
US–China duopoly. In sum, because the process of disentan-
glement preceded Trump in the US, and the EU is undergo-
ing a complex process of internal realignment, we find it 
hard to imagine the Western bloc could re-emerge intact 
even if more moderate political forces prevail in Washing-
ton. We therefore treat these tensions as a new constraint. 
Similarly, despite its rising global economic importance, 
India is another significant economic and political actor that 
has complicated relations with both emerging superpowers, 
reflected in its current ‘strategic autonomy’ positioning (see 
e.g., Monnappa, 2025).
As a result, the form of globalization in which MNE 
decisions are almost solely made on the basis of economic 
costs and benefits, which has defined the last four decades 
(Baldwin, 2016), is increasingly coming under pressure. The 
wider context for businesses in terms of free trade, outward 
FDI, joint and global regulation, and stable multilateral 
arrangements is, after decades of unimpeded growth, slow -
ing and even retreating in a world in which national jurisdic-
tions reign supreme. Global trade, international value chains, 
and Great Power politics are not disappearing in that new 
world, but their shape and effects are changing. 6 What this 
means for MNEs, as we will explain below, depends mainly 
on where the firms are located.
That, then, begs two questions: what, in such a bipo-
lar world, should be the policies of countries that are not 
fully aligned with either bloc, and how will these affect 
their MNEs? Despite its current urgency, this is far from 
a new question. After 1945, the world was economically 
divided between internally integrated but globally distinct 
and mutually almost exclusive Western and Soviet (CMEA) 
trade blocs (Eichengreen, 2019; Ellman, 2014; Grossman, 
1974), underpinned by military alliances (NATO and War -
saw Pact). Some countries, notably India and the former 
Yugoslavia, sought even then not to be politically or eco-
nomically aligned, a strategy that became increasingly and 
inherently difficult, with full-on losses or full-on gains as a 
result, but little in the middle (Berger et al., 2013).
History is not simply repeating itself, however, with 
China replacing the erstwhile Soviet Union. Combined, the 
US and the Soviet Union represented a somewhat smaller 
share of estimated global gross domestic product (GDP) 
in 1950 than the US and China do today: in 1950 the US 
and the Soviet Union accounted for around 36% of global 
GDP, while the US and China represented more than 42% 
in 2020.7 The main non-aligned countries in 1961 were rela-
tively small global players, with a combined share in global 
GDP around 3% (Maddison, 2007; WDI data). In contrast, 
the EU, the main economic bloc which is increasingly not 
aligned with either the US or China, accounted for around 
18% of global GDP in 2020, while India, not clearly aligned 
either, ranked as the third largest economy in the world in 
PPP terms and had a global share of over 3%. Thus, while 
the combined economic muscle of the two blocs is slightly 
larger, so is the potential heft of those countries not nec -
essarily fully aligned with either. As smaller powers have 
already noticed, the two contemporary hegemons are there-
fore in a better position to enforce their geopolitical wishes 
than their Cold War predecessors. However, because of the 
slow dealignment of the EU and India, which may attract

The character of the two superpowers is another impor -
tant point of variation. During the Cold War, the US built 
an integrated trade system based on rules and norms embed-
ded in the Bretton Woods regime (Eichengreen, 2019) and 
worked with its allies to support mutual economic growth 
(Ikenberry, 2005). At the same time, the Soviet Union built 
a trading system – the Council for Mutual Economic Assis-
tance (CMEA) – in which member states were effectively 
treated as specialized regions within a single planned eco-
nomic entity. Trade and FDI between the two blocs were 
minimal (apart from critical natural resources), not least 
because the CMEA explicitly aimed for internal autarchy. 
Today, both superpowers are more open for business but 
also highly nationalistic and transactional in their approach 
to economic and trade relationships (see Posen, 2025). Ber-
toldi and Buti (2025: 6), for example, argue that the US 
has become an ‘extractive’ superpower since Trump’s sec-

adversaries and allies alike through the use – and abuse – of 
the political, economic, diplomatic, and military tools at 
its disposal’. In contrast, they see China as a ‘dependency’ 
superpower, using exports, control over key supply chains 
and critical materials like rare earths, and the provision of 
credit and FDI to build dependency and goodwill among its 
economic partners. Other analysts may use different terms 
but characterize the developments in a similar way (e.g., 
Krugman, 2024; Luo & Van Assche, 2023 ; Mitter, 2025 ; 
Posen, 2025).
Whither comparative advantage?
Alongside these global political-economic realignments, 
micro-level changes are taking place as well. In many new 
sectors, businesses are uncertain about their technological 
trajectories, product market strategies, the regulatory envi-
ronment, and trade policies. Firms struggle with questions 
such as: will AI be the aggregate steamrolling force it is 
implied to be or will many lose and few gain in the new 
digital age? Will electric vehicles (EVs) become important 
products in their own right and thus pull other industries 
along, or never quite regain the promising momentum from 
a decade ago? And will digital infrastructures be part of the 
new economic backbone, or will their demand for energy 
and the perceived dangers of concentration impose a plateau 
on their development?
Against this background of deep technological, economic, 
and policy uncertainty, businesses are increasingly recon-
sidering their basic organizational models. Richard Bald-
win (2016) provided the definitive analysis of how global 
production arrangements, fed by technology and compara-
tive advantage, spanned the globe in a process of what he 
called ‘fragmentation’, in which separate jurisdictions were 
scouted for their fit with a particular stage in the value chain. 
These globe-spanning, border-crossing arrangements are 
increasingly under pressure today. At least two logics of 
reorganization seem to be unfolding – we think of these as 
conceptual ideal types that require more work before empiri-
cal testing.
In traditional sectors, these processes often take the shape 
of (debates about) relocations, including of systematically 
important suppliers, or friendshoring, often as part of a 
strategy of increased autonomy from one of the superpow -
ers (see, e.g., Van Assche & Narula, 2023). Companies in 
many jurisdictions will also increasingly face direct eco-
nomic pressures to shorten their supply chains: more intru-
sive carbon accounting and parallel regulations will force 
MNEs to reduce greenhouse gas emissions upstream in the 
value chain and curtail the currently relatively inexpensive 
but highly polluting transport of parts and finished products 
across the globe.8 Finally, political concerns about industrial 
restructuring associated with the digital and green transi-
tions are also likely to lead to a repatriation of activities 
to stabilize local economic activity including employment. 
Obviously, in older sectors, where employment cost factors 
dominate, changes may be more modest but probably never 
quite zero.
In many of the new leading industrial sectors – such 
as EVs, AI and digital equipment, and green energy and 
technology – the underlying organizational models already 
often eschew fragmented supply systems. Importantly, 
these industries are poised to generate the bulk of innova-
tion, growth, jobs, and investment in the future. Instead of 
orchestrating their value chains along the principles of many 
suppliers with low inventories and few buffers, the largest 
companies in these sectors are searching for ways to ver -
tically (re-)integrate to reduce uncertainty and maximize 
control over intellectual property and strategic processes. 
Some of this reconfiguration may have started before the 
COVID-19 pandemic, but the supply shocks associated 
with the pandemic unequivocally drove home the fragility 
of such long, thin supply chains (Baldwin & Freeman, 2022; 
Vertinsky et al., 2023). In newer sectors, where technolo-
gies and markets remain deeply uncertain, companies also 
seem to benefit from much greater control over the entire 
process – at least until both technology and markets have 
stabilized (Cui et al., 2022). However, such ‘settling down’ 
may take some time since technological change faces ever 
shorter cycles. Take the example of BYD, the world’s largest

Significant vertical reintegration is also underpinned by 
a cold monetary calculus (see Philippon, 2019). It allows 
businesses to reap two types of monopoly profits that would 
not easily be available under the old logic of fragmentation. 
If every step in the value chain is uncertain, it is potentially 
a source of massive profits. Batteries, for example, make up 
roughly half the value of an EV and software a large part 
of the remainder; controlling those steps in the value chain 
has always been a lucrative idea and reintegrating them into 
existing processes is therefore the logical next step. How -
ever, vertical reintegration also reinforces the profits associ-
ated with the interconnection of these steps. Since batteries 
and software (the latter also to steer the former) make up a 
large part of the value of an EV, controlling both, directly 
through ownership or indirectly through quasi-exclusive 
joint ventures, promises more than the separate gains of 
each. It creates a systemic lock-in of customers – a tactic 
popularized by many of the robber barons over a hundred 
years ago, updated by Apple in the 1980s, and reinvented by 
the remaining digital giants (including Apple) today. That 
helps understand why Apple, OpenAI, Intel, and Nvidia are 
investing vast sums in each other and why Meta and others 
are keen on acquiring the talents and intellectual property 
of another company (but not the company itself; cf. Boyle, 
2025). Whatever the exact shape of the vertical reintegra-
tion, the outcome, and arguably the motivation, is always the 
same: cornering markets through multi-layered technologi-
cal lock-ins in an attempt to hedge against the sometimes 
extreme technological, policy, and economic uncertainty.
How macro‑politics and micro‑economics interact
These two processes – geopolitical realignment and 
increased reliance on vertical integration – have not hap-
pened at the same time by chance. The macro-political and 
the micro-economic shifts share an underlying worldview 
that questions the benefits of traditional globalization. The 
emergence of new political-economic alliances in a (likely) 
bi-polar world will reintroduce obstacles to international 
business by way of tariffs, prohibition of trade with some 
nations or other forms of restraint and retaliation. It is also 
implied in abandoning the logic of global value chains that 
were built on comparative advantages (Baldwin, 2016; Hall 
& Soskice, 2001), while explicit in the regional reconcen-
tration of industrial activities, the enforcement of tighter 
local content rules, and regulatory control over different 
aspects of digital services. Finally, once in motion, either of 
these processes reinforces a retreat from globalization in the 
other. Tighter political control over trade means that com-
panies are forced to focus on the possibilities of local value 
chains; in turn, the dominance of local value chains, often 
supported by industrial policy measures, opens the door to 
more aggressive international trade and economic policies. 
Since Orbán, Erdoğan, and Trump, governments have redis-
covered their power over business decisions – from media 
over manufacturing to digital services – through regulation, 
immigration policies and subsidies. They can influence loca-
tion decisions, staffing levels, product market strategies, and 
oversight, ownership, and capital structures to support their 
policies. The emerging global political-economic bipolarity 
will therefore necessarily include a recentering of economic 
activity within friendly jurisdictions – in the limiting case, 
within the national boundaries. If economics led politics in 
the age of globalization, politics trumps economics in this 
new era of geopolitical polarization.
What does this mean for business?
Going forward, IB strategies will become significantly more 
contingent on the policies and politics of the bloc to which 
the firm’s home country is aligned as a result – including as 
a group the non-aligned countries – and of the bloc in which 
the host country is located (Beugelsdijk & Luo, 2024; Bod-
dewyn & Brewer, 1994). We denote the China bloc by C, the 
US bloc by US, and the non-aligned countries by NA below 
to characterize three distinctive environments for MNEs to 
pursue their internationalization strategies.
Intra‑bloc internationalization (C‑C; US‑US)
For MNEs within each of the two blocs, it will look much 
like IB as usual within the bloc, though with much more con-
strained horizons outside the bloc. As long as they operate 
within the confines of their own bloc, their strategic options 
for internationalization remain similar to those previously 
available in a more extensively globalized world. Moreover, 
since each bloc represents a large proportion of the world 
economy, MNEs retain considerable international scope for

their strategic choices. They will probably be able to invest 
or export relatively freely within the bloc, while firms from 
the smaller countries in the bloc may be strongly encour -
aged and incentivized to do so. That is what we have seen 
recently with announcements of large Korean and Japanese 
FDI into the US. Value and supply chains are also likely to 
follow this political reconfiguration of the relevant economic 
geographies, with further regional concentration (Gereffi, 
2020). This may ultimately force MNEs within the bloc to 
patriate activities into a single national economy, for exam-
ple from Taiwan or South Korea into the US. All else equal, 
these processes are likely to lead to stabler employment and 
higher wages (and prices) within each of the blocs, but also 
to lower average levels of living standards and productivity 
because of reduced international business and competition.
Inter‑bloc internationalization (C‑US; US‑C)
Because of these political restrictions, which raise costs and 
increase risk, global value chains that span blocs are likely 
to wither (Contractor et al., 2026). Even so, in contrast to 
the situation during the Cold War, firms supplying non-
strategic goods, especially low technology labor-intensive 
manufactured products, may still be able to trade and pro-
duce fairly freely, if only on a short-term and transactional 
basis. The restrictions will be more binding, however, for 
MNEs in sectors deemed ‘strategic’. In fact, given the high 
levels of interdependency, including between rivaling blocs, 
technologically advanced sectors may also receive special 
dispensation for trade if it can be used as leverage: China 
and the US may trade access to advanced (though probably 
previous generation) semiconductors for access to equivalent 
hardware, for example.
At the same time, each bloc will probably try to develop 
its own supply chains of critical resources and other core 
components internally – as China has already done for rare 
earths and the US envisaged under Biden’s industrial pol -
icy initiative – and encourage its MNEs to rely primarily 
on those. It will be particularly important to see whether 
each bloc develops its own payment system, or continues to 
rely, as now, on the dollar, giving the US a strategic global 
advantage (Eichengreen, 2019). Much of this will resemble 
aspects of the trade arrangements during the height of the 
Cold War, as we suggested earlier. Economic effects beyond 
the sectors immediately involved are probably relatively 
marginal. Since MNEs will trade, almost by design, either 
(politically allowed) semi-commoditized or (almost unique) 
highly specific products, they will likely face extreme com-
petition in the first case and enjoy monopoly rents in the 
latter. In sum, most FDI and much trade between blocs will 
likely be limited by tariffs, controls, exclusions, and even 
sanctions, especially in more technologically advanced or 
militarily sensitive sectors. MNE decisions will have to 
operate within these constraints.
Relations between the blocs and non‑aligned 
countries
This third constellation offers perhaps the most interesting 
feature of the emerging new world order. The incentives for 
the blocs and the NAs do not entirely align. In terms of 
internationalization, each bloc will want to encourage its 
MNEs to export to NAs, especially perhaps China because 
of the economy’s strong incentives for over-production of 
manufactured goods; the benefits from FDI, however, which 
can be seen as reducing domestic employment, are less obvi-
ous. At the same time, companies based in NAs have equally 
strong incentives to exploit the large markets within each 
bloc, subject to possible import-substitution strategies that 
Beijing and Washington accept.
Each bloc also has strong political incentives to persuade 
non-aligned countries to join them, to increase their global 
heft – though these incentives may also conflict with the ten-
dencies towards techno-nationalism and populism at the root 
of the changes to the world economic order (Posen, 2025).9 
Moreover, there may be concerns that large NA’s (or large 
NA blocs, like the EU) might pose a competitive threat in 
the future. For the NAs, the crucial question that arises is to 
what extent independence from one bloc or the other is an 
optimal or even a feasible strategy.10 As we will see below, 
this is a major concern for the EU. This question is partly an 
issue of whether the markets that remain open to them are 
sufficient for scale of production and supply. Perhaps equally 
relevant is the feasibility and reliability of supplying some 
goods and services if critical elements of their global supply 
chain derive primarily from within the C or US blocs. The 
supply of technology highlights perhaps even deeper issues. 
Between them, the US and China have a far greater control 
over global technology, indicated for example by the share 
of patents, than their share of global GDP: in 2023 they 
applied for 64% of global patents (WIPO, 2024). Including 
Japan and South Korea in the US bloc raises the share of 
the two blocs in world patent applications to almost 80%. 
The US also punches significantly above its weight in global 
capital markets, with more than 40% of global capital flows,

Nonetheless, if these constraints can be overcome, NA 
status may be attractive, at least for larger economies and 
MNEs within them (Bhaumik et al., 2025). NAs with large 
domestic markets and internationally competitive compa-

Indeed, NA governments can play the two blocs off against 
each other, extracting benefits in terms of reduced tariffs 
and better terms for FDI for their companies in return for 
internal market access. Provided these beneficial arrange-
ments can be negotiated, NA firms therefore have the best 
of both worlds, being able to produce and sell pretty much 
anywhere as well as in their home market, a rather similar 
situation to the recent past – even though geopolitical insta-
bility and climate costs may still lead them to localize their 
supply chains. Their firms can also gain economies of scale 
by selling to both blocs simultaneously, unlike the situations 
for MNEs based within one or the other bloc, whose markets 
are likely to be more limited.
This would lead to the paradoxical outcome that outsiders 
may be better off within each bloc than domestic companies 
that toe the line. While that is certainly something most in-
bloc strategists would prefer to avoid, it is not difficult to 
imagine some scenarios with such asymmetric outcomes. 
One would be that NA holds a specific asset that neither C 
nor US controls. The Dutch company ASML, for example, 
has exclusive control over extreme UV lithography technol-
ogy at the basis of the most sophisticated chip fabs. While 
this leverage is currently only sparingly utilized toward 
China for the latest-generation equipment, nothing (besides 
US part-ownership of the company, perhaps) stands in the 
way. However, NA countries, especially the EU, have other 
potential points where they can exercise strategic leverage: 
the largest and most advanced consumer market in the world, 
extraterritorial effects of in-bloc regulation (the DMA and 
related initiatives), or access to health data for pharmaceu-
tical research. To examine different possible outcomes, we 
distinguish below between trade and investment flows into 
and out of the two blocs to examine the different scenarios 
for business.
Blocs and non‑aligned countries (C‑NA; US‑NA)
Non-aligned countries would remain attractive locations, 
especially for trade but also investment by firms in both 
blocs. One could imagine MNEs playing a role in bridging 
between them and facilitating, through their FDI subsidiar -
ies in a non-aligned location, exports of goods and services 
from one bloc to another. NA countries would likely adopt 
largely free trade policies of the sort that pertained globally 
until recently, which would further encourage engagement 
with both blocs. However, populist political pressures seem 
likely to continue to limit external activities for all US firms, 
notably those in ‘strategic’ sectors, especially outside the 
bloc. At the same time, and despite pressures to export, trade 
has a lower status within China’s dual circulation model. 
Even so, Apple’s example of relocating iPhone production 
suggests that non-aligned India stands to benefit from decou-
pling between China and the US.
Non‑aligned countries and blocs (NA‑C; NA‑US)
The blocs would remain attractive locations for trade and 
perhaps especially FDI by MNEs based in NA countries, 
because of their economic size and technological sophisti-
cation: market and strategic motives would likely predomi-
nate. However, the extent of trade and FDI would ultimately 
depend on political factors. With China explicitly and the 
US to a more limited extent adopting a perspective of eco-
nomic nationalism, inter-bloc FDI or trade would be much 
more subject to politically motivated interventions than 
comparable internationalization within a bloc. While the 
intensity of the economic nationalism shifts in both blocs 
(but more or less averages out the same despite its differ -
ences), it is not that important for our purposes. The shift in 
economic ideology introduces a discontinuity that changes 
the context for business strategies, as we argue below. For 
example, as we are beginning to see in the US, exports from 
NAs are increasingly subject to tariffs. Moreover, there is 
increasing scrutiny of FDI through acquisition, especially 
in technologically advanced sectors, and this seems likely to 
grow further. Whatever the ultimate shape, while business 
relationships between NAs and the US and C blocs may be 
relatively smooth, the old economic order will not return.
Implications for international business
Two decades ago, Thomas Friedman introduced the idea 
of a flat world (Friedman, 2005): business could choose an 
investment location almost anywhere purely based on eco-
nomic considerations. In the new global economic order, 
however, the world is no longer flat, or even nearly flat, but 
very bumpy. To make sense of this discontinuous world, 
consider the notion of distance, a central IB construct that 
is fundamentally affected by the changing world order 
(Beugeldijk & Luo, 2024). Think of distance as the differ -
ences from an MNE perspective between the costs and ben-
efits of operating in a host country rather than the home loca-
tion. While the concept of distance operates along several 
dimensions simultaneously (Ghemawat, 2007) – geography, 
institutions, or economics – each of these could in principle 
be measured along a monotonic scale and aggregated into 

## Page 8

261Journal of International Business Policy (2026) 9:254–266 
a single risk-adjusted measure to guide location choices. 
Behind such an analysis was the view that economic con-
siderations drove political outcomes; corporate decision-
making was therefore driven by balancing purely material 
benefits against costs while political actors played a role 
only indirectly as part of the evaluation of political ‘risk’ 
(Contractor et al., 2026).
However, this changes when the world is divided into two 
blocs and a group of NA countries: the quasi-continuous dis-
tribution of opportunities that characterized the flat world is 
morphing into several discrete, only marginally overlapping 
sets. In this new world order, discontinuities based on the 
political alignment of nations disrupt the continuous meas-
ures of distance. Standard conceptions of distance, such as 
geography, will remain an important indicator of barriers to 
international business for countries within a bloc. However, 
when political factors displace economic and business moti-
vations in the new world order, distance measures between 
two countries in different blocs will instead effectively 
become infinite. The home country’s relationship to each of 
the two hegemonic blocs – its alignment with or opposition 
to one bloc or its non-alignment to both – will define the 
measure of distance that each MNE must calculate for each 
different host location. Some countries will be close (within 
the bloc), some more distant (NAs) and some effectively 
too distant to allow significant trade or investment activ -
ity, whatever the other indicators of distance might suggest. 
The impact of political alignment on international business 
strategy will also vary according to the sector in which the 
MNE operates. It will likely be much greater in strategically 
sensitive sectors, including for new technologies and some 
key resource inputs (Baldwin et al., 2025), but relatively 
unaffected in traditional industries.
As we implied above, this idea of discontinuous distance 
also applied during the Cold War. One of its stranger fea-
tures was that, for firms in East Germany, trade to or invest-
ment in the rest of what had been Germany before 1945, 
i.e., West Germany, was for the most part impossible. At the 
same time, very distant places along traditional geographic 
measures, like Angola, Cuba, or Kazakhstan, became impor-
tant locations for East German internationalization. In a par-
allel way, in the new global order, the simple mechanics of 
calculating the various elements of distance to make loca-
tion choices of exports or FDI are dominated instead by the 
question of whether locations are on the correct side of the 
emerging discontinuity.
Distance in this sense has one important iterative conse-
quence for ‘real’ economic activity, increasing internaliza-
tion within both the firm and the bloc. Reincorporating pre-
viously outsourced activities will also lead to more economic 
integration within the blocs (see e.g., Baldwin & Freeman, 
2022; Evenett & Pisani, 2023). While firms are likely to keep 
the most important and technologically advanced elements 
of their value chains close to home within the blocs, the less 
strategic elements are likely to be relocated to the periphery 
of the bloc or to NAs. This has already been an important 
strategy in the car industry since the 1980s, with the US 
automotive industry and the Mexican suppliers, German car 
manufacturers and Central European suppliers and assem-
bly plants, and Japanese auto firms and their subcontrac-
tors across East Asia. We expect these core-periphery links 
within each bloc to gain in importance while inter-bloc sup-
ply chain links weaken, at least to the extent that substitutes 
can be found or developed in response to bottlenecks. The 
responses of firms to the new global political economy thus 
also produce sharper boundaries of those political economic 
blocs themselves.
What does this mean for MNEs?
Many of the implications of the new global economic order 
for MNEs depend on the policies of their home economies 
in terms of alignment with one or the other bloc. If they are 
based within a bloc, MNEs will be able to operate interna-
tionally freely within that bloc – and less so within non-
aligned countries – but very little if at all into the other bloc. 
If they are based in a NA, however, they may be able to 
operate relatively freely on a global basis, perhaps with some 
additional costs, but, unlike firms from competing blocs, 
not facing a complete trade barrier. The impact of this form 
of deglobalization will, therefore, mainly be felt by MNEs 
based within the dominant competing blocs, especially those 
in strategically significant sectors. Of course, some of this 
has already been true for a while in high-tech industries. 
Thus, China has for decades operated behind the Great Fire-
wall, with the result that US tech firms like Alphabet, Ama-
zon and Meta have their Chinese counterparts in Alibaba, 
Baidu and Tencent. They do not usually compete directly 
in each other’s home markets, but competition is fierce in 
other countries.
For these MNEs within a bloc for whom a shrinking IB 
context is a concern, an interesting question arises as to 
whether the firm might attempt to change its home loca-
tion. One possibility would be to relocate, for example from 
a peripheral country within a bloc to the core of the bloc 
– a notion that sheds some light on TSMC’s strategies in 
the face of Chinese threats to take over Taiwan. Another 
might be to relocate from one bloc to a non-aligned coun-
try to increase the options for trade and investment, though 
obviously with the potential loss of valuable benefits such 
as industrial policy aid, other subsidies and favorable trade 
terms. A third possibility might be to shift from a non-
aligned location to a base in a bloc, for example, to obtain 
access to critical minerals or to technologies only available 
for firms located within the bloc. Finally, a firm might try

262 Journal of International Business Policy (2026) 9:254–266
to move from one bloc to another to keep its foothold in 
a remunerative market: this might be part of what we are 
seeing with recent developments in the US part of TikTok.
Supply chains are likely to follow suit in this new world. 
As we explained earlier, inward-looking blocs beget shorter 
supply chains and vice versa. Factoring in the likely increase 
in non-tariff trade barriers like carbon emissions taxes to 
compensate for lower environmental standards, regulation of 
new digital activities, and general (social) dumping clauses 
in trade and investment agreements, there are many political 
and economic considerations that lead to quasi-internaliza-
tion and vertical reintegration. In contrast to the last four 
decades, which saw a turn towards core competencies and a 
global geographic spread of value chains, especially in firms 
using the new technologies, we are entering a new world 
of less lean companies, which control many more aspects 
of their production more directly and in a spatially more 
concentrated way.
The new world order is, therefore, not an unambiguously 
bad thing for all MNEs. There are obvious losses, such as 
the straight-up economic benefits of vertical disintegration 
based on comparative advantage, and the MNEs may lose 
out on markets, investment possibilities, and even access to 
technologies, depending on the bloc where they are located. 
The objectives of firms and states may diverge as a result, 
especially for technology firms located in NAs. However, 
in other ways, MNEs stand to gain a lot. Especially for 
firms within each bloc, reduced access by major competi-
tors to their home or primary overseas markets enhances 
their monopoly power, thus increasing their ability to charge 
higher prices. Moreover, since active industrial policy 
seems to be the handmaiden of economic nationalism, each 
hegemon might use political pressures as well as financial 
support to enhance the market position and profitability of 
its leading MNEs within their bloc and more widely – what 
we are seeing today with the US and its tech companies.
Implications for international business 
policy
This brings us to government policy. As we have seen, the 
new techno-nationalism implies much greater support for 
firms through industrial policy, a tendency already apparent 
in both the US (Van Assche & Luo, 2024) and China (Gar -
cia-Macia et al., 2025; Wang & Kroeber, 2025), and with 
powerful allies in Europe (McNamara, 2024). Thus, non-
market activities may yield greater rewards than previously 
(Doh et al., 2012). In this more politicized business environ-
ment, governments will also need to put increased effort into 
ensuring alignment between businesses that fall in line with 
their political objectives. Further, governments will be aware 
of the risks of being left behind and the consequences of 
underinvestment in the necessary social, technological and 
institutional infrastructure to reduce uncertainty.
In particular, the changing political environment, coupled 
with shorter technological cycles, significantly increases the 
reliance on public goods. If the future is uncertain, we need 
general (instead of specific) skills, multiple quasi-experi -
mental innovation models, and robust but flexible institu-
tions that can adapt to new developments. Individual com-
panies, as a rule, do not invest in these public goods, but they 
will be crucial when markets open: training workers, build-
ing new infrastructure or developing public policies after 
the opportunities have arisen is almost certainly too late. In 
short, without strong and capable government, willing to 
take on the roles of information clearing house, negotiating 
platform and provider of public goods, not unlike what the 
French state did in the heyday of indicative planning from 
the 1950s to the 1980s (Estrin & Holmes, 2026), businesses 
will be left on their own in a world that is hard to navigate 
without an exoskeleton of public goods and services.
Finally, as blocs turn inward, businesses will also require 
more stable regulation and policy in their home economies 
(effectively a first-order public good). The green transition 
in energy and cars is stalling today because of the policy 
oscillations over deadlines and processes. The value of semi-
stranded green assets in the global car industry – think of 
these as investments that exist or are committed but yield a 
considerably lower return than anticipated – is, according 
to former Stellantis CEO Tavares, estimated to be close to 
$1trn and rising (FT 28 Oct 2025). In 2024, the energy com-
pany Ørsted abandoned a wind power park in the North Sea 
because shifts in regulation meant it could no longer profit-
ably produce wind energy.11 Since the start of US President 
Trump’s second mandate, green energy providers and green 
tech firms have faced significant drops in their share value. 
These examples can easily be extended across all sectors 
and inevitably lead to the same conclusion: if policy flips, 
business suffers. Business can adapt to stringent regula-
tions – especially in Europe with its strict food standards, 
REACH rules for the chemical industry, precautionary medi-
cal assessment, and strict labor regulations – because com-
panies know how to optimize strategies under those rules. 
However, business cannot develop strategies if the rules, and 
therefore the payoffs, are frequently revised. Put differently, 
policy uncertainty, especially when combined with techno-
logical, political and economic uncertainty, kills Keynes’ 
animal spirits, and the greater the influence over MNEs in a 
world in which the scope for international expansion is more 
restricted, the more pronounced this effect will be.

What does this mean for Europe?
Where do these likely changes to the world economic order 
leave the largest and most significant potentially non-aligned 
bloc, the EU, and firms within it? In principle the EU could 
gradually become non-aligned and reap the economic ben-
efits from that position. However, to what extent is this 
realizable? The EU has many potential assets but a prob -
lematic decision-making structure that impedes moves into 
a more active, offensive pattern. Draghi (2024), for exam-
ple, may have diagnosed some of Europe’s problems in his 
assessment of the continent’s competitiveness, but short 
of abandoning EU rules – impossible without difficult and 
long-winded Treaty revisions – his recommendations are, 
technically, utopian. In fact, Europe’s founding logic of ‘neg-
ative integration’ (the removal of barriers; Scharpf, 1999) 
almost precludes a creative response that requires compe-
tency transfer to the center. Single European Market (SEM) 
and Economic and Monetary Union rules, which effectively 
prohibit activist government policies, make a coordinated, 
offensive answer to the new global order very difficult. 
Merger rules stop European companies from growing to a 
scale that is competitive globally, while EMU’s disinflation-
ary bias and fiscal constraints preclude significant public 
investment.12 For the foreseeable future, therefore, and until 
the EU changes its decision-making structures, the relative 
pace of the EU, which reflects its byzantine, self-restraining 
policy set-up, is not going to change much.
If the world is increasingly splitting into two relatively 
stable blocs, the EU is therefore likely to remain a bystander 
– large enough to create problems for others through its mar-
ket size and extraterritoriality (though the ‘Brussels Effect’ 
is increasingly challenged), but perhaps not strong enough 
to claim a seat at the top table. Europe remains politically 
too divided and therefore weak and a relative economic 
lightweight, in macro-political economy terms as well as 
innovation. Despite a very hawkish independent central 
bank, the euro remains far from a reserve currency after 25 
years. The continent lacks an integrated military, internal 
discord is rampant, the EU’s soft power ambitions have led 
to relatively weak trade positions that aim to please as many 
counterparties as possible, and political obstacles block the 
green transition where Europe was in pole position. The 
single largest advanced consumer market in the world is 
unable to negotiate alternative political-economic arrange-
ments directly, neither with China nor with the (future) US, 
and even though political elites in Europe are increasingly 
aware of these problems, they appear unable to do much 
about them.
Conclusions
This essay started from two recent processes of reorgani-
zation – one at the level of the global political economy, 
the other in firms, sectors, and supply chains. We suggested 
that the world is transforming into two large strategic blocs, 
revolving around the US and China – though there remains 
a large non-aligned component which may include Europe. 
MNEs, in turn, are increasingly rethinking their organiza-
tional logic. In old industries, this happens through a recon-
sideration of the economic and, increasingly, political costs 
and benefits of long, thin supply chains, and which often 
takes the form of hard and soft cross-ownership patterns in 
new industries. In new industries, higher levels of vertical 
integration around the home country base reinforce benefits 
that accrue to them because of the key roles of proprietary 
technology and control of critical resources. This has impli-
cations for IB strategy and IB policy. While international 
value chains and cross-border trade are unlikely to disap -
pear as a result, their shape and effects will change. How an 
MNE approaches these big shifts in the world economy will 
ultimately depend on its home location: activities between 
political blocs will become more expensive and diminish in 
relative importance, while the opposite is true within each 
bloc. MNEs from non-aligned countries may be better off, 
since they will be able to trade with everyone (even though 
that will no longer be at today’s relatively low cost). The way 
governments address this new situation will largely depend 
on where they find themselves in this new global economic 
order.
This analysis has several implications for research and 
policy. If we are correct in our characterization of the two 
scenarios and the links between them, we see three perti-
nent research questions that follow. First, our scenarios 
exist here in ideal-typical form, and they are unlikely to 
play out exactly as we analyzed them. What, then, would 
be the short-term forces pushing back – in supply chains 
and vertical integration process (including the many forms 
of cross-ownership that we see popping up everywhere) 
obviously, but also in geopolitical alignment and, not least, 
the EU’s response? Will comparative advantage, especially 
driven by costs, notably labor costs, remain the powerful 
force that it has been over the past 40 years of globalization 
or will political factors instead impose binding constraints? 
Will, as Canada’s Mark Carney suggested in Davos in Janu-
ary 2026, a third bloc emerge, which operates beyond not 
being aligned with either bloc, parallel to how, for example, 
the BRICS emerged as a loose coalition a quarter century 
ago? Could that third bloc even become a more integrated

horses. If globalization changes in logic, theories that are

The possibility of deeper integration of the non-aligned 
bloc brings us to our second topic for further research. One 
of the EU’s founding fathers, the French civil servant Jean 
Monnet, is famous for his maxim that the EU will be formed 
as the sum of the responses to a crisis it faces – a logic that, 
while insightful on process, says little about substantive out-
comes. We think, in contrast, that the EU’s decision-making 
structures are probably too complex to address the current 
tensions creatively. We accept that we may well be wrong in 
practice – the EU may overcome its many problems of coop-
eration and coordination in the face of adversity. However, 
in that case, we need a theory, preferably beyond problem 
load, to explain such a sudden burst of political integration.
Finally, there is the link with the green and digital tran-
sitions. We deliberately kept our analytical distance from 
these to avoid muddying the argument, but the question is 
to what extent the processes that we identify here support 
(or hinder) the latter? Are they also linked to the geopoliti-
cal and industrial organization shifts through what we call 
(following Weber) ‘selective affinity’, in which they emerge 
independently but then mutually reinforce each other? And 
does this mean that we may be witnessing different path-
dependent, contextualized green and digital transitions in 
different parts of the globe? If the answer to most of these 
questions is a version of ‘yes’, we will again see, in an echo 
of the Cold War, very different routes of development, decar-
bonization, and automation emerging.
Our new world produces not just new research questions 
but also a new landscape for business, and which rejigs poli-
tics and policy. If one of the outcomes is a closer alignment 
between the objectives of business and government, the 
latter will need to significantly boost their administrative 
capabilities. Many governments, however, are entering this 
process from a very weak position after several decades of 
gently dismantling active state capabilities. While this seems 
a problem across all the rich capitalist economies, the US 
in particular will see a strong tension emerge between this 
capability-building imperative and the traditional political 
right’s conventional emphasis on reducing the size and reach 
of government.
In addition, the uncertainty of the new world puts a pre-
mium on being able to develop simultaneously a more stra-
tegic sense at the center and flexible decentralized processes 
and structures to govern adjustment in skills, technology 
transfer, public support services, infrastructure and informa-
tion exchange. There is a big difference between knowing 
what to do and knowing how to find out what to do, and as 
uncertainty increases, the latter gains significantly in impor-
tance. Being able to organize decentralized debates about 
where to go and how to get there, preferably with signifi-
cant input from the most important stakeholders, will ensure 
that shifts to address new problems are also ‘owned’ by the 
affected parties.
Uncertainty is, in sum, the name of the new game – from 
the global political economy over policy and innovation to 
the organization of supply chains. The emerging new world 
order raises a series of policy conundrums that business 
and governments will have to address – often with highly 
imperfect knowledge of today’s problems and the effects of 
their solutions. Yet one thing is clear. Even if they only now 
become apparent, these developments have been fermenting 
for decades. The scenarios we have outlined here may not 
be entirely accurate in practice, but the world is unlikely to 
return to the giddy days before 2008.
