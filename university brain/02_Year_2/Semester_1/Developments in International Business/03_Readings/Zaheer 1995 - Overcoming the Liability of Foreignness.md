# Zaheer (1995) - Overcoming the Liability of Foreignness

**Author:** Srilata Zaheer  
**Journal:** *Academy of Management Journal*, 38(2), 341-363  
**Session:** 1  
**Role:** Supplementary reading  
**Core concepts:** Liability of foreignness, firm-specific advantage, local isomorphism, administrative heritage, integration vs responsiveness

> This is a comprehensive structured extraction for study/search purposes rather than a verbatim reproduction of the copyrighted JSTOR article.

## Research question

Zaheer asks whether foreign firms really face a measurable **liability of foreignness (LOF)** and, if they do, how multinational subsidiaries can overcome it.

The paper compares two competing responses:

1. **Import firm-specific organizational capabilities from the parent/home country.**
2. **Adapt to the host country by copying successful local organizational practices (local isomorphism).**

The empirical setting is foreign-exchange trading rooms of major Western and Japanese banks in New York and Tokyo.

# Liability of foreignness

## Definition

LOF is the set of **additional costs incurred by a firm operating abroad that a comparable local firm would not incur**.

Zaheer identifies four broad sources.

### 1. Spatial-distance costs
- travel;
- transportation;
- communication and coordination across distance;
- time-zone separation.

### 2. Firm-specific unfamiliarity costs
- lack of local experience;
- unfamiliarity with customers, institutions, norms, information networks, and practices;
- weak local roots.

### 3. Host-country costs
- lower legitimacy of foreign companies;
- discrimination or economic nationalism;
- local stakeholder preference for domestic firms;
- host-country institutional barriers.

### 4. Home-country costs
- constraints imposed by the firm's own country;
- example: restrictions on technology exports or activity in particular foreign markets.

The relative importance of these costs varies across:
- industry;
- company;
- home country;
- host country.

All else equal, LOF should imply lower foreign-firm profitability and potentially lower survival probability.

## Where LOF should be strongest

Zaheer argues that LOF may be especially strong for **horizontal, market-seeking MNEs** whose foreign units largely replicate local-for-local activities and directly compete with domestic firms.

Vertical or networked MNEs may experience LOF differently because foreign units have specialized roles inside an integrated global value chain and can benefit from global scale, scope, or differentiated subsidiary roles.

# Empirical setting: foreign-exchange trading

The study uses foreign-exchange trading rooms because the context makes the theory unusually demanding.

- FX is a highly globalized industry.
- The product—currencies—is essentially undifferentiated.
- Technology is widely available from a small number of common suppliers.
- Many conventional advantages such as product differentiation are therefore weak.
- Performance depends heavily on information, organizational routines, control systems, trader behavior, and access to local information networks.

Local trading rooms may possess superior access to:
- local customers;
- central banks;
- policy makers;
- local market information;
- informal information networks.

That creates a plausible LOF even in a seemingly global and friction-light industry.

# Hypotheses

## Hypothesis 1 - LOF exists

Foreign trading rooms should be **less profitable than local trading rooms in the same city**, all else equal.

## Hypothesis 2a - imported firm-specific advantage

Foreign trading rooms whose organizational practices more closely resemble those of their own firm's home-country trading room should exhibit **less liability of foreignness**.

The underlying resource-based logic is that MNEs possess organizational capabilities that are difficult for competitors to copy and that can be transferred across the multinational network.

## Hypothesis 2b - local isomorphism

An alternative institutional-theory prediction is that foreign trading rooms that more closely resemble successful **local firms** should exhibit less LOF.

Local adaptation can arise through:

- **coercive isomorphism** - regulation or formal host-country requirements;
- **normative isomorphism** - professional standards and accepted ways of organizing;
- **mimetic isomorphism** - imitation of successful local organizations.

# Firm-specific advantage versus local adaptation

To overcome foreignness, an MNE can potentially:

- bring superior resources or capabilities from home;
- decentralize and become more locally responsive;
- copy successful local practices;
- combine home capabilities with local adaptation.

Traditional FSAs may include:
- brand;
- technology;
- differentiated products;
- scale or scope economies;
- cost advantages;
- organizational routines;
- learning capability;
- managerial skill;
- ability to transfer knowledge across the MNE network.

In FX trading, product and technology advantages are limited, which makes **organizational capabilities** particularly important.

# Organizational practices studied

Zaheer identifies two practices that differ strongly between Western trading rooms in New York and Japanese trading rooms in Tokyo.

## Market controls

Western rooms use more market-like employment and incentive practices, such as:

- high performance-linked compensation;
- hiring experienced traders from the external labor market;
- relatively high turnover.

The logic is that strong performance incentives attract and motivate capable traders. Because room-level profit is closely linked to individual trader performance, market control is expected to improve performance.

## Bureaucratic microcontrols

Japanese rooms use stricter detailed limits on traders' intraday and overnight open positions by currency and trader.

These controls aim to manage risk and impose discipline.

However, in a fast speculative market, Zaheer argues that excessive microcontrol can reduce performance because the trader closest to the market often has the best information and must make decisions in seconds. Tight centralized limits may prevent traders from exploiting opportunities.

# Method

## Sample

The full sample included 28 trading rooms:
- 13 in New York;
- 15 in Tokyo.

The central hypothesis tests used a matched sample of **24 trading rooms** belonging to six Western and six Japanese banks, with each bank represented in both its home and foreign location.

The survey evidence was based on **174 traders** in the matched sample.

Subgroups:
- Japanese rooms in Tokyo;
- Japanese rooms in New York;
- Western rooms in Tokyo;
- Western rooms in New York.

## Data collection

Zaheer used:
- observation;
- interviews;
- surveys of individual traders;
- separate questionnaires/interviews with trading-room heads.

Using different respondents for practices and performance reduced common-method bias.

## Measuring LOF

For each foreign room, LOF was operationalized as the gap between:

**average local-room profit per trader in that city - focal foreign-room profit per trader.**

A larger positive difference means a larger liability of foreignness.

## Measuring local isomorphism

For market controls and bureaucratic microcontrols, Zaheer measured the distance between the foreign room's practice and the average practice of local firms in the host city.

Smaller distance = greater local isomorphism.

Both signed and absolute distance measures were considered because the **direction** of difference could matter, not only its magnitude.

## Measuring imported firm-specific advantage

For each practice, the foreign room was compared with its paired home-country room in the same parent bank.

Smaller distance from the home room = stronger transfer of parent/home organizational practice.

## Risk control

Because FX profitability depends on risk-taking, the analysis controlled for actual trading risk using indicators of open positions and loss-taking behavior.

# Results

## Evidence for liability of foreignness

After controlling for trading-room age and size, local trading rooms had significantly higher profits per trader than foreign rooms.

This supports **Hypothesis 1**.

Importantly, LOF appears even in an industry that is:
- globally integrated;
- highly competitive;
- technologically standardized;
- based on an undifferentiated product.

Zaheer interprets this as strong evidence for the classic Hymer argument that doing business abroad generates real extra costs.

Neither room age nor size adequately explained the profitability difference.

## Home practice versus local imitation

The results generally favor **imported firm-specific organizational capability / administrative heritage** over simple imitation of local practice.

The key pattern is not that local adaptation is always bad. Instead:

- local isomorphism and imported FSA pull subsidiaries in different directions;
- the effects differ by organizational practice;
- copying local practices is difficult because practices are embedded in organizational systems and histories;
- a practice that works for a successful local competitor may not work when transplanted into a foreign firm's different administrative system.

For market controls, greater similarity to home-country routines was associated with better performance and less LOF.

Local imitation did not consistently reduce LOF. In some analyses, being **farther from local practice** was associated with better performance, contradicting a simple mimetic-isomorphism prediction.

The results therefore provide stronger support for **Hypothesis 2a** than for a general version of Hypothesis 2b.

# Administrative heritage

One of the paper's most important ideas is **administrative heritage**.

Organizations develop historically embedded routines, structures, skills, and ways of controlling people. These are not modular practices that can simply be swapped in and out.

If a parent firm's advantage lies in its organizational capabilities, the foreign subsidiary may perform better by preserving and transferring those capabilities than by fully copying the host-country model.

This also explains why imitation is difficult:

> Observing a successful practice does not mean a competitor can reproduce the broader organizational system that makes that practice effective.

# Important nuance: adaptation can still matter

Zaheer explicitly cautions against the interpretation "always standardize globally."

The result depends on what generates the firm's competitive advantage.

If the FSA is primarily:
- technology;
- brand;
- scale;
- product differentiation;

then organizational practices may be more freely localized without destroying the underlying advantage.

Some practices may also need to converge locally because of:
- regulation;
- professional norms;
- physical or economic constraints;
- local efficiency requirements.

Example: office layout may resemble the local norm not because the firm is copying competitors but because local real-estate economics impose similar constraints.

Other practices can become **globally isomorphic** because the industry itself imposes a common efficient model.

# Integration versus responsiveness

The study reframes the classic international-strategy problem.

The relevant question is not simply:

**Global integration or local responsiveness?**

Instead ask, for **each organizational practice or value-adding activity**:

1. Is this practice a source of firm-specific advantage?
2. Is it tightly embedded in the firm's administrative heritage?
3. Does the host environment require adaptation?
4. Is there a global industry norm?
5. Can the local practice actually be copied effectively?

The optimal balance can differ across practices inside the same subsidiary.

# Limitations / caveats

- Small number of trading rooms, although the measures aggregate responses from many traders.
- One industry and two countries limit generalizability.
- Performance was observed at one point in time.
- The importance of LOF sources and FSAs differs across sectors.
- Results should be treated as suggestive rather than a universal rule.
- Organizational-practice effects are highly context-dependent.

# What the article contributes

1. Provides empirical support that **LOF exists** rather than treating it only as a theoretical assumption.
2. Shows that LOF can exist even in a highly global, seemingly borderless industry.
3. Connects MNE theory to the resource-based view through **firm-specific advantages**.
4. Shows that institutional pressure to resemble the host environment does not automatically improve performance.
5. Highlights the strategic importance of **administrative heritage**.
6. Shows the difficulty of copying organizational routines.
7. Adds nuance to the integration-responsiveness problem.
