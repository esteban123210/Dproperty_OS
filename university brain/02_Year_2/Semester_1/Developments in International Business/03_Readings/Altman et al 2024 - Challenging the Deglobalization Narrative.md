# Altman, Bastian & Fattedad (2024) - Challenging the Deglobalization Narrative

**Authors:** Steven A. Altman, Caroline R. Bastian, Davis Fattedad  
**Journal:** *Journal of International Business Policy*, 7, 416-439  
**Published:** 7 October 2024  
**License:** Creative Commons Attribution 4.0  
**Session:** 1  
**Role:** Supplementary reading

## Central claim

The widespread claim that the world has already entered an era of deglobalization is not supported by the observed pattern of international flows. Deglobalization remains a **risk**, but not a demonstrated current macro-level reality. International trade, capital, information, and people flows have shown substantial resilience through Brexit, U.S.-China trade tensions, COVID-19, and Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

The article is an important update to Ghemawat's semi-globalization argument. Ghemawat warned against assuming the world had become borderless; Altman et al. warn against the opposite overreaction—assuming international integration is collapsing.

## Research questions

The paper asks four empirical questions:

1. Are international flows declining relative to domestic activity?
2. Are international flows becoming more regionalized?
3. Are international flows fragmenting along geopolitical lines?
4. How much do globalization levels and trends differ across countries?

The first three are used to test the deglobalization narrative. The fourth shows why aggregate global narratives can obscure large cross-country variation.

## The five strands of the deglobalization narrative

The authors distinguish five different things that are often mixed together:

1. **Preferences** - public or specialist attitudes becoming less supportive of globalization.
2. **Public policy** - more barriers, industrial policy, investment screening, data-flow restrictions, or weakened international institutions.
3. **Plans** - corporate announcements about reshoring, nearshoring, friendshoring, diversification, or footprint changes.
4. **Predictions** - forecasts that policy and geopolitical changes will reduce or redirect global flows.
5. **Actual patterns of international activity** - observed changes in the size and geography of trade, capital, information, and people flows.

The article accepts that strands 1-4 contain many real developments and therefore imply a **higher risk of future deglobalization**. Its disagreement is specifically with strand 5: actual flow data do not show a clear, generalized reversal of international integration.

## Definition of deglobalization

The authors define deglobalization as declining integration or interdependence among countries. Therefore, claims of deglobalization should be tested using actual cross-border activity rather than rhetoric, announced intentions, or isolated policy changes.

## Why misdiagnosis matters

The article warns of a self-fulfilling-prophecy problem. If policymakers and managers falsely assume that globalization is already reversing, they may adopt strategies and policies that accelerate fragmentation.

The authors cite an IMF estimate that bifurcation of world trade into two rival blocs could reduce global GDP by as much as about 7%, with potentially even larger losses if technological systems fragment.

## Intellectual positioning

The paper locates itself in a long-running IB debate about exaggerated globalization narratives.

- Rugman emphasized the regional concentration of many MNEs.
- Ghemawat argued that distance continues to constrain international activity and described the world as **semi-globalized**.
- Later work documented that managers and the general public systematically overestimate international flows.
- Recent scholarship has swung toward a deglobalization narrative; Altman et al. argue that this new narrative also needs empirical discipline.

The paper uses the **DHL Global Connectedness Index**, which distinguishes:

- **Depth**: how large international flows are relative to domestic activity.
- **Breadth**: how widely those flows are geographically distributed.

This distinction is critical: globalization is not only about how much crosses borders but also about how geographically extensive those connections are.

# 1. Intensity / depth of international flows

## Overall connectedness

The DHL Global Connectedness depth index aggregates four pillars:

- trade - 35% weight;
- capital - 35%;
- information - 15%;
- people - 15%.

The overall depth index reached a new record high in 2022 and projections suggested a similar level in 2023.

### Trade

The trade pillar, based on international trade in value-added terms relative to world GDP, reached record-high levels in 2022.

The familiar gross exports-to-GDP ratio had fallen after the 2008 global financial crisis, which is often used as evidence for deglobalization. However:

- the ratio rebounded strongly in 2021 and 2022;
- by 2022 it was roughly back near its 2008 record;
- commodity-price movements influence the measure;
- compositional changes in the world economy can reduce the ratio without implying that countries are deliberately becoming less integrated;
- services trade has continued to become more internationally intensive even when some goods indicators stagnated.

The article therefore rejects simple use of a post-2008 decline in gross trade/GDP as proof of deglobalization.

### Capital

The capital pillar also reached a record high in 2022.

FDI flows as a share of gross fixed capital formation are below historic peaks and recently showed declines, but the authors argue that this indicator is too volatile and incomplete to support broad conclusions by itself.

Reasons include:

- FDI flow peaks are often driven by M&A waves;
- conduit and purely financial transactions can distort the numbers;
- FDI statistics omit contractual internationalization modes;
- multinational activity financed locally in the host country can be missed;
- FDI **stocks** still capture continuing international ownership and control relationships;
- announced greenfield FDI and cross-border M&A provide complementary evidence.

When Luxembourg's unusually large financial transaction is excluded, the recent FDI-flow picture becomes less negative.

### Other measures of multinational activity

The article examines multiple additional indicators, including:

- inward FDI stocks relative to GDP;
- inward FDI stocks relative to stock-market capitalization;
- announced greenfield FDI value and project counts;
- cross-border M&A value and counts;
- international share of M&A transactions;
- output of MNE foreign affiliates as a share of world output;
- foreign-affiliate employment relative to the global working-age population;
- international intellectual-property payments;
- foreign revenue shares of listed firms.

Taken together, these measures do not indicate a generalized retreat of multinational firms from foreign markets.

### Information

The information pillar remained around a record high after two decades of substantial growth. Measures include international scientific collaboration, international patenting, intellectual-property flows, and related cross-border information activity.

### People

The people pillar remained below its 2019 peak in 2022, but the authors attribute this primarily to the slow recovery of international travel after COVID-19 rather than to structural deglobalization. Migration and international education remained important, and tourism was expected to recover to or above pre-pandemic levels.

## Depth conclusion

At the aggregate level, international activity remains at or near historical highs. There is **no general shift from international toward domestic activity**.

# 2. Geographic extensity / regionalization

A major deglobalization argument says firms are shortening supply chains and moving from global to regional activity through reshoring, nearshoring, and regional sourcing.

The authors separate corporate discussion and intentions from actual observed flow patterns.

## Corporate resilience responses

Many firms did respond to shocks, but not primarily through simple nearshoring. Common responses included:

- digitization;
- supplier diversification;
- dual sourcing;
- inventory and resilience measures;
- selective footprint changes.

Temporary pandemic-era shipping costs also encouraged discussion of localization, but those shipping costs later fell substantially.

## Intra-regional trade

Some prior studies found a rise in the share of trade occurring within regions after around 2012. Altman et al. show that:

- the result depends strongly on how regions are defined;
- the apparent regionalization did not continue after around 2016;
- since COVID-19, the intra-regional share of trade actually declined under several alternative regional classifications.

## Average distance traveled by flows

The authors use average geographic distance traversed by flows as a second measure of regionalization, avoiding arbitrary region definitions.

If globalization were clearly becoming regionalized, average flow distances should fall. The paper does not find a broad, sustained decline consistent with a general shift toward regional business.

## Regionalization conclusion

There is **no clear current macro-level shift from global toward regional flows**. Some firms, sectors, and countries are reorganizing, but it is inaccurate to infer a universal regionalization trend.

# 3. Geopolitical extensity / fragmentation

The authors distinguish **geoeconomic fragmentation** from ordinary geographic regionalization.

Geoeconomic fragmentation refers to policy-induced changes in the sources and destinations of cross-border flows driven by strategic concerns such as national security, sovereignty, or geopolitical alignment.

## U.S.-China decoupling

The article finds genuine weakening in U.S.-China ties.

Across a broad set of trade, capital, information, and people flows:

- China's share of U.S. flows fell in most categories;
- the U.S. share of China's flows also fell in most categories;
- average bilateral flow shares declined materially between 2016 and the latest available observations.

This is real decoupling.

However, the authors argue that not every decline should automatically be attributed to geopolitics. They propose four criteria for identifying **geopolitically driven decoupling**:

1. There must be a clear recent decline in the relevant bilateral flow share.
2. The decline must be unusual relative to the prior trend.
3. It must coincide with policy changes or a major rise in geopolitical risk capable of affecting decisions.
4. The decline must not simply reflect a general fall in the partner country's interactions with the whole world.

Merchandise imports provide one of the clearest examples of geopolitically driven U.S.-China decoupling.

## Russia

Russia's international flows changed much more dramatically after the 2022 invasion of Ukraine. Sanctions and political realignment produced a profound redirection of trade, capital, and other relationships.

## Rival blocs?

Despite U.S.-China decoupling and the restructuring of Russia's external ties, the authors do **not** find convincing evidence that the entire global economy has split into two comprehensive rival blocs.

Geopolitically driven fragmentation remains concentrated most strongly around the countries at the center of major current conflicts.

This distinction is essential:

> Bilateral decoupling and selective geopolitical fragmentation are real; generalized global deglobalization is not yet established.

# 4. Country-level variation

A central conclusion is that globalization levels differ much more **across countries** than they typically change **over short business-planning horizons**.

This means a global average can be a poor guide for firm strategy or national policy.

Two countries can face radically different levels of international connectedness because of:

- economic size;
- geography;
- market openness;
- logistics and infrastructure;
- institutions;
- regional integration;
- trade structure;
- investment regimes;
- geopolitical alignment.

Therefore, policymakers and firms should use granular country- and activity-level measures rather than relying on broad narratives such as "globalization is ending."

# Reconfiguration is not necessarily deglobalization

One of the paper's most important conceptual distinctions is between **reconfiguration** and **reversal**.

If U.S.-China trade becomes more indirect—for example through third countries—international activity may be changing its routes without becoming less international overall.

The authors interpret such adaptation as evidence of the resilience of international business: firms and countries often redesign networks to preserve the value of cross-border activity under new constraints.

# Conclusion

The paper concludes that:

- globalization has faced substantial shocks;
- political and corporate discourse about deglobalization has intensified;
- policy barriers and geopolitical risk have increased;
- U.S.-China decoupling is real in important areas;
- Russia's international flows have been radically redirected;
- nevertheless, aggregate international flows remain resilient;
- there is no broad shift from international to domestic activity;
- there is no broad shift from global to regional activity;
- there is not yet a comprehensive split of world flows into rival geopolitical blocs;
- deglobalization remains a **risk**, not a demonstrated present global condition.

Future measurement remains necessary because current policy trends could eventually transform the risk into reality.

# Managerial implications

1. **Do not confuse rhetoric with realized flows.** Corporate and political statements may change long before actual international networks do.
2. **Avoid binary globalize/deglobalize thinking.** Networks can reconfigure while remaining highly international.
3. **Analyze the relevant flow.** Trade, FDI, information, talent, IP, and travel can move differently.
4. **Analyze the relevant country pair.** U.S.-China dynamics cannot automatically be generalized to all country relationships.
5. **Build resilience without assuming retreat is optimal.** Diversification, redundancy, digitization, and indirect network structures may preserve international advantages.
6. **Expect selective geopolitical fragmentation.** Some sectors and bilateral relationships can decouple sharply even when aggregate globalization remains resilient.

# Link to Ghemawat

Ghemawat (2012): the world is **less globalized than people think**.  
Altman et al. (2024): the world is **not clearly deglobalizing as much as people now claim**.

The combined lesson is empirical humility: measure actual international activity rather than extrapolating from fashionable narratives.
