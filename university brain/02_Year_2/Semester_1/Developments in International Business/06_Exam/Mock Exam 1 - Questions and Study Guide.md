## Practice guidance created for the Brain

This is study guidance, not a lecturer marking scheme. The four source questions below cover liabilities of foreignness, behavioral biases and AI, geopolitical institutional change, and stakeholder engagement linked to a foundational IB framework. The sheet specifies 120 minutes and 250–500 words per question; confirm the real exam instructions separately.

1. **Walmart and LOF:** Define additional foreign-firm costs relative to local competitors, then map the supplied greeter/smiling, language, labor-code and remote-leadership facts to unfamiliarity, legitimacy and coordination costs. Explain how FSAs and local adaptation could offset them. Distinguish an ordinary cost disadvantage from a specifically foreignness-related mechanism.
2. **Walmart, biases and AI:** Identify judgment and present-state biases from the transfer of the home operating model. AI can broaden local evidence and compare scenarios, but may repeat biased data or optimize the wrong objectives. Explain human validation, local stakeholder input and oversight rather than promising bias-free automation.
3. **BYD and geopolitics:** Explain how weaker supranational enforcement and security-oriented industrial policy change location advantages and the durability of tariff-driven investments. Compare efficient global sourcing with regional duplication and vertically integrated resilience. Discuss sunk costs, uncertainty and reversible investment stages.
4. **BYD and stakeholders:** Explain how government, community, workforce and supplier cooperation turn assets into realized cash flow. Connect stakeholder engagement explicitly to OLI location advantages or internalization and hold-up risk, then discuss limits. Use Henisz et al. valuation evidence carefully; it does not supply a guaranteed financial return for BYD.

Suggested practice allocation: 5 minutes to scan and plan, about 25 minutes per question, and 15 minutes to review. This is a study recommendation, not an official rule. Judge answers by accurate definitions, fact-to-mechanism application, comparison and a qualified recommendation.

The Walmart and BYD narratives are supplied mock-exam premises. They have not been separately verified as current company or policy reporting.

1.Question
Refer to the case study below to describe and illustrate the conceptliabilities of foreignness.
W almart in Germany: The Smile That Cost a Billion
When Walmart entered Germany in 1997 by acquiring the Wertkauf hypermarket chain, and
added Interspar stores a year later, the move looked like a natural extension of a formula
that had made it the largest retailer in the world. “Everyday Low Prices,” high-volume logis-
tics and an intensely service-oriented store culture had worked in Mexico, Canada and the
UK. Germany, Europe's largest consumer market, was to be the bridgehead for continental
expansion.
The company transferred its operating model largely intact. Store entrances were staffed with
greeters. Cashiers were instructed to smile at customers and make small talk; employees
were expected to offer help to any shopper within ten feet. Mornings began with a group
chant to build energy before opening. A corporate ethics code, distributed in translation,
asked employees to report colleagues' misconduct through a hotline and restricted romantic
relationships between staff. Later, a German labor court ruled partly against these provisions
and management met resistance from works councils and the retail union ver.di.
German shoppers reported finding the greeting and the smiling unnatural, and some were
uncomfortable with strangers handling their purchases at the till. Employees were reluctant
to perform the routines. Meanwhile the price promise ran into structural limits: Aldi and
Lidl operated at cost levels Walmart could not match.
Leadership decisions added further complications. The first head of the German operation,
an American, did not speak German and required English as the working language of man-
agement. His successor, a Briton, ran the business largely from England.

3.Question
Refer to the case study below as an illustration of the growing geopolitical tensions that
multinational enterprises face. Use the case study to explain how the retreat of supranational
institutions and the growing importance of security-motivated industrial policy disrupts
international business.
BYD: Building Behind the T ariff W all
BYD began in 1995 as a Shenzhen battery maker with 20 employees, supplying Motorola
and Nokia before buying a small carmaker in 2003 and launching its first plug-in hybrid in
2008. This was also the same year Beijing began its sustained program of subsidies and state
support for the sector. By 2025 it was the world's largest producer of electric passenger cars.
Success at home has since become a liability. A price war among Chinese manufacturers
cut BYD's 2025 net profit by roughly 19%, to 32.6 billion yuan and exports have become the
profit engine. Higher-value export sales lifted gross margin in the first half of 2026 even as
domestic volumes stayed under pressure.
Each destination market carries its own political terms. The EU imposed anti-subsidy duties
on Chinese-built EVs in 2024. Its answer was a plant at Szeged in Hungary and a separate
project in Turkey. Meanwhile, in January 2026 Brussels and Beijing announced a framework
under which minimum import prices could replace the tariffs altogether, potentially removing
the very barrier that motivated these investments.
The Americas offer a more extreme version of the same issue. The US market is effectively
closed to BYD by tariffs and foreign-entity rules. A long-discussed Mexican plant remains
unannounced amid tariff uncertainty and the USMCA review. Brazil, now BYD's largest
overseas market, hosts a converted former Ford plant at Camacari, opened in October 2025
after Brazil raised EV import duties.
BYD increasingly faces the challenge to make irreversible investments in an environment
where economic logic depends on trade rules that governments can rewrite in a seemingly
arbitrary manner.
