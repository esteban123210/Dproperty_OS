I. HEADQUARTERS ASSESSMENT. By the end of the first year, AES-Telasi had lost $40 million and had already invested more than its 
original ten-year commitment, while collections amounted less than 10%, rather than the expected 40% return, across about 410,000 
customers. At first sight, the direct losses appeared marginal relative to AES's $12.9 billion asset base and the contract remained formally 
protective; however, the overall economics of the transaction were highly depended on multiple external factors, including government 
payment, enforceability challenges, reliable supply, regulatory dynamics, employee integrity and customer legitimacy. As a result, the 
larger global concern is that AES may continue to replicate the same exposure while expanding rapidly to other emerging markets, giving 
local businesses extensive autonomy with limited headquarters oversight (Henisz and Zelner, 2010). 
 
II. HOW THE CRISIS MAY AFFECT AES GLOBALLY 
Finance and capital allocation. While the case does not demonstrate group-level impairments or adverse results to AES’ cost of 
capital, further operating losses or investment could lead to reduced cash availability for other projects, on top of making renegotiation 
or exit more difficult. 
 
Investor confidence and portfolio strategy. The contract that appeared capable of supporting a 20% return ended it up not protecting 
the business from nonpayment, serving as primary evidence of AES’s poor global pre-investment risk assessment and scenario planning. 
As a result, investors may apply a higher risk discount to AES's emerging market portfolio. In addition, the situation may cause the 
headquarters to reassess its overall investment strategy in emerging markets, such as assets distribution, import dependence, political-
risk analysis, lobbying protocols, and contingency planning. While establishing a new investment protocol, the review should still 
distinguish administrative, geographic, and economic distance rather than treat all emerging markets alike (Ghemawat, 2001). 
 
Governance and decentralization. Telasi operated with almost complete local autonomy, under the supervision of an inexperienced 
team and limited specialist support from Arlington. The crisis suggests that great autonomy detaches local subsidiaries from 
headquarters’ purview, particularly when political-risk review, staffing standards and escalation triggers are absent. AES may need a 
more balanced investment strategy with stronger enterprise -wide safeguards (based on earlier headquarters intervention ) that also 
preserves useful local decision authority. 
 
Reputation, values, and legitimacy. AES, Telasi and Scholey identities were strongly disconnected. W hile AES linked its identity to 
fairness and integrity, Telasi was known for its low social responsibility interest, and Scholey became the personalization of an imperialist 
American company. The lack of cohesion could therefore jeopardize AES's reputation, legitimacy with communities, governments, 
lenders and employees in other markets, reinforcing its liability of foreignness (Zaheer, 1995). 
 
Political bargaining. Certain mechanisms, such as tariff indexation, cost pass-through and London arbitration, could not on their own 
create the necessary conditions to collect revenue and secure supply. As AES committed more capital, Georgian actors gained leverage, 
while such investment did not proportionally advance AES ’ local bargaining power . Furthermore, any local operating bargain or 
negotiation concession could lead to a cascade effect and may negatively influence how other host governments perceive AES 
(Ramamurti, 2001). 
 
People capability and learning. The project lacked experienced staff, clear career incentives, and adequate support for its employees; 
thus, exposing recruitment , retention , and duty -of-care issues. However, to the extent the headquarters is able to learn from such 
negative experiences, it could implement better anti-corruption action s, stakeholder mapping plans and public engagement ; 
strengthening, therefore, AES's ability to build locally embedded operations elsewhere (Santos and Williamson, 2015). 
 
III. HEADQUARTERS RESPONSE AND CONCLUSION 
1. Stabilize and bound the exposure. Run a cross-functional review of cash needs, receivables, supply, risks/safety concerns, legal 
options, and stakeholder escalation. Set explicit conditions for further funding, renegotiation, partnership, paused investment, or exit. 
2. Apply the political -risk process across the portfolio. Use Rice and Zegart's “Understand, Analyze, Mitigate and Respond ” 
framework to stress-test other assets for government arrears, tariff and foreign -exchange pass-through, import dependence, state 
counterparties, and disconnection authority. Add central political risk , legal, security, and staffing support, together with clear 
escalation triggers. 
3. Build an institutional coalition. Convert Scholey's experience in Georgia and lessons learned to develop a comprehensive guide to 
assess, plan and execute investments in emerging markets , including a protocol to establish healthy and legally supported 
relationships with the government, regulators, customer groups, suppliers, and multilateral institutions. Communication should 
connect reliable service, fair payment, affordability and anti-corruption to AES's values and follow operational evidence. 
 
Conclusion. AES should treat Telasi as a portfolio-wide governance and learning event. The $40 million first-year loss matters, but the 
greater global learning is that AES's expanding decentralized model may reproduce policy risk faster than the company can identify and 
manage it. In short, headquarters should preserve local authority while adding institutional support, accountability, and explicit funding 
or exit gates.
