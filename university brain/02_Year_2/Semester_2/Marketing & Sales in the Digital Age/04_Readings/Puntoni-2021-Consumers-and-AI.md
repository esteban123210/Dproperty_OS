# Consumers and Artificial Intelligence

**Authors:** Puntoni, Reczek, Giesler, and Botti (2021)  
**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing*, 85(1)  
**Source in this workspace:** User-provided condensed reading; consult the original article for formal citation and direct quotations.

## Main argument

AI should be evaluated as a consumer experience, not only by accuracy, speed, and efficiency. The same capability can benefit a customer and create a human cost.

| AI capability | Helpful experience | Possible harm |
|---|---|---|
| Listening / collecting data | The customer feels served by relevant support. | The customer feels exploited or surveilled. |
| Predicting / classifying | The customer feels understood. | The customer is misunderstood or discriminated against. |
| Producing outcomes / performing tasks | The customer gains time or capability. | The customer feels replaced or loses control and competence. |
| Social interaction | The system connects customer and firm. | The interaction alienates or reproduces stereotypes. |

The article is a conceptual review that organizes earlier research and examples; it is not a single new experiment.

## Relevance to Blue

If Blue considers AI-generated follow-up or personalized health content, the team should assess both the intended benefit and the customer's experience of data use. Explicit consent, understandable explanations, meaningful opt-out, clinician oversight, and a clear boundary between engagement support and medical diagnosis would be central design questions. An efficient system can still damage trust if people feel watched, wrongly classified, or left without a human.

## Use in the assignment

Use this article to frame the **consumer and trust** side of an AI recommendation. Pair the claimed customer benefit with a human risk and a safeguard. Do not present the conceptual framework as empirical proof that a proposed Blue feature will increase retention.
